Why this matters

You have to be a thinker.

Every clinical question deserves an evidence-based answer. Information reaches you constantly — from colleagues, speakers, salespeople and now AI — and most of it is opinion wearing the costume of fact.

Two ways to plan

Opinion-based vs evidence-based

Opinion bias

The commonest approach — not because it's right, but because information is incomplete. You read, then reinforce what you already think. With experience this hardens into "that's just how it is" — sometimes correct, sometimes confidently wrong.

Evidence-based

The ideal: all the information you need, appraised critically. In reality you only ever have part of it — the skill is using that part well, and knowing what you don't know.

Watch for

Surgeons evolve over decades — most become less invasive, not more. Today's certainty is tomorrow's outdated technique.

Philosophy
"I don't want to believe — I want to know."

Most people just want the recipe: do this, then this, like cooking spaghetti. But you need to understand how it works — because in implant dentistry, things are very often not as they seem.

A new tool, a new risk

AI appraises better than the average dentist — and still invents things

The upside

It condenses huge literature in seconds and writes clearly. Esposito uses it to soften blunt emails so colleagues aren't offended.

The trap

It hallucinates — fabricating answers "to be helpful." It currently reports the literature without truly appraising it, and counts conclusions ("seven papers say impossible, so it's impossible") even when the conclusion is wrong.

The deeper danger

Critical-skill atrophy — a generation that goes straight to the answer without learning how to reach it, with shrinking concentration spans.

Adapt to your skill

Always have a Plan B

Skill develops — but some highly skilled operators are dangerous: they attempt sophisticated surgery confidently because the hands are ready before the judgement is. If your skill isn't there yet, a simpler therapy can work very well.

  • You don't have to do vertical augmentation — most attempts fail before you eventually stop trying.
  • When choosing any therapy, ask: "If this goes wrong, what is Plan B?" Sometimes there isn't one — and that should change your decision.
The cardinal error
Solve the patient's problem — not the problem in your mind.

The conference/social-media reflex is to chase perfect aesthetics across multiple interventions. But the 60-year-old man in the chair just wants something that works. Treat the patient, not the photograph.

Study design · 1

You cannot claim success without a control

"100% success rate" means nothing on its own. Any study of efficacy needs a proper comparison — against no therapy, a placebo, or the gold standard.

vs nothing

No-therapy control

Needed where the natural course is unknown — e.g. an asymptomatic periapical radiolucency: treat or watch?

vs placebo

Placebo control

Possible for drugs; hard for surgery (you can't fake placing an implant).

vs best

Gold-standard control

e.g. grafts tested against autogenous bone. In peri-implantitis, no clear gold standard exists.

Study design · 2

It must be in humans

There are many ligature-induced peri-implantitis studies in dogs. Esposito once arrived at a consensus meeting to find a draft recommending treatment "based on this animal study."

His verdict

"Throw it out the window. Have you seen a pig brush its teeth — or smoke?" Animal work hints at mechanism; it does not set clinical protocol.

Study design · 3

The "controlled clinical trial" is a dentist's invention

Methodologists don't recognise the term. Its fatal flaw: the clinician allocates the patients — and will, consciously or not, steer the best-prognosis cases into the treatment they favour.

Selection bias built in: the result is decided before treatment begins.

The principle

Small samples swing wildly. The larger the sample, the more balanced the groups — for known and unknown factors alike.

Study design · 5

Sample size & the limits of randomising

  • A big difference between treatments may show with ~20 per group; a small difference may need thousands — which is why we pool studies in meta-analyses.
  • Balanced groups differ only in the treatment — so the treatment alone explains the result.
  • You can't randomise everything: you can't randomly assign smoking. Those questions need different designs.

Study design · 7

Where the failures go to hide

A favourite trick: remove the patients who recur. "This works… if you delete the failures." These papers get published and read uncritically.

Dropouts

Patients who don't return. You must know why — angry from pain? Or "ran off with a younger partner"? Only one is a treatment failure.

Withdrawals

Must be justified. Beware exclusions on flimsy pretexts ("smoked 11, not ≤10 — out") that quietly change the result.

Missing data

Began with 150, reports on 20 — where are the other 130? Always reconcile the numbers.

Study design · 8

Primary vs secondary outcomes

Primary — what the patient feels

A functioning prosthesis, the ability to chew, quality of life, longevity of the treatment. This is what success means.

Secondary — surrogate measures

Bleeding, pocket depth, 0.1 mm of bone. The patient doesn't notice these — we use them only to predict.

The surrogate trap

"I can't even see 0.1 mm with my glasses."

Surrogate outcomes predict imperfectly: some patients with plaque, bleeding and pockets never lose the implant; some stable ones deteriorate. Useful — but not reliable.

Reading rule

Beware papers building drama on a 0.1–0.2 mm difference. Ask: would the patient ever notice this? If not, how "significant" is it really?

Reading the literature

Narrative ≠ systematic ≠ meta-analysis

Narrative review

No scientific method. Fine for mechanisms — bin it for "what works best." Often published on invitation; popular, not rigorous.

Systematic review

A process: find all studies, appraise risk of bias, base therapy questions on RCTs. Harder than running a trial.

Meta-analysis

A statistical technique pooling quantitative data to increase precision. Only done when combining is valid — not a synonym for systematic review.

Reliability & decay

Even good reviews go stale

  • Cochrane reviews are the most reliable level — if done properly. A flawed review yields completely wrong conclusions.
  • A 20-year-old trial may still hold. A 20-year-old review usually won't — reviews must be updated.
  • The UK exposes you far more to methodology and Cochrane than most countries — use that advantage.
Metrics that mislead

Popularity is not quality

The h-index and impact factor measure citations and popularity — not truth. A paper can be cited heavily precisely because it's wrong, or merely because it agrees with prevailing belief.

Analogy

"Being popular doesn't mean you're intelligent." Likes ≠ rigour — for a person or for a journal.

How we learn

Beware single-technique teaching

For a 5 mm ridge over the nerve, each guru teaches a different technique — and, followed faithfully, each "works." Istvan Urban teaches one; others use interposition grafts or onlay blocks. They can't all be equally right.

The bias

Every master promotes the technique they invented. Textbooks have the same flaw — a book by one author won't fairly present a rival's method. Get 1–2 mentors, but keep appraising.

The first skill
The first skill of a successful dentist is not the hands — it's communication.

Highly skilled operators who can't communicate don't succeed; modest operators who communicate brilliantly do. Patients now arrive pre-informed (and sometimes trying to trap you) — so this will only matter more.

Information hygiene

Social media feeds you what you already believe

The algorithm maximises your time online (for advertising) by serving what you want to hear. Be aware of it. The "best case by the dentist" is never what they do every day — and it has been curated, edited, and now AI-enhanced.

Publication bias

Negative results often go unpublished — especially company-funded ones. The literature can't help you if the data isn't published.

Consolidate

Five things to carry into clinic

1

Question everything. Information is opinion until appraised — including the confident speaker and the polished AI answer.

2

No control, no claim. Demand a comparison, in humans, with adequate numbers and ≥1-year follow-up.

3

Randomisation beats allocation. If the clinician chose the groups, suspect the result.

4

Follow the dropouts. Reconcile the numbers; deleted failures fake success.

5

Treat the patient, not the photo. The primary outcome is the one the patient can feel.
