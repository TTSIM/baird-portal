Mandibular Anatomy on CBCT

The ID canal — your principal landmark

The inferior dental neurovascular bundle enters the mandible at the mandibular foramen, descends through the ramus and body, and exits at the mental foramen. Beyond the mental foramen it continues as the incisive branch of the ID canal into the pre-foraminal mandible.

Why the ID Canal Isn't Always Clear

Cortication depends on surrounding trabeculation

You hope the ID canal looks like a nice distinct corticated circle on coronal cross-sections. It often doesn't — and this is not pathological, it's anatomical.

Why some patients have indistinct ID canals

The "cortication" around the ID canal is formed by surrounding trabecular bone compacting against the neurovascular bundle. In patients with sparsely trabeculated, soft bone there simply isn't enough trabecular volume to form a clear corticated outline. The canal becomes faint or incompletely corticated.

🔍
Workaround: if the canal is hard to see on the coronal view, switch to the sagittal view through the jaw. You can usually trace the path of the canal along its length on sagittal, then correlate that position back to your coronal cross-section. Bonus: the patients with the softest bone and the most indistinct canals are often the same ones where you most need to know exactly where it is.

Inside the ID Canal

What's actually in there

About 80% of the canal volume is nerve. The artery occupies just ~20% — but it sits at the top of the canal, so when an osteotomy hits the canal roof, the first structure traumatised is usually the vessel.

💡
This explains why most ID nerve injuries from drilling are vascular — they cause a haematoma, which causes pressure oedema on the nerve, which is why most resolve over time. True direct nerve laceration is much less common.

Lining Up the Coronal Cross-Section

The only error is human error

For accurate implant planning, you must line up your coronal cross-section to exactly match the angulation at which you intend to place the implant. The scan itself gives mathematically perfect measurements — any error is human error in alignment.

  1. 1 Open the axial view, find the implant site
  2. 2 Rotate the coronal cross-section line to match your planned implant angle
  3. 3 On that aligned coronal slice, measure crest of ridge → roof of ID canal
  4. 4 Measure buccolingual width at the level of your planned fixture
  5. 5 Allow your safety margin (typically 2 mm above ID canal)

Variant: Retromolar Branch of the ID Canal

Common at lower 8s, easy to miss on OPG

The most common variant of the ID canal is a retromolar branch — a branch coming up vertically from the main canal towards the crest of the ridge, distal to the third molar. Mainly relevant for wisdom-tooth surgery (and occasionally ramus grafting).

When to worry
  • Large diameter (close to size of main canal)
  • Close to the tooth being removed

If it's tiny and distant, no concern.

Prevalence
  • Some literature claims up to 25%
  • Realistic clinical experience: ~1 in 20, maybe 1 in 10
  • Much more commonly seen on CBCT than on OPG (only ~7/31 visible on the corresponding OPG in one study)
⚠️
If you traumatise a large branch — even one anterior to the mental foramen or above the main canal — the resulting haematoma and pressure oedema can still track to the main bundle and cause altered sensation. Consent the patient if you identify a significant variant.

Anterior Loop vs Incisive Branch

A widely misunderstood pair

This is one of the commonest naming errors made by clinicians: people see a canal in the first premolar region and call it the "anterior loop". It almost certainly isn't.

Anterior loop

A small forward extension of the ID canal before it loops back to exit at the mental foramen. Located just at or behind the mental foramen. Usually small (a few mm at most). Not always present.

Incisive branch (incisive canal)

The continuation of the ID nerve past the mental foramen — the mental foramen is just where it gives off branches to soft tissues, not the end of the nerve. Runs forward into the pre-foraminal mandible. Usually clearly visible in the first premolar region.

📐
Practical rule: for implants in the first premolar region, measure from the roof of the incisive branch to the crest, not from the lower border to the crest. By the canine region the incisive branch usually disperses into a plexus and is no longer visible — at which point you don't need to worry about it.

Case: The Numb Lip from an Incisive Branch

Why this anatomy matters

Real case
The case

An edentulous mandible patient was treatment-planned for two implants in the anterior mandible to retain an overdenture. They received bilateral mental nerve blocks and infiltrations — no ID block was given. As the surgeon began the right osteotomy, the patient experienced sudden electric-shock pain.

The surgeon panicked, looked back at the CBCT, and only then noticed an unusually prominent incisive canal extending all the way to the central incisor region. They aborted the case. The patient developed temporary numbness of the lip and chin from trauma to the incisive branch — and per the radiologist's understanding, it did not fully resolve.

⚖️
Medico-legal corollary case: a separate dentist who placed full-arch mandibular implants had a patient develop neuralgia-type pain in one side a year later. Post-op CBCT showed an implant placed directly into the incisive branch. The expert witness noted: "you didn't put an implant in the ID canal, you didn't put an implant in the mental foramen, but you put an implant into the incisive branch." Once it's visible on a scan, you're obliged to identify it.

Lingual Canals of the Anterior Mandible

The one place "canal" means vessel only

Most canal-like structures in the jaws are neurovascular. The midline lingual canals of the anterior mandible are the exception — they are purely vascular, carrying terminal branches of the sublingual artery into the mandible at the level of the genial tubercles.

Anatomy
  • Every patient has at least one
  • Some have 1, 2 or even 3
  • Take an oblique course towards the central incisor region
  • Always visible on CBCT (unlike the tiny incisive plexus)
Risk profile
  • Trauma inside the bone usually self-tamponades when the implant is placed
  • Trauma before the artery enters the bone is more dangerous — soft-tissue bleed
  • Greatest concern in atrophic anterior mandibles where the canal becomes very close to the crest

Sublingual Haematoma — A Rare Catastrophe

Why we identify these canals before we drill

Real case
The case

A patient had implants placed in the anterior mandible. The surgeon traumatised one of the lingual artery branches before it entered the bone — outside the alveolus where there's nothing to tamponade the bleed. The bleed was not controlled. Half an hour later, after the patient had left the chair, they returned with a sublingual haematoma.

💀
Historical case: there is a published case from ~30 years ago of death from dental implant surgery caused exactly this way — anterior mandible implant, traumatised lingual artery branch, expanding sublingual haematoma pushed the tongue against the palate, the patient lost their airway and died. This is rare but real — and is why even the central incisor region is not as safe as it looks.
🛡️
Management if you do hit a vessel: control the bleed. Bipolar diathermy if you have it, otherwise pressure or under-suturing. Use the CBCT preoperatively to plan around it — and if it sits very close to where you'll be working in an atrophic ridge, consider doing the case with a more experienced colleague nearby.

Maxillary Anatomy — The Switching Rule

When to worry about nerve vs artery

Simple memory rule
RegionIf you see a canal, worry about…
Posterior mandibleNERVE (ID nerve)
Midline anterior mandibleVESSEL (sublingual artery)
Posterior maxillaVESSEL (PSA artery)
Anterior maxillaNERVE (anterior superior alveolar)

The maxilla carries posterior, middle and anterior superior alveolar nerves and vessels. In the posterior maxilla — which is where you do most of your sinus and implant work — the structure that catches you out is almost always the posterior superior alveolar (PSA) artery.

The Posterior Superior Alveolar Artery

The lateral wall canal you must identify

The PSA artery branches off the maxillary artery (which itself comes off the external carotid). It runs in a bony canal in the lateral wall of the maxillary sinus. For lateral window sinus grafting, you want your window below this canal to avoid a pulsatile arterial bleed.

⚠️
Variant alert: in 95%+ of patients the PSA runs in the lateral wall as expected. Very rarely it can run within the floor of the sinus — and this is dangerous because you can hit it during a crestal-approach (transcrestal) sinus lift, with no easy access to control the bleed (it bleeds upwards into the sinus). Always identify the PSA before any sinus floor work — crestal or lateral approach.

Sinus Anatomy & the Ostium

Why a black sinus is a happy sinus

The maxillary sinus drains into the nasal cavity through its ostium, located high up at the level of the orbital floor. The sinus is lined with respiratory mucosa with cilia that pump secretions upwards towards the ostium — the so-called mucociliary clearance.

Healthy sinus
  • Ostium patent
  • Mucus drains freely into the nose
  • Sinus appears full of black air on CBCT
  • Thin, delicate mucosal lining
Unhealthy sinus
  • Mucosa swells and obstructs the ostium
  • Fluid trapped inside
  • Sinus appears grey/opacified on CBCT
  • Mucosal thickening visible at the periphery
💡
Practical insight: you do not need to scan high enough to actually visualise the ostium itself (which would require ~8 cm height, above the orbital floors and outside dentist scope). If the sinus is full of black air with little mucosal thickening, the ostium has to be patent — otherwise where would the air come from? Black air = patent ostium = healthy sinus, by inference.

Sinus Pathology — Look for a Dental Cause

Don't graft an unhealthy sinus

Maxillary sinusitis is commonly of dental origin. If you find an unhealthy sinus on a CBCT, the first question is: is there a dental cause I can fix?

1. Find a dental cause

Periapical infection? Retained root? Existing OAC? Treat the dental problem and the sinus often resolves.

2. Refer to ENT

No dental cause? Refer for ENT assessment. The patient may need medical management before any surgical sinus work.

3. Don't graft yet

Sinus augmentation on an unhealthy sinus is asking for trouble — graft material gets infected, ostium obstructs further, complications cascade.

⚠️
Always correlate clinically — does the patient have actual sinusitis symptoms? Mucosal thickening on imaging in an asymptomatic patient is very common and may not need treatment.

Foreign Body Migration in the Sinus

Mucociliary clearance pumps everything towards the ostium

If a foreign body — implant, root tip, alginate, graft material — is displaced into the maxillary sinus, it does not stay where it lands. The sinus mucosa engulfs it and the mucociliary system pumps it superiorly, towards the ostium.

📷
The same-day imaging rule: if you're going into a sinus through a Caldwell-Luc to retrieve a foreign body, never rely on a CT scan from 2–3 weeks ago. Take a fresh scan as close to the day of surgery as possible — ideally on the same day. Documented case: a root tip that was in the sinus floor at the time of original imaging had migrated through the ostium and into the nasal cavity by the time of surgery — and the ENT surgeon eventually removed it with a suction tip via the nostril.

Sinus Foreign Body — Three Cases

Different materials, different management

Case 1: Implant

Upper 7 implant displaced into sinus. Caught quickly — still near sinus floor on CBCT. Lateral window approach by oral surgery, OAC closed, antibiotics + decongestants, resolved.

Case 2: Alginate

After extraction the dentist took an alginate impression for a healing plate; alginate displaced into the sinus. Weeks later it had been engulfed by mucosa and pumped towards the ostium. Needed ENT removal.

Case 3: Root tip

Upper 6 palatal root apex pushed into sinus during extraction. Day-of-surgery CBCT showed root no longer there — it had passed through the ostium into the nasal cavity. ENT removed it with suction.

🤔
If something stays at the sinus floor and doesn't migrate (e.g. a sealer extrusion that stayed sub-mucosal), it's often best left alone. The danger comes when something is inside the airspace and gets caught up in mucociliary clearance.

Case: Subtle Apical Pathology

When CBCT changes a borderline diagnosis

Real case · Upper right 6
History

Patient: a lady booked in before going travelling for 6 months. Vague intermittent pain from an upper right 6 (bridge abutment) over the last year, settled with paracetamol. Tooth not obviously TTP. Vitality test unreliable through the crown. No deep pocketing. Periapical: ambiguous — some thought it was suspicious, some not.

CBCT findings
  • Distobuccal root: tiny dot of lucency, possibly normal
  • Palatal root: normal PDL, no apical pathology
  • Mesiobuccal root: definite apical bone loss with remodelling pushing the sinus floor superiorly, fenestration of the buccal cortex, and 2-1-2 canal morphology visible
✅
Outcome: CBCT confidently confirmed chronic apical periodontitis on the MB root. Justified the (risky) decision to drill through the bridge to access and treat the tooth before the patient left for 6 months of travel. This is the "added confidence" half of the RCR justification definition in action.

Beware History Bias on Periapical Imaging

When the clinical story makes you see what isn't there

The thought experiment

Once you have a clinical history — pain, TTP, vitality result — your brain forms a preconception of what you expect to see on the radiograph. You then "see" pathology that confirms it. Show clinicians the same periapical with no clinical history and they often disagree completely about whether there's any lesion at all.

👁️
The post-trauma upper incisor case: a 25-year-old with a hockey-ball history at age 16, and a pink cervical discoloration on the upper right 1 — classic external cervical resorption (ECR). Without history, the periapical looks subtle and ambiguous. With history, ECR jumps out at you. CBCT removes the ambiguity. 3D imaging is most valuable when 2D imaging is making your judgement biased by clinical context.

External Cervical Resorption on CBCT

The pathognomonic appearance

External cervical resorption (ECR) often gets misdiagnosed as internal resorption because the lesion sits inside the tooth. The CBCT appearance is highly characteristic:

External cervical resorption
  • External point of entry — often just a tiny pinpoint channel (occasionally a frank cavity)
  • Lesion spreads around the pulp, peripheral to the root canal
  • Pulp itself is usually preserved until very late
  • Apical pathology develops only when resorption finally reaches the pulp
Internal resorption (contrast)
  • Centred on the root canal space
  • Balloons outward from the canal
  • Always involves the canal directly
🔍
The diagnostic test: if you can't immediately see the external communication, look more carefully — there will be at least a tiny pinpoint channel somewhere on the external surface. Resorption that spreads around the pulp without involving the canal directly = ECR, not internal resorption. Always.

Vertical Root Fracture — The Bone Loss Pattern

Pathognomonic, even when you can't see the crack

Real case · Lower right 7

Aching lower right 7, TTP, devitalised, no deep pocketing on probing. Periapical shows an apical lucency. Why would a tooth with only a small occlusal restoration suddenly devitalise? Suspicion: vertical root fracture.

Bone loss patterns compared
  • Narrow, deep, focal defect running vertically along the root → highly suggestive of VRF
  • Broad, J-shaped lesion → less specific, could be VRF or apical infection tracking
  • Frank endo-perio defect → too late, non-specific
💡
You may never directly visualise the crack — patient motion, low resolution, metal posts, or simply a thin crack line beyond voxel resolution all obscure it. Catch them early on the bone-loss pattern alone, not on direct visualisation. CBCT is more sensitive than periodontal probing for finding these tight focal defects (which often need LA + heavy pressure to probe).

Case: Don't Skip the Periapical

Why CBCT cannot replace 2D imaging

Real case · Upper canine root canal retreatment

Upper right 3 with a metal-ceramic crown and cast post-and-core. Periapical clearly shows a fractured file in the apical third and apparent internal resorption.

CBCT findings (with severe metal artefact)
  • The fractured file is buried in the metal artefact — would have been missed if only CBCT were taken
  • What looked like "internal resorption" on the periapical is actually a lateral periapical lesion superimposed on the root, mimicking internal resorption
⚖️
The lesson: CBCT and 2D imaging each have advantages and disadvantages. Some things are obvious on a periapical and invisible on CBCT (especially anything obscured by metal artefact). Some things are obvious on CBCT and invisible on 2D (anything obscured by superimposition). CBCT is a special test — it adds to bitewings and periapicals, never replaces them.

Beam Hardening — The Black Artefact

Why you cannot diagnose caries on CBCT

The X-ray beam is polychromatic — it contains photons of many different energies. When the beam passes through a dense object (metal crown, post, even enamel) the lower-energy photons are absorbed preferentially. Only the highest-energy photons make it through. The beam literally becomes "harder".

When all those high-energy photons hit the detector, the reconstruction algorithm gets confused, and that confusion is rendered as black low-density streaking — beam hardening artefact.

🚫
Why you cannot diagnose caries on CBCT: beam hardening occurs from anything denser than dentine — including unrestored enamel — so you can get black streaks inside healthy teeth that look convincingly like caries. Guidance is firm: CBCT is not indicated as a method of caries detection. The gold standard for caries remains the radiograph (and clinical examination). If you see something on CBCT that looks like caries, it's not caries until you've reproduced it on a radiograph or proven it clinically.

Metal Artefacts — Black + White Combined

Worst case: two parallel metal objects

Black streaks (low-density)
  • Caused by anything denser than dentine
  • Worst from metal (crowns, posts, implants)
  • Two parallel metal objects → complete blackout between them
  • Mimics caries, mimics peri-implant bone loss
White streaks (high-density)
  • Tend to come more from metal objects
  • Some from gutta-percha and dense restorations
  • Can make root fillings look better-condensed than they are (artefact obscures voids)
  • Can mimic strip perforations
⚠️
Peri-implantitis trap: a CBCT of a clinically sound implant can show a halo of "bone loss" around the fixture that is actually pure beam-hardening artefact. If you really need to assess a failing implant on CBCT, always combine it with a periapical and clinical assessment to triangulate what's real bone loss and what's artefact.

Other Artefacts to Recognise

Motion, scatter and what increases vs decreases artefact

Motion artefact

Blurring from patient movement during the scan. Even a small movement can obscure subtle findings — especially crack lines. Patient cooperation is essential.

Higher dose helps

Higher resolution (more projections) gives the algorithm more information to distinguish real anatomy from artefact. Low-dose scans have worse artefacts, not better.

Exposure factors

Most machines have presets (low / standard / high res). The thing changing under the bonnet is usually exposure time (sampling frequency). Increasing kV also helps but most machines fix kV.

💡
Counter-intuitive but important: if you cut dose by switching to a low-dose preset, you get worse artefacts, not the same artefacts at lower dose. The single biggest factor improving image quality is exposure time (sampling frequency). mA changes the photon flux per unit time. kV (when adjustable) changes photon energy. Increasing any of them improves image quality at the cost of dose.

The 90/10 Rule for Reading CBCT

Where to spend your interpretation effort

A practical rule of thumb

When you read a CBCT scan, spend 90% of your effort on the bone, the roots and the periapical tissues — these are what CBCT does well. Spend only minimal effort on the crowns of the teeth — CBCT is a poor modality for assessing crowns because of the artefacts that come off restorative materials.

AnatomyBest modality
Crowns of teeth, cariesBitewings + periapicals (clinical)
Bone levels, alveolusCBCT ⭐
Roots, root morphologyCBCT ⭐ (with periapicals as backup)
Periapical tissuesCBCT ⭐ (more sensitive than periapical)
Anatomical landmarks (ID canal, sinus, etc.)CBCT ⭐
Soft-tissue pathologyMedical CT or MRI

Justification Revisited

Bringing it all together

RCR definition

"A useful investigation is one of which the result, positive or negative, will inform clinical management and/or add confidence to the clinician's diagnosis."

Realistically, in everyday dentistry CBCT will rarely change the diagnosis or management — but it routinely adds confidence, lets you plan more predictably, and lets you offer the patient the best possible outcome. That's just as good a reason to take the scan.

💡
In implant dentistry, people placed implants for decades without CBCT. The technology is now affordable and low-dose enough that not taking advantage of it is hard to defend. The same trend is now overtaking endodontics — Endodontists who don't use CBCT and a microscope are starting to look out of step with current practice. Orthodontics is probably next.

Don't Forget Scope of Reporting

Anatomy you should NOT report on independently

✓ Within scope
  • Teeth and roots
  • Alveolar bone
  • Periapical tissues
  • ID canal, mental foramen, incisive branch
  • Lingual canals
  • Maxillary sinus floor & up to ⅔ of sinus
  • PSA artery in lateral wall
✗ Outside scope
  • Above orbital floors
  • Ostium of maxillary sinus (need to scan high)
  • Skull base, pituitary fossa
  • TMJs (can't image without ear)
  • Airway, cervical spine
  • Anything not "dentoalveolar"
⚖️
If in doubt → second opinion. A radiology report typically costs ~£100, which is negligible compared to the cost of a missed pituitary tumour, ENT pathology, or cervical spine finding. There's no shame in referring scans for second-opinion reporting.

Quick Check

Click each question to reveal the answer

Q1 ▸ You see a canal-like structure in the first premolar region of the mandible. What is it most likely to be?
The incisive branch of the ID canal, NOT the anterior loop. The anterior loop is at or just behind the mental foramen. The incisive branch is the continuation of the ID nerve forward into the pre-foraminal mandible — distinctly visible in most patients in the first premolar region.
Q2 ▸ Why does black air in the maxillary sinus prove the ostium is patent?
If the ostium were obstructed, the sinus would fill with trapped fluid (mucus / pus) and appear grey or opacified. The only way the sinus can contain black air is if that air came in from the nose — and the only route is through the ostium. So a healthy black-air sinus has, by inference, a patent ostium, even if you can't visualise the ostium directly.
Q3 ▸ What is the pathognomonic CBCT bone-loss pattern of a vertical root fracture?
A narrow, deep, focal intrabony defect running vertically along the length of the root — directly over the line of the crack. Not a broad J-shaped lesion (less specific) and not a frank periodontal defect (too late). The crack itself is often invisible due to artefact, motion or resolution, so you diagnose on the bone-loss pattern alone.
Q4 ▸ Why should you not diagnose caries on CBCT?
Beam hardening artefact from anything denser than dentine — including unrestored enamel — produces black streaks inside teeth that look convincingly like caries. Combined with metal artefacts from any restorations, the rate of false positives is unacceptable. Use bitewings and periapicals (the gold standard) for caries diagnosis.
Q5 ▸ A patient has a foreign body that was displaced into the maxillary sinus 3 weeks ago. You're going in to retrieve it via Caldwell-Luc. What's the imaging rule?
Take a fresh CBCT as close to the day of surgery as possible — ideally on the same day. Mucociliary clearance pumps the foreign body towards the ostium over time, so the location on the original scan is no longer reliable. The object may have moved up the sinus wall, reached the ostium, or even passed through into the nasal cavity.
Q6 ▸ When you find an unhealthy maxillary sinus on CBCT, what is the first question to ask?
Is there a dental cause I can fix? Maxillary sinusitis is commonly of dental origin — periapical infection, retained roots, OAC. Treat the dental cause and the sinus often resolves. Only refer to ENT once you've excluded a dental cause. And never graft an unhealthy sinus.
