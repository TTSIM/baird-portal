Deterministic vs Stochastic Effects

Two completely different mechanisms of harm

Deterministic effects

Caused by cell death. Have a clear threshold dose — below it, nothing happens; above it, the effect happens 100% of the time. The severity scales with dose. Examples: radiation burns, mucositis, hair loss, salivary gland destruction in radiotherapy.

✓ Impossible at diagnostic dental doses — we are nowhere near the threshold.

Stochastic effects

Caused by cell mutation. No threshold — any dose, however low, could in theory induce a mutation that escapes repair and decades later becomes a malignancy. The severity doesn't scale with dose, but the probability does.

⚠ This is the only effect we worry about in dentistry.

💡
Most cell mutations are repaired or apoptosed by the body. Stochastic risk only materialises when a mutation slips through the body's repair mechanisms and survives and goes on to become a malignancy. The probability of all three at dental doses is vanishingly small — but not absolutely zero.

Risk Is Age-Dependent

Why we are extra cautious in children

Why kids are higher risk
  • Paediatric tissues are more radiosensitive — cells are dividing rapidly
  • Long latent period available for mutations to manifest
Why elderly are lower risk
  • Slower cell turnover
  • Crudely: they're more likely to die of something else before any radiation-induced cancer manifests

Why Effective Dose Is the Gold Standard

Whole-body equivalents make doses comparable

The readout you get on your scanner is the absorbed dose — useful for comparing two scans on the same machine, but you can't compare it directly to a chest X-ray, an aeroplane flight or a CT scan of the brain. The tissues being irradiated are too different.

The effective dose takes into account the radiosensitivity of every tissue irradiated and converts the result into a whole-body equivalent dose. This is what lets you make meaningful comparisons.

⚠️
The number on your control panel is not directly comparable to the effective doses we'll discuss in this lecture. They are different units measuring different things. Use the control panel value to compare scans on your own machine; use literature effective doses to compare against other modalities.

Radiosensitive Tissues in the Head & Neck

Why mandible scans cost more dose than maxilla scans

🩸 Bone marrow

One of the most radiosensitive tissues in the body. Why radiation-induced cancers are often haematological — leukaemia, lymphoma. The mandible has more fatty marrow than the maxilla.

🦋 Thyroid gland

Less than bone marrow, but still reasonably radiosensitive. Sits low in the neck — closer to mandibular scans, picks up more scattered radiation.

💧 Salivary glands

Mildly radiosensitive but a huge contributor in OPG dose because the parotids sit right in the path. Excluding the ramus and condyles can roughly halve OPG dose.

💡
Practical implication: a "focused" OPG limited to the dentition (without ramus/condyles/TMJs) delivers about half the dose of a full OPG, because it spares the parotid glands. If you only need periodontal bone levels, you don't need the TMJs in the picture.

Maxilla vs Mandible Scans

Same FOV size, different dose

Maxilla scan
  • Less fatty bone marrow
  • Further from thyroid gland
  • Parotid glands less in beam
  • Lower effective dose
Mandible scan
  • More fatty bone marrow
  • Closer to thyroid for scatter
  • Submandibular glands more in beam
  • Slightly higher effective dose
🎯
A small change in the anatomy you image can disproportionately change the effective dose if it adds or removes a radiosensitive structure from the beam. This is why "scan less anatomy" is the most powerful lever you have.

Background Radiation

The dose you can never escape

The average UK resident is exposed to about 2,700 µSv per year — roughly 7 µSv every single day. There is nothing you can do to avoid it.

Roughly 50% comes from radon gas, mostly from granite in the ground. The rest is cosmic rays, foods, building materials and (about an eighth) artificial sources like medical imaging.

🧠
Remember the number 7 = one day of background radiation. Remember the number 2 = a single periapical or bitewing.

The Linear Non-Threshold Model

The model legislation is built on

Three theoretical models exist for low-dose radiation risk. The linear non-threshold (LNT) model assumes no safe dose — risk rises in direct proportion to dose, all the way down to zero. The threshold model says low doses are safe. The hormesis model claims low doses are even beneficial (stimulating repair).

⚖️
The best available evidence supports LNT, and from a public health perspective writing legislation, you must use the most conservative model. UK and EU radiation legislation is built on LNT — so even if some endodontists or commentators argue otherwise, you must practise within it.

A craniofacial-sized scan that costs you ~200 µSv on CBCT would cost over 1,000 µSv on medical CT — at least 5× the dose. This is why we should encourage ENT and maxillofacial colleagues to use CBCT, not medical CT, when they only need hard-tissue information (facial trauma, orthognathic planning, zygomatic implants).

⚠️
Marketing trap: manufacturers sometimes claim "we can scan both jaws at the same dose as an OPG". Technically true — but only by reducing exposure time by a factor of 4, which means 4× fewer projections and a noticeably lower-quality image. Don't fall for low-dose marketing numbers if it costs you diagnostic confidence.

Patient Communication

IRMER 2017 changed the rules

IRMER 2000 said you didn't need to give patients dose-risk information — that doing so might cause "unnecessary anxiety". IRMER 2017 reversed this: you must now give patients adequate information about benefits and risks of the exposure in advance.

📋
What "adequate" means: the 2020 guidance update confirmed that displaying a poster in your practice is sufficient. The Society of Radiographers / Royal College of Radiologists / IPEM produced an excellent reassuring poster — it explains that dental X-ray doses are similar to everyday environmental sources, and notably never mentions the word cancer.
❌ Don't do this

"I'm going to take a periapical now, there's a 1 in 10 million chance it could give you cancer." Technically true. Practically terrible.

✓ Do this

Display the official poster. If asked, compare doses to everyday things — Brazil nuts, an aeroplane flight, a few days of background radiation.

Thought experiment: if every UK dentist switched tomorrow from periapicals to small-volume CBCTs for every root canal extraction and post-op review, the population dose from dentistry would jump ~20-fold. Individual patient risk would still be tiny — but the profession's contribution to cumulative population dose would be substantial. This is why we use the smallest justifiable FOV every time.

Pregnancy Enquiries

A common point of overreach

Who routinely asks female patients "is there any chance you could be pregnant?" before taking a dental X-ray? About half of clinicians do. You don't need to.

The guidance says (verbatim)

"Such an inquiry is not relevant in dental radiography because the primary beam will not irradiate the pelvic area during a properly conducted dental radiograph or dental CBCT."

😬
If you do choose to ask, the guidance defines childbearing age as 12 to 55 years. Which means the same logic compels you to ask the 13-year-old whose parents are sitting in the corner of your surgery. Awkward. The simpler approach: don't ask routinely.

CBCT in Pregnant Patients

When the answer changes — and when it doesn't

Dental imaging is safe in any trimester — first, second or third. The primary beam doesn't reach the abdomen. Some clinicians choose to delay treatment to the second trimester, but that's a different decision from imaging.

Routine — defer it

Patient on maternity leave wanting an implant consultation? Take the CBCT after the baby is born. It's not urgent. Common sense.

Acute — take the scan

Pregnant patient with an acutely symptomatic wisdom tooth that looks close to the ID canal on OPG, and you've decided to surgically remove it during pregnancy? Take the CBCT. The risk of nerve injury doesn't change because she's pregnant. The medico-legal risk of not imaging is greater than the imaging dose.

⚖️
The harsh truth: "you're more likely to be criticised for not imaging a patient than for imaging them" — particularly in a medico-legal context. If the patient consented to the scan and the guidance says it's safe, taking the scan is the defensible choice.

Justification

The Royal College of Radiologists definition

Definition (RCR)

"A useful investigation is one of which the result, positive or negative, will inform clinical management and/or add confidence to the clinician's diagnosis."

Some people will challenge a CBCT prescription with "how is it going to change your management?" That's a useful test, but it's only half of justification. The RCR definition explicitly includes adding diagnostic confidence — being more sure of your diagnosis, more sure of your treatment plan, or being able to execute that plan more predictably and safely.

Half 1 — change of management
  • New diagnosis revealed
  • Treatment plan altered
  • Surgical approach modified
Half 2 — added confidence ⭐
  • More confident diagnosis
  • Better-planned treatment
  • Safer execution
  • Equally valid grounds for a scan

Justification Workout

Click each scenario — is the CBCT justified?

▸ Scenario 1: Implant planning at upper right 6, sound dentition, no anatomical concerns visible on PA
JUSTIFIED. Although a competent implant could likely be placed without it, the scan adds diagnostic confidence — you can verify bone height, width, sinus floor anatomy, and assess the PSA artery in the lateral sinus wall. The "added confidence" half of the RCR definition makes this justifiable.
▸ Scenario 2: Routine 1-year review of a clinically sound implant, no symptoms, no concerns
NOT JUSTIFIED. For routine review of clinically sound implants, periapical imaging is the standard of care. American AAOMR guidance specifically says: "in the absence of clinical signs or symptoms, use intraoral periapical radiographs." Taking a CBCT every year for routine implant review would add 20× the dose with no clinical benefit.
▸ Scenario 3: Patient with new altered sensation in lower lip after lower 8 extraction
JUSTIFIED. Post-operative altered sensation is a clear clinical indication. CBCT will tell you where the implant or root fragment sits relative to the ID canal and inform management — both halves of the definition met.
▸ Scenario 4: Sinus augmentation prior to implant placement
JUSTIFIED — and arguably mandatory. European guidance (EAO 2011) explicitly says CBCT is essential before any sinus augmentation. You cannot reliably assess sinus health on 2D imaging, and operating on an unhealthy sinus risks serious complications.
▸ Scenario 5: Just placed an implant, all went perfectly, want a baseline image
NOT JUSTIFIED — use a periapical. Even American guidance, which mandates pre-op CBCT, says the immediate post-op baseline image should be a periapical radiograph, not a CBCT. CBCT post-op is reserved as a problem-solving tool when something has gone wrong or when later assessing a failing implant.

The 2D Measurement Trap

Why even simple cases benefit from CBCT

Case: lower left 6 implant planning

A periapical shows 9 mm from crest to ID canal. You plan a 7 mm implant, leaving a 2 mm safety margin. Sounds fine.

You then take a small CBCT. The mid-crestal height is actually only 6 mm — what you saw as the "bony peak" on the periapical was the lingual aspect of the ridge, which is higher than the buccal and mid-crestal bone. A 7 mm implant would have placed 1 mm into the ID canal.

CBCT Is More Than Just Measurement

Five things you should be looking for, every scan

  1. 1. Bone height & width at the planned site, on coronal slices angled to your intended implant trajectory
  2. 2. Bone quality — by structural assessment of the trabecular pattern (not Hounsfield numbers)
  3. 3. Anatomical landmarks — ID canal position and outline, mental foramen, anterior loop, incisive branch, sinus floor, nasal floor
  4. 4. Anatomical variants — retromolar branches, lingual canals, prominent incisive canals, the PSA artery's bony groove in the lateral sinus wall
  5. 5. Sinus health — mucosal thickening, opacification, ostium patency, retained roots, OACs
⚠️
If you "do low-dose for implant planning and high-dose for endodontics" you may pick up the gross things (where is the ID canal?) but miss the subtleties — a small bony groove containing the PSA artery, an unexpected incisive branch, a sclerotic sinus floor. If you're going to scan, scan at diagnostic quality.

Bone Quality — Don't Trust Hounsfield Units

Why the numbers your software gives you are unreliable

Most planning software lets you highlight an area of bone and gives you a numerical "density" value — called a Hounsfield unit, CT number or grey value (all the same thing). Higher number = denser bone, supposedly. The literature consensus is that these numbers should generally be avoided.

Why HUs fail in CBCT
  • Hounsfield units were designed for medical CT, not CBCT — the physics doesn't translate
  • Restoration artefacts (black/white streaking) artificially raise or lower the number
  • Even anatomy outside the FOV can cast shadows that affect the value
  • Most patients have some restorative material in the mouth, so the numbers are nearly always contaminated
What to do instead

Structural evaluation — physically look at the bone with your eyes on a coronal cross-section through the implant site:

  • How thick are the cortical plates?
  • How small/large are the trabecular spaces?
  • How thick are the trabeculae between them?
👁️
Rule of thumb: small trabecular spaces with thick coarse trabeculae = denser, better primary stability. Large radiolucent spaces with paper-thin trabeculae = soft fatty bone, worse primary stability. You can fit this to the Lekholm & Zarb type 1–4 classification if you like classifications, but accept it's subjective.

Why Sinus Augmentation Is the One Mandatory Indication

Even European guidance is firm on this

European guidance is generally permissive about CBCT for implants ("if you want to") — but for sinus augmentation it changes its tone and says CBCT is essential. Why?

2D imaging fails for sinus health
  • Periapical: pretty much impossible to assess sinus
  • OPG: extremely unreliable for sinus pathology
  • You can't see ostium patency on 2D
  • You can't reliably see mucosal thickening or fluid on 2D
Why this matters surgically
  • You should never do sinus augmentation on an unhealthy sinus
  • Underlying dental cause? Fix it first
  • No dental cause? Refer to ENT before grafting
  • Need to identify and avoid the PSA artery in the lateral wall

Bigger or Multiple Smaller?

A counter-intuitive rule and two important exceptions

📏
General rule: if you need to image two distant areas, one larger scan is usually a lower total dose than two separate small scans. Counter-intuitive, but true. The downside is more anatomy to interpret and more chance of incidental findings.
Exception 1: Lower wisdom teeth

Take two separate small volumes, not one big one. You don't want a wisdom tooth at the very edge of the scan — you might miss part of it, and you need to see anatomy distal to the tooth (retromolar branches, variant ID canal anatomy).

Exception 2: Endodontics

Take multiple small volumes. Some machines only allow the highest-resolution settings on the smallest FOVs (because they want to keep their marketing dose figures low). For endo you need maximum image quality, so accept multiple small scans rather than compromising resolution.
