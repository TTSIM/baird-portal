Anatomy

Maxillary Sinus Anatomy

A thorough understanding of sinus anatomy is essential before attempting any sinus procedure — internal or external.

Sinus Structure

  • Pyramidal air cavity within the maxillary bone, bilaterally
  • Lined by the Schneiderian membrane (pseudostratified ciliated epithelium)
  • Connected to the nasal cavity via the ostium — a small drainage canal
  • Air circulates in a 360° wave pattern, mucus drains via cilia
  • Primary function: air filtration and humidification before reaching lungs

The Schneiderian Membrane

  • Thin mucosal lining — as thin as an eggshell lining in places
  • Has two layers: periosteum (outside) + pseudostratified epithelium (inside)
  • Critical to preserve — perforation during sinus graft is a major complication
  • Thickness varies by patient — anatomy and history affect thickness
Pathophysiology

Sinus Pneumatization

After posterior tooth extraction, the sinus floor progressively expands downward — a process called pneumatization. This creates the implant placement challenge.

Why Pneumatization Occurs

  • The sinus is an air-filled cavity — air pressure drives expansion
  • Posterior tooth roots support the sinus floor mechanically
  • On extraction: sinus floor loses support and expands downward
  • Sometimes posterior teeth are so close that roots touch the floor
  • The moment such a tooth is extracted, the floor drops immediately

Clinical Consequences

  • Remaining bone height becomes insufficient for implant placement
  • Short implants alone may not be adequate
  • Implant placed into the sinus disrupts normal mucous wave
  • Chronic sinus infection results — often blamed on the clinician

⚠ "Scratching the floor is allowed — entering it is not." The implant apex can contact the sinus floor but must not perforate into the sinus cavity. Even small perforations risk chronic infection and blocked ostium drainage.

When Do You Need a Sinus Graft?

  • Remaining bone height <5mm → external sinus graft required
  • Remaining bone 5–10mm → internal sinus lift sufficient
  • Remaining bone ≥10mm → implant placed without sinus involvement
  • Also consider implant diameter and whether under-preparation can gain extra height
Historical Context

External Sinus Graft — History & Principle

The external (lateral window) sinus graft was the original procedure — designed for the most severely resorbed posterior maxillae.

Historical Development

  • 1980 — Boyne & Tatum: First described the lateral window approach. Used iliac crest autogenous bone.
  • Late 1980s — Tatum: Showed allograft and alloplastic particles could replace iliac crest harvest.
  • Designed for sinuses with <1–2mm residual bone — extreme cases
  • External approach allows maximum volume creation before implant placement

Indications for External Sinus Graft

  • Residual bone height <5mm
  • Bone width ≥6mm (enough for osteotomy)
  • Sinus floor is oblique (internal lift not possible)
  • Large volume needed — more than internal lift can provide

⚠ High-risk procedure: External sinus graft complication rate is significantly higher than internal lift. Indemnity rises from ~£4–5K to £9–11K. Recommended only after 100+ implants placed.

Surgical Technique

External Sinus Graft — Step by Step

Flap Elevation

Full-thickness mucoperiosteal flap in posterior maxilla. Expose lateral wall of sinus.

Window Preparation

Outline window on lateral wall using piezo. Check CBCT for bone thickness — avoid perforating the Schneiderian membrane during outlining.

Membrane Elevation

Detach the Schneiderian membrane from the sinus walls using "elephant foot" piezo tip. Baby steps — one wrong move perforates. Gentle, methodical elevation.

Window Removal

Remove the bone window. Place it inside the sinus under the elevated membrane — it acts as additional graft material and a floor support.

Graft Placement

Pack bone graft material under the elevated membrane — building from the floor up to the planned implant apex level. Allograft preferred.

Membrane Placement + Closure

Cover the window with a resorbable membrane to prevent fibroblast ingrowth. Reposition and suture flap. Wait 24 weeks (allograft) to 9 months (xenograft).

Risk Management

External Sinus Graft — Complications

Membrane Perforation

  • The most common intra-operative complication
  • Membrane is as thin as eggshell lining — very fragile
  • Small perforation: patch with collagen membrane or suture if accessible
  • Large perforation: abandon procedure, reschedule after healing
  • If bone graft enters the sinus: major complication

⚠ Case Study — Blocked Ostium: A clinician perforated the membrane undetected, packed bone, which migrated through the lining and blocked the ostium. Patient developed chronic sinus infection, destruction of the medial sinus wall, and chronic nasal discharge. Compensation: over £200,000. "Imagine living with nasal discharge for life."

Other Complications

  • Graft infection — especially xenograft if ostium blocked
  • Wound dehiscence — flap opening over the graft
  • Benign paroxysmal positional vertigo (BPPV) — from hydraulic pressure changes
  • Chronic sinusitis — if drainage impaired
  • Partial or complete graft loss

Technique Evolution

Internal Sinus Lift — Development

The internal (crestal) sinus lift was developed as a less invasive alternative for cases with moderate bone height.

Summers Osteotomes — Original Technique (1990s)

  • Developed by Summers for cases with 5–6mm residual bone
  • Drill to 1mm below the sinus floor
  • Use concave-tipped chisels to fracture the last layer of bone upward
  • Pack bone graft through the osteotomy
  • The dome-shaped elevation lifts the sinus floor 3–4mm

⚠ Patient Experience: Tapping with osteotomes was unpleasant for patients — "What are you doing to me?" Cases of tinnitus reported — the hammering transmitted through the ossicles in the middle ear. This technique is now largely abandoned.

Modern Internal Sinus Lift Alternatives

  • Hydraulic pressure: Water injected under the membrane detaches it gently. Low trauma, no tapping.
  • Versah burs: Specially designed drill that "sucks" bone from the sides and pushes it upward via reverse rotation. Acts as its own elevator.
  • Guided approach: Surgical guide confirms exact drill depth (1mm below floor). Second guide places implant. Precision without guesswork.

Modern Standard: Internal sinus lift should be in every implant dentist's skillset. The technique course (Baird) covers this in a dedicated full-day session. Ideally learned before you need it.

Surgical Technique

Internal Sinus Lift — Step by Step

Digital Planning

CBCT: confirm bone height, sinus floor anatomy (flat or oblique). Plan implant length and position. Use guided approach for accuracy.

Guide 1: Drill to 1mm Below Floor

Surgical guide controls osteotomy depth. Stop exactly 1mm below the sinus floor. Do NOT perforate at this stage.

Sinus Floor Elevation

Use Versah burs (reverse rotation), hydraulic pressure, or osteotomes to fracture the last bone layer and elevate the Schneiderian membrane 3–4mm.

Check Before Proceeding

Take periapical X-ray — confirm dome-shaped elevation (success). "Chimney smoke" appearance → membrane perforated. Decision: shorter implant or abandon.

Graft Packing

Pack allograft through the osteotomy — preferred over xenograft. If membrane perforated: allograft resorbs. Xenograft would block ostium permanently.

Guide 2: Implant Placement

Second guide (or guide with stoppers) positions implant correctly. Primary stability from surrounding bone — must have ≥5mm residual bone.

Clinical Decision Making

Sinus Graft Decision Tree

Use this framework to determine the appropriate sinus approach based on CBCT measurements.

Patient needs posterior maxillary implant. What is the residual bone height on CBCT?
≥10mm → No sinus involvement needed. Place implant directly.
5–9mm → Assess sinus floor shape
<5mm → External sinus graft (if width ≥6mm)
Bone height 5–9mm: Is the sinus floor FLAT or OBLIQUE?
Flat floor + ≥6mm width → Internal sinus lift (Versah/hydraulic). Place implant simultaneously.
Oblique floor → External sinus graft required (internal lift technically not feasible on oblique floor).
Internal sinus lift planned: Is bone width ≥6mm?
Yes → Proceed with internal lift + simultaneous implant placement with deep-thread implant for stability.
No → Ridge expansion (Versah) or staged grafting to widen the ridge before sinus involvement.

Evolving Threshold: Initially, 5mm was the threshold for internal sinus lift. With experience and deep-thread implants, many clinicians now perform internal lifts at 3mm. However, start with the conservative 5mm threshold.

Modern Instrumentation

Versah Burs — Multi-function Tool

Versah burs are a versatile drilling system originally designed for internal sinus lift that has expanded into ridge expansion and site optimisation.

Design Principle

  • Bur tips resemble airplane turbine engines
  • Forward rotation: removes bone (conventional drilling)
  • Reverse rotation: condenses and pushes bone laterally/superiorly
  • Sucks bone from the sides and redirects it upward
  • Can simultaneously drill, expand ridge, and elevate sinus floor

Clinical Applications

  • Internal sinus lift: Reverse rotation elevates the sinus floor atraumatically
  • Ridge expansion: Condenses narrow ridges laterally to gain width
  • Site optimisation: Compresses and improves D3/D4 bone density to achieve better primary stability

Training Note: There are only ~12 certified Versah trainers in the world. The Baird course instructor is one of them — trained by the inventor of the technique.

Material Selection

Sinus Graft Material — Why Allograft?

For both internal and external sinus grafting, allograft is the preferred material. Here's why.

Allograft for Sinus — Advantages

  • Remodels into vital bone — implant osseointegrates into live bone
  • Biodegradable — particles dissolve over time
  • If sinus membrane is perforated: allograft particles resorb and do not permanently block the ostium
  • Good primary stability achievable in the grafted sinus
  • Available in large quantities — no harvest site

⚠ Why NOT Xenograft for Sinus? Xenograft is extremely slow to resorb. If the membrane is accidentally perforated and xenograft particles enter the sinus, they remain permanently. Particles can block the ostium — leading to permanent drainage obstruction and chronic sinusitis. Allograft is far safer in the sinus environment.

Internal Sinus: Allograft + Versah

  • Pack allograft into the osteotomy first
  • Then use Versah in reverse to push allograft upward and lift the membrane
  • Allograft acts both as graft material and hydraulic medium
  • Simultaneous implant placed with second guide immediately after
Socket Preservation

Socket Grafting — Principles

Socket grafting is performed at the time of tooth extraction to preserve ridge volume. Without it, extraction leads to progressive and irreversible bone loss.

When to Socket Graft

  • Patient wants an implant but cannot proceed immediately
  • Any tooth extraction where a bridge pontic will be needed
  • Cases where future implant is likely
  • "Ideally, every tooth you extract should be socket grafted"
  • Even if doing an immediate or early implant: socket grafting the jumping gap IS part of that procedure

Why It's Underutilised

  • Adds cost and time to what feels like a "simple" extraction
  • Patients often decline when they don't understand the long-term benefit
  • Many clinicians underrate it — then face difficult reconstruction later
  • Education: show patients the animation comparing socket-grafted vs ungrafted pontic sites

Bridge Argument: Even for a three-unit bridge (no implant): socket grafting at extraction preserves pontic site volume → better bridge design → improved hygiene. Always worth discussing with the restoring dentist.

Clinical Decision

Socket Graft Material — Re-entry Timing Determines Choice

The single most important factor in socket graft material selection is when you plan to re-enter the site.

Re-entry: ~6 months (implant)
→
Use Allograft

Allograft remodels into vital bone within 6 months. On re-entry: good quality bone for implant primary stability. Xenograft would still be partially unremodelled at this timepoint.

Re-entry: 2–5 years
→
Use Xenograft

Xenograft's slow resorption provides long-term volume maintenance. The site looks good years later. Allograft would have fully resorbed by then — leaving minimal ridge preservation benefit.

No implant / Bridge pontic only
→
Use Xenograft

Need maximum long-term volume stability for aesthetic pontic profile. Xenograft excels here. Vital bone formation is not required — volume maintenance is the priority.

Common Question: "If I use xenograft for socket grafting and later want an implant, will it be OK?" Answer: After 5+ years, most xenograft has resorbed. But if re-entry is at 6 months, xenograft won't be integrated — poor implant bed. Match material to timeline.

Complex Reconstruction

Staged Bone Grafting — When & Why

Staged bone grafting means building the bone first — then placing the implant in a separate surgery weeks to months later.

Indications

  • Cannot place implant in correct prosthetic position (insufficient bone)
  • Cannot achieve adequate primary stability (<25Ncm)
  • Defect has 0 or 1 wall — GBR will fail (no scaffold, no blood supply)
  • Knife-edge ridge defect
  • Large-volume deficiency requiring block bone or sausage technique

Techniques

  • Block graft: Solid piece of bone (autogenous or human tissue bank) fixed with screws. Highest volume gain possible.
  • Sausage technique: Particulate graft (allograft + autogenous) applied in layers with a rigid membrane. Builds volume without a block.
  • Bone plates: Thin cortical plates used to contain particulate graft.
  • Layering on titanium mesh: Mesh tent filled with graft — good for large horizontal defects.
Staged Grafting

Block Grafts — Harvest Sites & Options

Autogenous Block Harvest Sites

  • Ascending ramus: Dense cortical bone — excellent quality. Minimal patient morbidity.
  • Chin (symphysis): Good volume. More patient discomfort post-operatively.
  • Body of mandible: Option for small to medium volumes.
  • Iliac crest: Largest volumes — maximum morbidity. Only for major reconstruction (e.g. full arch).
  • Calvarium / scalp: Used in some Italian schools for maximum cortical bone — extreme approach.

Allograft Block (Human Tissue Bank)

  • Processed cadaver block bone from a tissue bank
  • Available in standard sizes — can be shaped and adapted
  • Eliminates the need for a second surgical site
  • Slower remodelling than autogenous (24 weeks to re-enter)
  • Excellent option for trauma cases where autogenous harvest is an extra burden

Key Rule: "We do not go from –10 to +10 in one surgery." A staged block graft takes you from the deficit to approximately neutral (+1). Then a second surgery (implant + GBR) takes you to the final position.

Autogenous Collection

Bone Scraping for Staged Grafts

For staged grafts requiring autogenous bone, scraping offers a less traumatic collection method than a formal block harvest.

Bone Scraper Technique

  • Use a bone scraper within the already-reflected flap
  • Scrape from superior to inferior with firm pressure
  • Go from chin, ascending ramus, or body of mandible
  • Bone collects in the scraper chamber — not dispersed around the field
  • Much less painful for patients than block harvest — "like having a wisdom tooth removed, easier"
  • Sharpen scrapers annually or use disposable safe scrapers

Combining Autogenous + Allograft

  • Mix scraped autogenous with allograft to extend volume
  • Autogenous provides growth factors and osteogenic cells
  • Allograft provides volume and osteoconductivity
  • Better clinical results than allograft alone for large defects

Patient Communication: "Collecting the bone will be like having a wisdom tooth out — in fact, even easier. We won't be cutting a block — just scraping." This reduces patient anxiety significantly about the staged approach.

Clinical Case

Case Study: High Smile Line — Staged Was Correct

Second Opinion — Presentation

Patient "Star" had been told by another clinician: "I can do the implant with GBR." She wasn't comfortable and sought a second opinion. High smile line — aesthetic risk is maximum.

Assessment: Implant Would Float in Air

Insufficient bone to place implant in correct prosthetic position AND achieve primary stability. The other clinician's plan would have resulted in a long clinical crown — unacceptable for a high smile line patient.

Decision: Staged Block Graft

Block graft (autogenous or human tissue bank) placed first. Secured in position. Patient informed of timeline: "Ideal scenario requires two surgeries with a 6-month wait between them."

After Integration: Implant Placed

Now sufficient bone for correct prosthetic position AND primary stability. Implant placed with simultaneous GBR for convexity. Good outcome achieved.

Key Learning: Explaining the staged approach justifies the additional fee (£2,400 for bone grafting) by helping patients understand WHY you're doing it. "The more you educate your patients, the better the acceptance will be."

Staged Technique

The Sausage / Layering Technique

The sausage technique is particulate staged grafting — building bone without a solid block, using layers of graft material contained by a rigid membrane.

Technique Principle

  • Build a "sausage" shape of bone graft on the deficient ridge
  • Layer autogenous (inner) and allograft/xenograft (outer)
  • Contain with a rigid membrane (magnesium or titanium mesh)
  • Stabilise membrane with titanium pins
  • Suture tension-free

⚠ Case Learning: "I grafted the implants perfectly — but forgot to graft the central pontic. The imperfect sausage accidentally grafted the lateral pontic, but not the central. I ended up with a ridge-lap at the central pontic." Always plan the full restorative span including every pontic site.

Advantages over Block

  • No donor site surgery needed (uses allograft/xenograft)
  • Can build irregular shapes to match planned restoration
  • Less patient morbidity than block harvest
  • Magnesium membrane provides rigidity without permanent foreign body

Graft to Pontic Sites Too

  • Bridge case: graft under ALL pontic sites — not just implant sites
  • Pontic site: xenograft for long-term volume (vital bone not needed)
  • Implant sites: allograft or autogenous for vital bone
  • Result: flat emergence profile, hygienic ovate pontics, no ridge-lap
Planning Complex Cases

Complex Case Planning — Think Prosthetics First

Every complex bone grafting case should begin with the prosthetic plan — not the surgical plan.

The Correct Workflow

  • Decide: what prosthesis does this patient need?
  • Decide: which implant positions will support that prosthesis?
  • Decide: what is the ideal position for each implant (prosthetically)?
  • Then: assess what bone exists vs what bone is needed
  • Then: plan bone grafting to enable the prosthetically correct position

Old school approach: "Place implant where bone exists, worry about prosthesis later." This is backwards. "Old school will place the implant in the bone and then worry about prosthesis. You and I will say: I have a defect, let's decide which prosthesis, then plan the bone grafting to enable it."

Implant Number for Span

  • Replacing 3 anterior teeth (2 centrals + lateral): typically 2 implants, not 3
  • Implant-to-pontic papilla looks better than implant-to-implant papilla
  • Mechanically: 3 implants + 2 units → cantilever risk; 2 implants + pontic → better load distribution
  • Leaving the lateral as a pontic: aesthetically and mechanically preferable

Inter-implant Space: Wider space between implants = better papilla support + better prosthetic design. Choose smaller diameter implants if needed to maintain adequate inter-implant distance (≥3mm minimum, ideally more).

Clinical Case

Case Study: Young Patient — Block + GBR + Pontic Grafting

Presentation: Iraqi Medical Student

5th-year medical student, about to graduate. Lost multiple anterior teeth in childhood. Very little residual bone — significant congenital deficit. Needed life-changing restoration.

Staged Block Grafts

Human tissue bank blocks placed — two separate staged grafts to build ridge to an acceptable level. Followed by integration period.

Implant Placement — Two Laterals

Two implants placed at lateral incisor positions. GBR performed around the implants AND in the central pontic region — important not to miss the pontic site.

Soft Tissue Augmentation

Connective tissue grafts added for volume and soft tissue quality. Further enhances the emergence profile.

Final Bridge

Clean, hygienic three-unit bridge with ovate pontics at the central. Perfect emergence through the ridge. Patient can clean properly. The central pontic grafting made this possible.

Key Learning: The pontic site grafting was done both for the implants AND the central pontic. This is what makes the final restoration truly excellent — the grafting strategy was driven by the prosthetic outcome, not just the surgical convenience.

Complete Framework

Master Decision Framework — Complete Bone Management

An integrated decision pathway connecting all four bone grafting types.

Step 1: Prosthetic Planning

Define the prosthetic goal. Determine ideal implant positions. Then assess what bone you have.

Step 2: Bone Assessment

CBCT analysis: height, width, density, sinus floor, nerve position, defect wall count, phenotype.

Step 3: Choose Grafting Strategy

Socket graft at extraction → Staged graft if large deficit → Simultaneous GBR at implant → Sinus lift if posterior maxilla.

Step 4: Material Selection

Inner layer: autogenous/allograft. Outer: xenograft. Sinus: allograft. Socket: timing-dependent. Membrane: complexity-dependent.

Step 5: Soft Tissue + Prosthetics

Temporary crown sculpting → final impression → screw-retained crown → review. The pink must be right for the white to look right.

Summary

Lecture 3 — Key Takeaways

🌬

Sinus Decision

<5mm → External (lateral window). 5–9mm flat floor → Internal lift. ≥10mm → No sinus involvement. Oblique floor always needs external approach.

⚠

Schneiderian Membrane

As thin as an eggshell — treat with extreme care. If perforated: allograft is forgiving (resorbs). Xenograft can permanently block the ostium → chronic sinusitis.

🪥

Socket Grafting

6 months re-entry → allograft. 2–5 years or bridge only → xenograft. Match material to timeline every time.

🏗

Staged Bone Grafting

For 0–1 wall defects or insufficient bone for prosthetic implant position. Block or sausage technique. Plan from the prosthesis backwards.

⏱

Healing Times

Autogenous: 12–16w. Allograft: 24w. Xenograft: 8–9 months (staged). PTFE membrane extends all to 24w. Internal sinus: simultaneous placement possible.

🎯

Pontic Sites Too

Graft every pontic site in your implant bridge span. Xenograft for pontics (volume only). This is what separates good prosthetics from great prosthetics.

Course Summary

Complete Course — Core Messages

Biology first, always. Every surgical decision must respect post-extraction biology. Bundle bone will resorb. Plan for it — don't react to it.

Prosthetic position is non-negotiable. Build the bone so the implant can go in the right place — never compromise position to avoid grafting.

Layering is not optional. Inner: autogenous or allograft. Outer: xenograft. Never xenograft directly on exposed threads. This single distinction makes you a Level 3 implantologist.

Graft pontic sites too. Your bridge prosthesis is only as good as the bone underneath it. A ridge-lap at the pontic is a failure — it's avoidable with socket grafting.

Educate to justify. Every staged approach, every graft, every additional cost — can be justified with education. Patients who understand biology will accept the correct treatment.
