The Modern Challenge
Why implant dentistry evolved beyond osseointegration

In the 1970s, the challenge was getting titanium to integrate with bone. Today — any titanium rod will integrate.

The real challenges of modern implant dentistry are:

  • Preserving osseointegration over the long term
  • Maintaining bone levels which support soft tissue
  • Achieving stable, healthy soft tissue — not just aesthetic soft tissue
  • Understanding that losing soft tissue = losing bone and vice versa
Key Principle

An implant is a foreign body. Success depends on building the right biological environment around it — adequate bone and stable soft tissue — not merely on the implant itself integrating.

Peri-Implant Zones
The four anatomical zones around a dental implant — click each to explore
E Zone (Aesthetic Zone)
The visible portion of the peri-implant mucosa. What the patient sees. Goal: correct colour, texture, and emergence profile. Supported by the bone and tissue beneath it.
B Zone (Biological Seal)
Where hemidesmosomal attachment occurs to the abutment. In health, this creates a partial seal slowing bacterial spread toward the bone. Requires clean, polished titanium or polished (not glazed) zirconia.
C Zone (Crestal Zone)
If bacteria reach here — game over. The body dispatches leukocytes which damage surrounding bone non-selectively. Result: marginal bone loss and initiation of peri-implantitis.
Dead / Transitional Zone
The zone between B and C. Thickness influenced by: soft tissue thickness, oral hygiene, and abutment material/polish. Good soft tissue thickness = deeper dead zone = better bone protection.
Hemidesmosomal Attachment
The biological seal around implant abutments

The hemidesmosomal attachment is the implant's equivalent of the junctional epithelium — a partial biological seal between mucosa and abutment.

Hemidesmosomes attach to:

✓ Clean, polished titanium
✓ Polished (not glazed) zirconia
✗ Glazed zirconia — too smooth, forms a pocket instead

Clinical Implication

When removing a screw-retained crown from a healthy site, you will see bleeding points — these are disturbed hemidesmosomal attachments, NOT disease. Avoid unnecessary crown removal at annual reviews to preserve this seal.

Important — Glazed Zirconia

Labs may glaze restoration all the way subgingivally for convenience. This creates a super-smooth surface where hemidesmosomes cannot attach — resulting in a permanent pocket and pathway for bacterial ingress. Always specify: glaze stops at the gingival margin.

The bone has reduced protection. Bone resorption begins. Less buffer against bacterial ingress to the crestal zone.

Consequences of Inadequate Soft Tissue
The clinical cascade when the 2+3 rule is violated
Scenario: Mucosa only 1mm thick, implant placed at bone level

Biological width kicks in → bone resorbs to create 3mm height → implant threads become exposed → marginal bone loss → risk of peri-implantitis

Scenario: Mucosa 3mm thick, implant placed at bone level

Biological width satisfied → bone remains stable → threads covered → healthy peri-implant tissues long term

Correct Surgical Protocol

Never place implants in a fixed position relative to bone. Always: (1) identify the future gingival margin, (2) measure 3–4mm apically — that is where the implant shoulder should sit. If mucosa is 1mm, the implant must be 2mm below the bone.

Biological Width
Supracrestal attachment — the body's non-negotiable dimension

Biological width is the minimum distance the body will maintain between the base of the sulcus/mucosa and the crestal bone. It is not optional — if violated, the bone resorbs.

Natural Teeth
  • Distance: crest of bone → base of sulcus
  • Includes: sulcular epithelium + junctional epithelium + connective tissue
  • ≈ 2–3 mm total
  • Has sulcular epithelium as partial barrier
Dental Implants
  • Distance: crest of mucosa → crest of bone
  • NO sulcular epithelium — open continuous space
  • ≈ 3–4 mm required
  • Greater height needed — less protection
Modern Terminology

The term "biological width" is increasingly replaced by "supracrestal attachment" in recent literature. Be aware of both terms in your reading.

Abutment Design & Crown Contour
How prosthetic choices directly influence soft tissue stability
Correct — Concave Subgingival Profile

Concave or flat subgingival contour allows soft tissue to rest without pressure. No recession caused by crown seating. Provides adequate space for hemidesmosomal attachment.

Incorrect — Convex Subgingival Profile

As the crown is tightened, convex contour pushes outward on the soft tissue. If tissue is thin, this causes immediate recession. Pressure impairs blood supply and attachment.

Posterior Teeth — Emergence Profile

A molar abutment should begin as a circle (at the implant level) and transition to a rectangular/square shape at the crown base — matching the tooth form. Circle-to-circle emergence creates food traps and cleaning difficulties.

Lab Instructions — Always Specify

• Glaze only supragingival surfaces
• B and C zones must be polished, not glazed
• Subgingival emergence profile must be concave or flat
• Clean all lab work before fitting (remove dust)

Key Message

Wrong abutment design = wrong subgingival contour = pressure on thin tissue = recession = bone loss. The lab technician must understand peri-implant biology, not just aesthetics.

Effects of Tooth Loss on Soft Tissue
Understanding the deficit you start from before placing any implant

The moment a tooth is extracted, predictable and irreversible soft tissue changes begin. Understanding this is essential to realistic planning.

Volume Loss

Rupture of periodontal ligaments → immediate recession of ≥1mm even in ideal extractions. Mucosa loses its attachment scaffold.

Mucogingival Changes

The mucogingival line migrates coronally. Keratinized mucosa width decreases. Mobile mucosa expands into the extraction site.

Papilla Loss

Papilla between adjacent natural teeth can be up to 5mm. After extraction: contact area lost → papilla regresses to normal mucosa thickness of 3–4mm. Loss of bony peaks accelerates this.

Clinical Take-Home

You start from minus five the moment a tooth is removed. Simply placing an implant without addressing soft tissue means the implant will never look natural. Active soft tissue management is not optional for high aesthetic cases.

Five Factors for Papilla Stability
What the interdental papilla requires to survive and thrive

Papilla between natural teeth grows to 5mm. After tooth loss it regresses to normal mucosa (~3–4mm). For papilla to grow back, all five conditions should be met:

  • 1. Contact Area — No contact area, no papilla. The contact point defines where the papilla apex sits.
  • 2. Bony Peaks — Interdental and mesial/distal bony crests must be present and healthy. Papilla grows ~3–4mm from bone crest.
  • 3. Embrasure Space — There must be a triangular space for papilla to grow into. Square teeth eliminate embrasures and eliminate the papilla.
  • 4. Oral Hygiene — The embrasure must be clean. Plaque-filled embrasures will not allow papilla creep or growth.
  • 5. Soft Tissue Thickness — Adequate tissue volume/thickness at the site. Thin tissue struggles to regenerate papilla height.
Gingival Anatomy — Key Zones
Understanding the tissue types and their roles
  • Free Marginal Gingiva — First 1mm of tissue. Keratinized. Separated from attached gingiva by the free gingival groove. Lost after extraction.
  • Attached Gingiva — Keratinized, firmly bound to bone. Between free gingival groove and mucogingival line.
  • Mucogingival Junction (MGJ) — The boundary between attached gingiva and mobile alveolar mucosa. Critical landmark for surgical planning.
  • Alveolar Mucosa — Non-keratinized, mobile, stretchy. Inferior to the MGJ. Not suitable as periimplant tissue.
Implant vs Tooth — Important Difference

Around teeth: keratinized mucosa = attached gingiva + free marginal gingiva (larger zone). Around implants: no free marginal gingiva exists — keratinized mucosa ≡ attached mucosa. This makes implants more vulnerable to mucosa width deficiency.

Why Keratinized Mucosa Matters
Evidence-based importance of keratinized tissue around implants

Minimum 2mm of keratinized mucosa around dental implants is now evidence-based, not just opinion. Without it:

Increased Disease Risk

Higher plaque accumulation. Greater bleeding and inflammation. Elevated peri-implantitis risk. More marginal bone loss over time.

Patient Discomfort

Mobile mucosa is painful to brush. Patients with non-keratinized tissue around implants report significant discomfort with oral hygiene. This perpetuates plaque retention.

Progressive Recession

Longitudinal studies show greater mucosal recession over time where keratinized mucosa is minimal. Less KM = more recession. More recession = harder to clean = more recession.

Evidence Summary (Italian Academy / Academy of Osseointegration)

Keratinized mucosa ≥2mm associated with: less plaque, less inflammation, less bleeding, less bone loss, less recession, better patient satisfaction, and easier oral hygiene maintenance. This is now established evidence, not expert opinion.

Gingival Phenotype Classification
Thick vs thin — the most clinically important patient characteristic

Phenotype refers to the combined thickness of mucosa AND underlying bone. They are biologically linked. The threshold is 2mm mucosa thickness.

✦ Thick Phenotype (>2mm)
  • Flat gingival architecture
  • Short, wide (square) teeth
  • Short, broad papillae
  • Contact area more apical
  • Thick cortical labial plate
  • More coronal alveolar crest
  • Dense, fibrous connective tissue
  • Rich blood supply
✦ Thin Phenotype (<2mm)
  • High scalloped gingival architecture
  • Long, narrow (triangular) teeth
  • Long, pointed papillae
  • Contact area near incisal edge
  • Thin or absent labial plate
  • More apical alveolar crest
  • Less dense connective tissue
  • Reduced blood supply
How to Assess at the Chairside

Gently insert a periodontal probe into the sulcus of the adjacent tooth mid-labially. If you can see the metal shadow through the tissue — thin phenotype. If not visible — thick phenotype.

Clinical Consequences of Phenotype
How phenotype predicts surgical and aesthetic outcomes
Thick Phenotype — Advantages

• Less bone loss after extraction (5–20% of labial plate)
• Resistant to recession — tissue volume maintained
• Predictable surgical healing
• Better blood supply → better healing
• Papillae recover well after flap elevation

Thick Phenotype — Caution

• Dense fibrous tissue → MORE scarring
• Avoid unnecessary vertical incisions — scars are visible and persist for years
• Inflammatory problems → pocketing, not recession

Thin Phenotype Warning

After extraction: up to 50% bone loss on labial plate. Start with 8mm → end with 3.5–4mm. Immediate implant placement considered contraindicated by many authors in thin phenotype. Plan accordingly before extraction.

Phenotype & Surgical Planning
Matching flap design and timing to phenotype
Thick Phenotype — Surgical Notes
  • Immediate placement possible and predictable
  • Envelope flaps tolerated — papillae will recover
  • Avoid unnecessary vertical lines — scarring is dense and visible
  • If vertical lines needed: scar will eventually fade (5+ years)
  • Inflammation → pocketing not recession
  • If scarring occurs: preferable to black triangles
Thin Phenotype — Surgical Notes
  • Immediate placement: challenging to contraindicated
  • Early replacement (8 weeks post-extraction) preferred — allows thin to become thick
  • Vertical lines less problematic — scarring is minimal
  • High risk of black triangles if papillae disturbed
  • Papillary-preserving flap strongly preferred
  • Socket grafting recommended at extraction for aesthetic cases
CBCT Assessment of Phenotype

On CBCT at 8 weeks post-extraction: Thick phenotype → dense white fill visible in socket (woven bone). Thin phenotype → grey fill (likely granulation tissue). Key distinction: if bounded by remaining labial wall, assume woven bone; if no labial wall present, assume granulation tissue.

Pre-Extraction Assessment
Four factors to evaluate before extracting any tooth being considered for implant

Assessment before extraction prevents post-operative surprises and allows honest treatment planning discussions with patients.

✓
Width of Keratinized Mucosa — Measure around tooth to be extracted. If ≤2mm, pre-warn patient about need for free gingival graft. Discuss timing: before or after surgery.
✓
Phenotype Assessment — Thin or thick? Use the probe shadow test. Determines implant timing, flap design strategy, risk of bone loss and recession.
✓
Soft Tissue Thickness — Probe at mid-labial to assess mucosal depth. Thickness determines biological width and implant depth positioning. Thin phenotype needs implant placed deeper.
✓
Supracrestal Attachment (Biological Width) — Assess health of adjacent teeth and attachment levels. Will inform flap design decisions and bone sounding at surgery.
Digital Planning & Soft Tissue
Integrating soft tissue assessment into CBCT and STL planning

In digital guided surgery, the lab technician needs to know both where the future gingival margin will be and the phenotype of the mucosa to correctly position the implant shoulder.

Lab Input Required

Merge CBCT + STL. Create digital wax-up showing future gingival margin. Measure 3–4mm from that margin → implant shoulder position. Lab sees soft tissue thickness on CBCT scan.

Phenotype Default Rule

If you do not tell the lab technician the phenotype, they will assume 4mm (thin phenotype protocol). Always specify: thick phenotype (3mm margin) or thin phenotype (4mm margin).

Digital vs Freehand

With digital guides: all planning is done on the computer. During surgery you are essentially blind — trust the guide. Remove the guide and check before placing the implant. Verify position.

Planning Error — Implant Too Shallow

If digital plan shows implant only 2mm below gingival margin: incorrect. Biological width of 3–4mm will be needed → bone will resorb to achieve it. Either use a shorter implant, plan for internal sinus lift, or accept future marginal bone loss.

Five Routes to Ideal Soft Tissue
The management toolkit — an overview

Achieving and maintaining ideal peri-implant soft tissue involves five clinical strategies — all will be covered throughout the course:

01
Flap Design — The single most important decision. Wrong flap = wrong result. One size does not fit all. Lecture 3 covers this comprehensively.
02
Tension-Free Closure — Requires periosteal release. Wound under tension = dehiscence = bacterial ingress = bone loss. Covered in Lecture 2.
03
Soft Tissue Grafting — Connective tissue graft for thickness; free gingival graft for keratinized mucosa width. Specialist topic, dedicated day on course.
04
Suturing Technique — Vertical and horizontal mattress techniques to maintain papilla height. Interrupted sutures flatten papillae — avoid where possible.
05
Prosthetic Soft Tissue Sculpting — Emergence profile, crown contour, and temporary restoration design to guide and maintain soft tissue form.
Pink Aesthetics Score
Measuring peri-implant soft tissue outcomes — the Belser criteria

The pink aesthetic score (PAS) evaluates peri-implant soft tissue in three dimensions — a frontal view alone is insufficient.

Pink Aesthetic Score Components

Evaluated in 3D:
• Mesial papilla presence
• Distal papilla presence
• Soft tissue level / curvature
• Soft tissue contour
• Soft tissue colour
• Soft tissue texture
• Alveolar process (convexity)

Convexity — The Critical Zone

Root convexity / soft tissue colour and texture score is 2/10 points on the Belser scale. Some consider this low — but convexity (especially viewed occlusally) separates average from exceptional outcomes. This is what defines truly natural-looking implant restorations.

Above Average Implantology

Implant in the right 3D position + ≥2mm bone on all surfaces + correct soft tissue height and thickness = the benchmark for excellence. Not just osseointegration.

Soft Tissue Integration
Beyond aesthetics — biological protection as the primary goal

Soft tissue integration is not about making the tissue look nice — it is about creating a stable biological environment that protects the implant for the long term.

Stability

Stable soft tissue protects underlying bone. Unstable or inflamed tissue = progressive bone loss. Stability is dependent on adequate thickness, adequate keratinized mucosa, and correct abutment design.

Blood Supply

Adequate tissue thickness = adequate blood supply = adequate resistance to infection and mechanical challenge. Thin tissue = poor vascular bed = reduced healing capacity.

Health Maintenance

Healthy tissue = less plaque = less peri-implantitis risk. Keratinized mucosa = easier patient hygiene = lower inflammation burden. The cycle works in either direction — virtuous or vicious.

Core Message

Success in implant dentistry is all about getting the soft tissue right. Once the soft pink tissue is correct, the white restorative outcome becomes a matter of communication between you, the patient, and the laboratory.

Connective Tissue vs Free Gingival Graft
Understanding when to use each soft tissue augmentation approach
Connective Tissue Graft (CTG)
  • Epithelium is removed from the graft
  • Graft placed under the flap (buried)
  • Addresses: lack of tissue THICKNESS
  • Acts like padding under a carpet
  • Blood supply from periosteum and flap
  • Used with immediate placement
  • Connective tissue heals to connective tissue
Free Gingival Graft (FGG)
  • Epithelium is kept on the graft
  • Graft placed on top of periosteum
  • Addresses: lack of keratinized mucosa WIDTH
  • Taken from palate (best) or edentulous ridge
  • Blood supply from underlying periosteum
  • NOT done at same time as bone grafting
  • Do before or after surgery, not simultaneously
Critical Rule

Do NOT put FGG under a flap — epithelium on epithelium will not attach (creates pocket). Do NOT put CTG exposed on the surface — without epithelium, it will disappear without protection.

Overdenture Cases — Different Rules
How implant planning differs for denture-retained prostheses

Overdenture implant planning is fundamentally different from fixed prosthetic implant planning. It deserves its own decision framework.

Overdenture Advantages

• Smaller implants suitable (3.3mm)
• Placement position is more palatal/lingual — not under the tooth position
• Crest placement is acceptable and desirable
• Less demand on bone volume
• Simpler restorative pathway

Denture Wearers — Soft Tissue Challenge

Long-term denture wearers typically have very thin, atrophic mucosa. Combined with thin phenotype from atrophy, this increases biological width demands and complicates soft tissue management.

Positioning Rules

For overdentures: do not place implant directly under the tooth position — that is incorrect for overdenture biomechanics. Implants for denture retention use different positioning and distribution rules.

Course Coverage

A full dedicated masterclass covers overdenture planning, surgery, and restoration. By course end: competent to perform overdentures; fixed full-arch cases require additional advanced training beyond this course.

Mucosal Architecture After Extraction
What happens to soft tissue quantity and quality post-extraction
Immediate Changes (0–24hr)

Periodontal ligament fibres rupture. Even the most atraumatic extraction causes ≥1mm recession. Blood clot fills socket. Mucosa loses coronal support.

Early Changes (0–8 weeks)

Thin phenotype: fibroblasts dominate → granulation tissue fills socket. Thick phenotype: osteoblasts from 3 walls prevail → woven bone forms. Keratinized mucosa width decreases.

Long-term Changes

Ridge atrophy continues. Thin phenotype: major volume loss. Thick phenotype: moderate loss but strong cortical residual. Mobile mucosa creeps coronally into previous keratinized zone.

Early Replacement Protocol (8 weeks)

One significant advantage of early replacement (8 weeks post-extraction): thin phenotype tissue that was thin before extraction becomes thicker mucosa by 8 weeks as it heals. This is the ideal window to "cheat" — if sufficient bone remains (≥3mm) for primary stability, you can place into a more favourable tissue environment.

Implant Position & Soft Tissue
The T-Concept and correct 3D implant positioning

Implant position is the single most important factor in long-term soft tissue stability. Perfect soft tissue management is wasted if the implant is in the wrong position.

T-Concept (Palatal Placement)

Place implant as palatally as possible within the socket, aiming for the cingulum. This:
• Preserves labial bone
• Allows bone graft to fill the labial gap
• Creates better emergence profile
• Reduces labial plate pressure

Mesiodistal Position

≥1.5mm from adjacent tooth roots. ≥3mm between adjacent implants. These distances protect the interdental bone peaks and therefore the papillae.

Depth (Apicocoronal)

Always referenced to future gingival margin, not to bone. Measure 3–4mm apical to future GM. Deeper for thin phenotype (4mm), shallower for thick (3mm). Never compromise depth for fear of sinus proximity — use shorter implant or sinus lift.

Wrong Position = Inevitable Failure

An implant in the wrong position cannot be saved by any amount of soft tissue grafting. Correct position is the non-negotiable foundation. Everything else is secondary.

Labial Plate Preservation

Adequate labial plate bone is essential to support the soft tissue above it. Bone loss → soft tissue collapse → recession → aesthetic failure. The labial plate is particularly vulnerable in thin phenotype patients where it may be almost entirely bundle bone — lost the moment the periodontal ligament is removed.

Guided Bone Regeneration

When bone is insufficient: GBR (guided bone regeneration) using bone graft particles and resorbable membrane. Covered in detail in subsequent lectures. Key principle: membrane must be away from flap margin, and passive tension-free closure is mandatory.

Annual Review Protocol
Maintaining peri-implant health long term

Long-term implant success depends on systematic annual review. The following are assessed at every review appointment:

✓
Probing depths — Gentle probing around all implants. Document and compare year-on-year. Any increase signals disease progression.
✓
Bleeding on probing — Gentle probing only. Spontaneous bleeding = disease. Probing-induced = may reflect disturbed hemidesmosomes (not always pathological).
✓
Radiographic bone levels — Periapical radiograph. Any marginal bone loss from previous review = active peri-implantitis until proven otherwise.
✓
Keratinized mucosa width — Ensure ≥2mm maintained. Address deficit before it progresses to peri-implantitis.
✓
Crown removal — Only if signs of disease present. Unnecessary crown removal disturbs hemidesmosomal attachment and restarts healing. Do not remove routinely.
Socket Grafting vs Replacement
Decision framework for managing the extraction socket
Socket Grafting (GBR at extraction)
  • Bone graft + membrane placed at time of extraction
  • Preserves ridge volume for future implant
  • Essential for high-aesthetic thin phenotype cases
  • Required when insufficient residual bone for primary stability
  • Allows optimal delayed implant placement (4–6 months)
  • Higher complexity, cost, and patient burden
Early Replacement (8 weeks)
  • No grafting at extraction — wait 8 weeks
  • Thin mucosa matures to thicker by 8 weeks
  • Requires ≥3mm apical bone for primary stability
  • If insufficient bone: not possible → socket graft
  • Simpler, fewer visits, less morbidity
  • Better for non-aesthetic cases with adequate bone
Immediate Placement

Reserved for thick phenotype, adequate labial plate, low-risk sockets. Requires: intact labial plate, no acute infection, sufficient bone for primary stability (>25Ncm). Simultaneous connective tissue graft often used to supplement labial soft tissue volume.

Lecture 1 Summary
Key Takeaways
  • Any implant will integrate — the challenge is maintaining bone and soft tissue long term
  • Peri-implant soft tissue has four zones (E, B, C, dead) — bacteria reaching the C zone initiates bone loss
  • The 2+3 rule: 2mm thickness, 3mm height above implant shoulder — violations trigger biological width-driven bone resorption
  • Biological width = supracrestal attachment: 3–4mm in implants (vs 2–3mm in teeth) — non-negotiable
  • Phenotype determines risk: thin phenotype loses 50%+ labial bone; thick loses 5–20%
  • Always assess 4 factors before extraction: KM width, phenotype, tissue thickness, supracrestal attachment
  • Minimum 2mm keratinized mucosa is evidence-based for peri-implant disease resistance
