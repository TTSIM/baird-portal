Why Tension-Free Closure Matters
The cascade from wound tension to implant failure

Even without bone grafting, closing a flap after implant placement creates tension — and tension causes dehiscence.

Why Tension Occurs Even Without GBR

1. The osteotomy drilled smaller than the implant → implant expands the ridge on placement
2. Normal wound contraction during healing pulls margins apart
Result: tension is almost always present — release is almost always needed

The Tension Cascade

Tension → suture weakens → wound pulls open → dehiscence → bacteria reach graft/implant → marginal bone loss → exposed threads → peri-implantitis risk

Scientific Threshold

Research shows flap tension >25 Newton centimetres leads to wound dehiscence. While we cannot measure this clinically, the tweezers test and visual blanching assessment are reliable proxies.

The Tweezers Test
Clinical assessment of flap tension before suturing

Before placing any suture, you must assess whether the flap can be closed passively. The tweezers test is your primary clinical tool.

✓ Passive Closure
  • Grasp flap edge with tissue forceps
  • Bring to desired position
  • Release tweezers
  • Flap stays 3–5 seconds then slowly retracts
  • → Safe to suture. Tension-free closure.
✗ Tension Present
  • Grasp flap, bring to position
  • Release tweezers
  • Flap springs back immediately
  • → DO NOT suture yet
  • → Perform periosteal release first
Post-Suture Check — White Blanching

After suturing, inspect the flap. If you see white blanching at the wound margins — the tissue is under ischaemic pressure from tension. Remove sutures, perform periosteal release, re-suture. Ignoring blanching guarantees dehiscence at 7–10 days.

Common Mistake

Many clinicians suture under tension thinking "it will hold" — the suture holds for days, then loses strength as the material degrades. Combined with wound contraction, the result is predictable dehiscence. Prevention is simple: test before you suture.

Periosteal Release — Anatomy
Understanding the layers before cutting

The flap has two key layers. Understanding their anatomy is the foundation of safe periosteal release:

Mucosa (Outer Layer)

Stretchable, elastic. Can expand significantly with stretching. Located superficially. Contains nerves and vessels apical to the mucogingival line within the muscle layer.

Periosteum (Inner Layer)

The "glue" — firmly attaches mucosa to bone. Inelastic and rigid. Extends from the crestal bone to the mucogingival line. Cutting the periosteum releases the glue → the elastic mucosa can then stretch freely.

Mucogingival Line (MGJ) — Key Landmark

Where periosteum ends. Below the MGJ: muscle layer with nerves and blood vessels. Periosteal release must be performed at or just coronal to the MGJ — never apical to it, where muscles, nerves, and vessels lie.

Periosteal Release — Technique
Step-by-step: the correct method to avoid perforation, bleeding, and nerve injury

Less than 50% of implantologists perform periosteal release correctly. The key is understanding that the right hand makes 1mm — the left hand gives the stretch.

1
Position — Stand behind the patient's head (supine). For upper arch: patient head tilted back. Never work from in front of the patient for this manoeuvre.
2
Left hand — tension — Use tissue forceps to evert the flap edge, pulling it posteriorly to create tension. Cutting in a stretched, taut structure is far more controlled than loose tissue.
3
Blade selection — Use a fresh blade (not the same blade used for the initial incision — it is blunt after contacting bone). Blade 15c is preferred for its fine tip.
4
Blade position — Blade tip points toward bone, not toward the flap surface. Lay the blade nearly parallel to the flap, resting the tip on it. Then rotate upright by just 5 degrees. Not 45° — only 5°.
5
The cut — Dig blade tip 1mm only into the periosteum at the MGJ. One millimetre. Then run that 1mm notch edge-to-edge across the full width of the flap.
6
Result — The 1mm notch, combined with the left-hand stretch, gives 5–10mm of release. Dry procedure. No bleeding. No perforation. Verify passive closure with tweezers test.
Periosteal Release — Common Errors
Why most clinicians get it wrong — and how to avoid their mistakes
Error 1 — Too Deep

Going >1mm deep → perforates mucosa. Result: hole in flap, wound dehiscence. The 1mm rule is absolute. Left-hand stretch does the rest of the work.

Error 2 — Too Apical

Cutting apical to MGJ = cutting in muscle layer. Consequence: hit infraorbital/mental nerve branch → numbness. Hit vessels → haematoma → black eye. Never go apical to MGJ.

Error 3 — Incomplete Line

Release line not run edge-to-edge across full flap width. Attached edges remain, preventing stretch. Result: only 1–2mm of gain instead of 5–10mm. Always run full width.

Signs of Correct Technique

Dry procedure — no bleeding (you are away from vessels). No perforation — blade angle is nearly parallel. Visible stretch — tissue springs to 5–10mm on left-hand pull. If you see bleeding: you are in the muscle layer — reposition coronally.

Extended Release — Vertical Bone Augmentation

For vertical GBR requiring even more release: after the 1mm periosteal notch, use a blunt instrument (Minimi) to mechanically brush the mucosa vertically while pulling. This mechanical stretch is additive to the passive stretch from the incision.

Frenectomy — Indications
When prominent frena compromise implant surgery outcomes

A prominent frenum is one that attaches at a low level — crossing or close to the mucogingival line. This creates a dynamic pull on flap margins every time the patient speaks or smiles.

Problems Caused by Prominent Frenum

• Continuous pull on sutures during healing → dehiscence
• Patient cannot clean the area effectively → increased plaque
• Impairs tension-free closure
• Compromises long-term tissue stability

When to Perform Frenectomy

Frenectomy indicated when frenum crosses or attaches at the mucogingival line. Timing: ideally 4–6 weeks before implant surgery. Allows full healing before raising the surgical flap. If done at same time as surgery: risk of perforation through weakened tissue.

Does Frenectomy Affect the Smile Line?

No. The frenum attaches mucosa to lip musculature — not to the actual smile arc. Removing it does not alter facial aesthetics. Patients often experience improved ability to clean the area.

Frenectomy — Surgical Technique
Step-by-step surgical frenectomy procedure
1
Mark the frenum — Identify the full extent of the frenum attachment from the lip to the gingiva/mucosa.
2
Clamp — Place mosquito clamp horizontally across the frenum at its base (or two clamps: one superior, one inferior). This controls the tissue and marks the excision line.
3
Incise — Cut above the upper clamp and below the lower clamp. This removes the clamped frenum tissue. The cuts are vertical (top to bottom direction).
4
Undermine — Use scissors to undermine the mucosa from the underlying muscle on both sides. Open and spread. The goal: mobilise mucosa away from muscle so you suture mucosa to mucosa, never muscle to muscle.
5
Suture — Note: incisions were VERTICAL but sutures are HORIZONTAL. 3–5 interrupted sutures placed across the wound (right to left orientation). Simple and effective.
Laser Frenectomy

Identical procedure but no sutures required. The laser coagulates the wound edges simultaneously. Preferred where available — less patient morbidity. Technique principle is identical.

Free Gingival Graft (FGG)
Increasing keratinized mucosa WIDTH around implants

FGG is indicated when keratinized mucosa width is insufficient (<2mm). The graft carries its own epithelium, which becomes keratinized tissue at the recipient site.

Recipient Site Preparation

Split-thickness incision — peel mucosa but leave periosteum on bone. Periosteum provides the blood supply for graft take. If you remove periosteum: graft fails. Naked bone = no blood supply = graft necrosis.

Donor Site

Best: palate (richest keratinized tissue source). Alternative: edentulous ridge (adjacent site — heals faster, less patient morbidity). Leave periosteum at donor site. Cover with collagen sponge + flowable composite splint for 72hr.

Graft Preparation

Remove any yellow fatty tissue from the deep surface. Fat impedes vascularity and integration. Keep epithelium intact. Graft should be uniform thickness — 1–2mm ideal.

FGG — Healing & Outcomes
What to expect and how to manage the patient post-procedure
Healing Timeline

• Day 0–3: Graft survives on plasma diffusion from periosteum
• Week 1–2: Neovascularisation begins — graft "takes"
• Week 2: Suture removal
• Week 4: Functional keratinized tissue forming
• 3 months: Assess final result

Expected Shrinkage

Expect approximately 50% shrinkage during healing. Plan accordingly — harvest graft larger than desired final result. Starting with 3.5mm KM → end with ~2mm minimum. Consistent and predictable.

Donor Site Management

Palate heals by secondary intention. Options:
• Collagen haemostatic sponge
• Flowable composite over etched palatal cusps → acts as a pressure dressing for 72hr–2 weeks
• Traditional palatal stent (impression-based acrylic) — older technique, rarely needed now

Key Evidence

FGG easier to perform when ≥1.5mm KM remains. Waiting until zero makes the procedure technically harder and outcomes less predictable. Act early — don't wait for complete deficiency.

Timing Relative to Implant Surgery

Do NOT perform FGG at the same time as bone grafting. Tissue needs a stable, healed base. Perform FGG either before (on native healed bone) or after (once GBR has healed). Never simultaneously.

Connective Tissue Graft (CTG)
Increasing soft tissue THICKNESS and volume

CTG is used when the issue is volume/thickness deficiency rather than lack of keratinized mucosa. It increases the amount of soft tissue, not its keratinization status.

CTG vs FGG — The Key Difference

CTG: epithelium removed → placed under flap (buried) → increases thickness. FGG: epithelium kept → placed on top of periosteum → increases keratinized width.

Why Remove the Epithelium?

If you bury a graft with epithelium under a flap, the epithelium creates a pocket — epithelium to epithelium won't heal (epithelium is for protection, not union). The connective tissue layer is what fuses and integrates.

Common Indications

• Thin labial mucosal drape over implant (especially immediate placement)
• Compromised emergence profile requiring tissue augmentation
• Post-recession defects at existing implants
• Mucoderm (acellular dermal matrix) is an animal-derived alternative — second choice when palatal harvest not possible

Mucoderm — Allograft Option
When palatal harvest is not ideal

Mucoderm is an acellular collagen matrix derived from porcine (pig) dermis. It serves as an alternative to autogenous connective tissue graft when donor site morbidity needs to be minimised.

Mucoderm — Advantages
  • No second surgical site (no palatal harvest)
  • Unlimited supply — any volume needed
  • Reduced patient morbidity
  • Consistent thickness
  • Can be used as membrane AND soft tissue graft
  • Good integration with host tissue
Mucoderm — Limitations
  • Not as effective as autogenous CTG
  • Second best option — autogenous graft preferred
  • Higher cost
  • Religious/cultural considerations (porcine origin)
  • No live cells — purely structural scaffold
  • Less predictable revascularisation
Clinical Use — Combined Role

Mucoderm can be placed as a soft tissue augmentation material AND used simultaneously as a resorbable barrier membrane for GBR. This dual role makes it attractive for combined bone + soft tissue defect cases. The course will include hands-on practice with Mucoderm.

Soft Tissue Tunnel Technique
Minimally invasive approach for connective tissue graft delivery

The tunnel technique allows CTG placement without raising a full flap — ideal when adjacent papillae must not be disturbed and the implant is already placed.

1
Harvest graft from palate or use de-epithelialized alveolar ridge tissue (remove shiny epithelium layer — 0.3–0.4mm). Connective tissue surface is matte/dull.
2
Create tunnel — Tunnel instrument passed subperiosteally at the recipient site without raising a flap. Creates a pocket for graft insertion.
3
Insert graft — CTG tucked into the tunnel. Positioned labially/buccally for volume gain. No sutures through the graft required — stabilised by the tunnel itself.
4
Close — Sling sutures or interrupted sutures close the tunnel entrance. Healing abutment placed or temporary crown seated to support tissue.
How to Identify Correct Graft Depth

Epithelium is shiny. Connective tissue is matte and dull. Once the shiny surface disappears during de-epithelialisation, you are at the correct depth. Epithelium thickness: 0.3–0.4mm.

Ideal Use Case

Adjacent implant with recession/inadequate tissue volume. Cannot raise flap without disturbing precious papilla supported by minimal blood supply. Tunnel preserves all existing soft tissue architecture while adding volume beneath.

Suturing — Principles
Choosing the right suture technique for implant surgery

Suturing is the final step in surgical tissue management. The wrong technique can undo everything achieved during surgery.

✗ Interrupted Sutures Alone
  • Flatten interdental papillae
  • Pull papilla tips coronally and horizontally
  • Result: flattened, blunted papillae
  • Loss of papilla height
  • Acceptable only where papilla aesthetic is not critical
✓ Vertical Mattress Sutures
  • Evert wound margins
  • Hold papilla in its natural height
  • Better primary closure tension distribution
  • Preferred for anterior aesthetic zones
  • Practice session tomorrow (pig heads)
Horizontal Mattress Sutures

Used where broader tissue mobilisation and eversion is required. Good for flap edges across wider defects. Less commonly used than vertical mattress in implant papilla management.

Suture Material

Resorbable (Vicryl, Monocryl) for most situations. Non-resorbable (Gore-Tex, Prolene) for membrane exposure cases where longer support needed. Remove non-resorbable at 2 weeks.

Wound Dehiscence
Prevention, recognition, and management

Wound dehiscence is among the most serious early post-operative complications. It exposes grafts or implants to the oral environment and bacterial colonisation.

Causes

• Closure under tension (primary cause)
• Prominent frenum not addressed
• Infection
• Patient trauma / smoking
• Membrane exposure too early
• Excessive patient activity

Recognition

• Wound open at 7–14 days (suture removal visit)
• White scar tissue visible centrally (necrosis + re-epithelialisation)
• Area may re-epithelialise but with low-quality tissue
• Marginal bone loss underneath is likely

Consequences

• Marginal bone loss (guaranteed if open for >48hr)
• Exposed threads → aesthetic failure
• Implant failure if infection spreads
• Even if re-epithelialised: bacteria already introduced

Prevention Protocol

Periosteal release → tweezers test → passive closure confirmed → suture with appropriate technique → check for blanching → frenectomy 4–6 weeks prior if prominent frenum identified. Prevention is surgical discipline, not luck.

Fixed Temporisation
The role of provisional restorations in soft tissue management

Fixed temporisation is superior to removable dentures for maintaining soft tissue stability between surgery and final restoration — particularly for papilla preservation.

Why Fixed Beats Removable

A denture resting on the soft tissue compresses and starves the papilla of blood supply. Without lateral support, the papilla loses its anatomical reference and shrinks. Fixed temporaries maintain papilla support even during healing.

Staged Approach Example

Patient needs implant adjacent to existing implant crown:
1. Make a 2-unit temporary bridge on existing implant before removing failing tooth
2. Extract tooth and fit temporary bridge immediately
3. Papilla maintained throughout healing phase
4. Graft at surgical date with protected soft tissue

Prosthetic Soft Tissue Sculpting

The temporary crown's subgingival contour is used to progressively mould the peri-implant soft tissue into the desired emergence profile. Begin with a concave profile, then gradually increase convexity over weeks to "inflate" the tissue to the target shape.

Cantilever Caution

As a temporary, a cantilever pontic (as large as the implant crown) is acceptable short-term to maintain papilla. Not suitable as a definitive solution. Always inform the patient of the temporary nature.

Long-Term Follow-Up Evidence

Cases reviewed at 14 years post-FGG show stable keratinized mucosa, with healthy tissue on the grafted side versus persistent inflammation and recession on non-grafted sites. The grafted tissue behaves as native keratinized mucosa in all measurable parameters — plaque index, recession rates, bone levels, and patient discomfort during oral hygiene.

Consensus Statement Summary

Italian Academy of Periodontology, Academy of Osseointegration: inadequate keratinized mucosa is associated with increased plaque, bleeding, recession, marginal bone loss, patient discomfort, and harder oral hygiene maintenance. This is evidence, not opinion.

Timing of Soft Tissue Procedures
When to do what — a practical decision framework
1
Frenectomy → 4–6 weeks BEFORE implant surgery. Allows full healing. If omitted: perform at surgery date (but risk of perforation in weakened tissue remains).
2
FGG for keratinized mucosa → Either before surgery (on native tissue, easiest) OR after implant healing (but not simultaneous with GBR). Never at the same surgical visit as bone augmentation.
3
CTG for thickness → Often performed with immediate implant placement. Alternatively at second-stage surgery. Compatible with implant surgery — often combined.
4
Soft tissue sculpting (temporary crown) → During healing phase, after implant osseointegration is confirmed (typically 8–12 weeks post-surgery). Before final impression.
5
Final prosthetic restoration → Only when soft tissue is stable, healthy, and the desired profile has been achieved. Typically 3–6 months post-surgery depending on complexity.
Periosteal Release — Interactive Review
Test your understanding of the technique steps

Patient Position

Patient: supine, head tilted back slightly for upper arch. Surgeon: standing BEHIND the patient's head (12 o'clock position). Never work from in front of the patient — you cannot control the blade angle correctly and your visibility is compromised. Nurse retracts the lip to improve access.

Blade Selection

Use a fresh blade — not the blade used for the initial incision. After contacting bone, a blade is blunted. A dull blade requires excess pressure → perforation risk increases significantly. Blade 15c preferred for its fine, pointed tip which facilitates the narrow periosteal cut.

Blade Angle — The 5-Degree Rule

Begin with the blade nearly parallel to the flap surface, tip resting on it, pointing toward the bone. Then rotate the blade upright by just 5 degrees — not 10°, not 45°. Five degrees. This angle allows the tip to engage the periosteum without the blade body perforating the mucosa above.

Depth of Cut — 1mm Only

The blade tip disappears into the periosteum by 1mm only. Not 2mm, not 3mm — just 1mm of the blade tip. The left hand provides the remaining 5–10mm of effective release through stretching. Cutting deeper increases perforation risk exponentially with no additional gain.

Signs of Correct Technique

Dry procedure — no haemorrhage means you are in periosteum, away from vessels. No perforation — blade angle respected. Visible stretch — tissue immediately springs 5–10mm when left-hand tension is applied. Tweezers test negative — flap holds position for 3–5 seconds when released.

Signs of Incorrect Technique

Bleeding — you are in the muscle layer (too apical/too deep). Reposition coronally immediately. Perforation — blade angle too steep, too deep, or tissue not stretched adequately with left hand. Minimal stretch — cut not run full edge-to-edge, or cut in mucosa not periosteum.

Bone Grafting & GBR — Overview
The role of guided bone regeneration in the soft tissue framework

Guided bone regeneration (GBR) is the cornerstone procedure for restoring bone volume before or concurrent with implant placement. Adequate bone underpins adequate soft tissue.

Principle of GBR

Bone graft particles + barrier membrane create a protected space preventing soft tissue ingrowth while allowing osteoblasts to regenerate bone. The membrane maintains the "tent" space — key to vertical and horizontal bone gain.

Flap-Closure Demand of GBR

GBR significantly increases the volume under the flap (the "big belly" effect). This makes tension-free closure far more challenging. Periosteal release is mandatory for every GBR case — not optional.

Membrane Position Rule

The membrane edges must be away from flap margins and away from adjacent tooth roots. Flap margins should rest on vital bone, not on synthetic graft material. This reduces dehiscence risk and improves membrane protection.

Remote Palatal Incision Advantage

A palatal-offset crestal incision provides extra tissue to assist GBR closure — the additional coronal tissue reduces tension at the wound margin. Five advantages (detail in Lecture 3).

Socket Grafting at Extraction
Preserving ridge volume for future implant placement

Socket grafting (ridge preservation) at the time of extraction reduces bone loss and maintains an adequate site for later implant placement.

1
Atraumatic extraction — Preserve labial plate. Periotomes, extraction forceps with minimal bone removal. No buccal plate elevation.
2
Degranulation — Remove all granulation tissue and PDL remnants. Special degranulation burs (800rpm) are efficient and bone-sparing. Available in multiple sizes (large to small).
3
Graft packing — Bone graft material packed into socket. Various options: autogenous, xenograft (bovine), alloplast. Often a combination.
4
Membrane coverage — Resorbable collagen membrane placed over graft. Sutured or tucked under flap margins.
Degranulation Burs — Investment Worth Making

Designed to run at 800rpm — fast enough to remove granulation tissue but not fast enough to damage bone. Buy once, use throughout career. Excellent for immediate and early placement, socket grafting, and treating peri-implantitis.

When to Socket Graft vs Not

Socket graft: thin phenotype, high aesthetic zone, anticipated significant bone loss, insufficient residual bone for early placement stability. Skip: thick phenotype, non-aesthetic zone, adequate residual bone, patient prefers fewer procedures.

Soft Tissue Management — Denture Wearers
Special considerations for edentulous or near-edentulous patients

Long-term denture wearers present unique soft tissue challenges compared to partially dentate implant patients.

Atrophic Mucosa

Years of denture pressure causes tissue atrophy. Very thin, fragile mucosa. Biological width demands are harder to satisfy. Susceptible to trauma and poor healing.

Keratinized Mucosa

Often dramatically reduced in edentulous ridges. Pre-implant FGG may be indicated to create an adequate keratinized band before implant placement, especially for mandibular overdenture implants.

Overdenture Advantage

Overdenture implants do not require ideal soft tissue architecture in the same way as fixed restorations. However, adequate keratinized mucosa still protects against peri-implant disease around bar and ball attachments.

Denture Stabilisation During Healing

After implant placement for overdentures: existing denture must be relieved over implant sites. Tissue conditioner applied. Prevents pressure on healing implants. Premature loading (from unrelieved denture) is a common cause of early implant failure in denture patients.

Prosthetic Soft Tissue Sculpting
Using the temporary crown to create ideal tissue architecture

After implant healing, the temporary crown is your most powerful tool for creating and refining the peri-implant soft tissue profile.

1
Initial temporary — Concave subgingival profile. Allows tissue to "relax" into a natural position after surgical manipulation.
2
Progressive loading — At 4–6 week intervals, add composite to the subgingival aspect to gradually increase convexity and "push" tissue into desired emergence profile.
3
Papilla management — Adjust the interdental contact point height to encourage papilla growth. Narrow the embrasure space progressively as tissue fills in (typically 12–24 weeks).
4
Assessment criteria — Before taking final impression: tissue must be stable for minimum 4 weeks, no recession, adequate keratinized mucosa, healthy probing depths, optimal colour and texture match.
Key Principle

The final crown captures the soft tissue profile created by the temporary. A good temporary means a predictable final outcome. A poor temporary means the final crown will never look natural, regardless of the lab's skill.

Managing Existing Implant Recession
Addressing soft tissue deficiencies around previously placed implants

When patients present with existing implants and soft tissue deficiencies (recession, inadequate KM, aesthetic concerns), the management approach depends on diagnosis:

Diagnosis — What's Missing?

• Is it KM width deficiency? → FGG
• Is it tissue volume/thickness? → CTG
• Is it incorrect abutment design? → Replace abutment/crown with concave profile
• Is it active peri-implantitis? → Treat infection first, then augment

Never Treat Without Diagnosis

Performing soft tissue augmentation on an infected or inflamed site = failure. All active disease must be controlled before any augmentation procedure.

Recession After Cleaning — Normal?

When treating early peri-implantitis by removing the crown and cleaning: some recession post-treatment is normal healing. The cleaning disturbs hemidesmosomal attachments → tissue reestablishes at a slightly more apical level. As long as healthy: acceptable outcome.

Patient Communication

Pre-warn patients: cleaning peri-implant disease may result in some cosmetic recession. This is a treatment success (healthy) not a failure. Inform before treatment to prevent misunderstanding.

Sinus Lift & Ridge Expansion — Overview
Advanced techniques that complement soft tissue management

When bone volume is insufficient for ideal implant placement, advanced bone management techniques become necessary. These are typically considered advanced skills beyond the core course but are introduced here for context.

Internal Sinus Lift

Used when bone height below the sinus is 5–6mm but a longer implant is desired. The sinus floor is "nudged" 2mm upward using special Versah burs — allowing placement of a 7mm implant where only 5mm native bone exists. Osteotome-free, patient-friendly technique.

Versah Burs (Densah)

Purpose-designed for ridge expansion and sinus floor elevation. Rotate in an osseodensification pattern — compacting rather than cutting bone. Eliminates the mallet-and-chisel technique previously associated with tinnitus risk (ossicle disturbance).

Ridge Expansion

For ridges ≤4mm width where a standard implant diameter is needed. Versah burs or traditional osteotomes expand the ridge laterally, allowing implant placement without bone graft in select cases.

Clinical Decision Summary
Integrating soft tissue management into every implant case
1
Pre-operative — Assess KM width, phenotype, tissue thickness. Plan any required FGG/frenectomy 4–6 weeks before surgery.
2
Flap design — Use the four-yes system (Lecture 3). Choose appropriate flap extent. Plan vertical incision positions if needed.
3
Surgery — Implant placement at correct depth referenced to future gingival margin. GBR if required. CTG if immediate placement in thin phenotype.
4
Periosteal release — Perform in ALL cases. Tweezers test to confirm passive closure. Check for blanching after suturing.
5
Suture — Vertical mattress for papilla preservation. Appropriate material. Tension-free. Review at 2 weeks for suture removal and wound inspection.
6
Healing phase — Fixed temporisation if possible. Prosthetic sculpting at 8–12 weeks. Final restoration only when tissue is stable.
