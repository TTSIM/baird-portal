Slide 2 · The PECOFA framework

Stabilise in one direction — plan in the other

Slide 3 · The foundation

Periodontal stabilisation comes first

You cannot place implants in an actively inflamed mouth — and you should not try. The analogy patients understand instantly is building a house on poor foundations. Picking curtains in the front room makes no sense if the walls are crumbling.

Bleeding is the marker. Absence of bleeding is one of the best indicators of gingival health we have. Patients with active perio are not implant patients — they are perio patients first.

The four reasons perio comes first

1. Bacterial reservoirs. Periodontal pockets harbour exactly the species that drive peri-implantitis. Until they are reduced, every implant placement contaminates the new site.
2. Behaviour test. If a patient cannot brush their teeth properly under your supervision over six weeks, they will not maintain implants over twenty years.
3. Honest restorability. Inflammation distorts pocket depths and hides true clinical attachment loss. Once tissues calm down, you see what you actually have to work with.
4. Prosthetic certainty. Loose grade-3 mobile teeth that resolve to grade 1 after stabilisation may now be saveable — and may change your whole plan.

Defining "success" strictly (pocket < 5 mm, no bleeding on probing, < 0.2 mm bone loss/year), the gap between perio and non-perio patients is dramatic. Patients with a history of periodontitis must understand they are signing up for life-long maintenance — not a one-off procedure.
Slide 5 · Measuring what matters

Plaque & bleeding indices — the basics

Modified plaque score

Six index teeth, four sites each (mesial / distal / buccal / palatal) → 24 sites scored 0 (no plaque) or 1 (plaque). Add and express as a percentage. Repeat blind at each visit — do not look at the previous score first. Aim for <20%.

Bleeding score

Same six teeth, same four sites, same binary scoring of bleeding on gentle probing. Less than 10% bleeding on probing is the threshold for periodontal stability.

Practical tip: most dental software has no built-in module for these. A simple Word or PDF template the nurse can fill in works fine. The point is the numerical baseline — patients respond powerfully to seeing a score drop.
Slide 6 · Coaching, not lecturing

Oral hygiene instruction is coaching

"You need to brush your teeth a bit better" is not OHI. Real OHI is patient-specific coaching — a skill rarely taught at dental school but central to perio outcomes.

The principles

  • Bring your tools. Ask the patient to bring everything they use to brush with — toothbrush, paste, floss, interdental brushes, mouthwash.
  • Watch them do it. "Show me how you brush right now." Telling vs showing reveals enormous gaps.
  • Sit them up. Eye-to-eye, not lying back in the chair. This is an adult-to-adult conversation.
  • Promote in stages. Master modified Bass first → then floss/interdental → then vertical/sub-gingival cleaning. Don't dump everything on visit one.
  • Bespoke solutions. The American-shift worker who falls asleep at her laptop needs to brush before the meeting, not at 2 AM. Adapt to the patient's life.
  • No fixed time limit. Brush until clean — for some perio patients that is 15 minutes. Book it into the diary.
Slide 7 · Terminology matters

Drop "scale and polish" — adopt PMPR

Why "scale and polish" is a problem

  • It sounds like a beauty treatment — like waxing the car.
  • Polishing has no real periodontal benefit.
  • It conditions patients to expect a six-month "spa" appointment.
  • Six-month recall has no clinical evidence base — it comes from a toothpaste advert.

PMPR — the modern term

Professional Mechanical Plaque Removal. It positions the appointment as disease control rather than cosmetics. Recall intervals are based on disease activity, not the calendar — anywhere from 3 to 12 months depending on risk.

Use the right words with patients

Slide 9 · Staging by bone loss

Reading bone loss off a radiograph

The current classification stages perio by extent of bone loss and grades by rate of progression (using the bone loss / age ratio).

StageApproximate bone loss
Stage I< 15% (coronal third only)
Stage II15–33% (coronal third)
Stage IIIMid-third — extending to apical third
Stage IVApical third — tooth loss likely or already occurring

Grading: bone loss ÷ age

  • Grade A — slow progression (BL/age < 0.5)
  • Grade B — moderate (0.5–1.0)
  • Grade C — rapid (>1.0) — for example, 50% bone loss in a 40-year-old.
Slide 10 · Three states

  • Pockets ≤ 4 mm
  • BoP > 10%

Cautious — case by case

❌ Unstable

  • Pockets ≥ 5 mm
  • Bleeding on probing
  • Active disease
Do NOT place implants
Slide 11 · Unresponsive sites

What to do with the site that won't heal

You have done everything right — OHI is good, you have done full subgingival PMPR — and at re-evaluation there is still a 5 mm bleeding pocket. There are four options:

  1. Re-instrument that specific site. Often local re-PMPR under LA is enough.
  2. Periodontal surgery — for residual pockets > 6 mm. Usually means specialist referral.
  3. Adjunctive antibiotics — local or systemic. Limited role and not first-line.
  4. Extraction — when the tooth is hopeless and other teeth or implants are at risk.
Refer to a perio specialist after you have done your job — not before. Specialist surgery on a patient who cannot brush properly is a waste of everyone's time and money.
Slide 12 · Endodontic diagnosis

Pulpal & apical diagnoses (AAE)

Pulp diagnoses

  • Normal pulp — symptomless, responds normally to testing.
  • Reversible pulpitis — short, sharp pain to stimulus, no lingering.
  • Symptomatic irreversible pulpitis — lingering, spontaneous pain.
  • Asymptomatic irreversible pulpitis — no symptoms but caries to pulp.
  • Pulp necrosis — non-vital, no response to testing.
  • Previously treated — root-filled tooth.
  • Previously initiated therapy — access opened, treatment incomplete.

Apical diagnoses

  • Normal apical tissues
  • Symptomatic apical periodontitis — TTP positive, often widened PDL.
  • Asymptomatic apical periodontitis — radiolucency without symptoms.
  • Acute apical abscess — pus, swelling, systemic signs possible.
  • Chronic apical abscess — sinus tract, low-grade.
  • Condensing osteitis — radio-opaque rather than lucent.
Slide 13 · Endo prognosis

The Eastman 11 prognostic factors — what really predicts success

The single biggest predictor of endodontic success — surprisingly to many — is the absence of a periapical lesion. A vital, symptomatic, irreversible-pulpitis tooth treated before a lesion has formed has an excellent prognosis.

Top factors

Slide 14 · Irrigants & rubber dam

Two non-negotiables for endo

🧪 Irrigation strategy

  • Sodium hypochlorite — primary disinfectant.
  • EDTA — removes the smear layer, allows hypochlorite to penetrate.
  • Avoid chlorhexidine — reacts with hypochlorite to form parachloroaniline (carcinogenic precipitate).
  • Saline and lidocaine are not effective irrigants — do not use them as a shortcut.

🛡 Rubber dam

If you cannot get a rubber dam on, you cannot do the endo. Without rubber dam you cannot use sodium hypochlorite, which means you cannot disinfect properly. Refer the case rather than compromise.

No dam = no endo. If you genuinely cannot isolate, send it to someone who can.
Slide 15 · Re-treatment

Should you re-do this root filling?

The single most useful question is: "Can I do this any better than the person who did it before me?"

Re-do indicated: Visible technical problems — short fill, voids, single cone, missed canals, poor coronal seal, inadequate length. There is room for improvement.
Re-do unlikely to help: Good length, good taper, good condensation, good coronal seal — but still an apical lesion. You will not improve on it. Consider apical surgery, monitoring, or extraction.
Reality check: Re-endodontic treatment generally has a lower success rate than primary endo. Refer to a specialist when in doubt.
Slide 16 · Endo–implant interactions

Retrograde peri-implantitis (RPI) classification

Endodontic lesions on adjacent natural teeth can affect implants placed nearby — and increasingly we are seeing apical lesions on implants themselves. A proposed classification:

ClassMechanismManagement
Class IImplant placement devitalised an adjacent vital toothRCT of the affected tooth ± consider implant removal if damage is severe
Class IIImplant apex infected by a persistent periapical lesion on adjacent toothAntibiotics, RCT or re-RCT of the tooth, surgical debridement of the implant ± grafting
Class IIIImplant apex placed outside the bone envelope (labial / lingual perforation)Surgical access, graft, ± remove and replace the implant
Lesson: don't place implants adjacent to active periapical pathology. Stabilise the endo first — antibiotic cover at placement is a useful adjunct in borderline cases.
Slide 17 · Coronal seal

The coronal seal is worth more than the endo itself

The data

A good coronal seal increases endodontic success approximately 11-fold. Studies disagree about whether the seal or the endo matters more — but the consensus is that good coronal restoration with mediocre endo generally outperforms good endo with mediocre coronal restoration.

Practical points

  • Get composite as far down into the pulp chamber as possible.
  • Cuspal coverage on posterior teeth dramatically improves longevity.
  • Aquilino & Caplan: crowned RCT teeth had a 6× higher likelihood of survival.
  • Second molars and carious teeth fail at higher rates.
Slide 18 · RCT teeth and crowns

Do all root-filled teeth need a crown?

No — but most posterior ones do, and almost any tooth that has lost a marginal ridge needs cuspal coverage.

Anteriors

  • Light loads, mostly tensile rather than compressive.
  • Often a well-sealed direct restoration is enough.
  • Crown only if structurally compromised or aesthetic need.

Posteriors

  • Heavy compressive loads.
  • Loss of one or more marginal ridges → cuspal coverage essential.
  • Onlay vs full crown: increasingly minimal-prep onlays driven by adhesive cementation.
Modern adhesion has shifted the balance: we used to need full coverage for retention/resistance form. Now we glue. A 0.5 mm e-max or gold onlay often does the job with minimal tooth removal.
Slide 19 · Restorability

Restorability indices & the ferrule

Restorability is partly intuition — you can usually eyeball a tooth and know — but indices give you a defensible framework. Two are commonly cited:

  • Tooth Restorability Index (TRI) — sextant scoring of remaining coronal dentine height/thickness/distribution.
  • Dental Practicality Index (DPI) — combines structural, periodontal, endodontic and contextual factors.

The ferrule rule

SiteMinimum height
Anterior teeth2–3 mm
Posterior teeth≥ 4 mm

You also need width, not just height. No ferrule = high failure risk. Crown lengthening or extraction may be the honest answer.

Slide 20 · Posts

Posts — the last chance saloon

If a tooth has a post in it, that means there was not enough coronal dentine to hold a core. If there had been enough dentine, you would not have needed a post in the first place. So a post by definition marks the last attempt to save a tooth.

Survival data: Post-restored teeth have around 72% success at 5 years. They tend to last when they last — but when they fail, they fail catastrophically (root fracture or extraction).
Removal myth: The Abbott paper (1,600 posts removed, 1 fracture = 0.06%) shows that ultrasonic post removal is far safer than its reputation. Most luted posts come out cleanly when the cement lute is shattered.
Threshold for extraction: A post-crowned tooth plus an apical lesion is a very low threshold for extraction. Include it in the implant plan from the start.
Slide 21 · Occlusion

Conform to ICP — or reorganise to RCP?

This is a whole course in itself. Two questions structure the decision:

1. Is there enough prosthetic space?

If you do not have room to build the planned restoration in the patient's existing intercuspal position (ICP), you must reorganise. Tooth wear cases are the classic example.

2. Are you maintaining the existing guidance?

Anterior guidance? Canine guidance? Group function? Whatever scheme is in place, your new restorations must respect it — or you have to plan a whole-mouth reorganisation around centric relation (RCP).

Practical sequence: Provisionalise first. Confirm the new occlusion is comfortable and stable in temporaries before committing to definitive implant placement. The temporaries are your insurance policy.
Slide 22 · Tooth wear

Tooth wear cases — the classic reorganisation

Tooth wear is the most common reason to reorganise the bite. The clinical workflow:

  1. Identify the cause — erosion, attrition, abrasion, parafunction. Treat the cause or you will rebuild and watch it wear again.
  2. Wax up the final result at the new vertical dimension.
  3. Trial it as temporaries — direct composite build-ups or milled provisionals.
  4. Live with it for several months. Adjust as needed. Confirm phonetics, comfort and aesthetics.
  5. Convert to definitive — only once the bite is stable and tolerated.
Increasing the OVD often creates posterior space that previously did not exist for implant restorations. The plan you could not see in ICP becomes possible at the new vertical.
Slide 23 · Aesthetics & smile line

Where the lower lip dictates incisal edge position

Aesthetic planning is personal — patient values trump clinician preference. But there is a clinical anchor: the smile line is determined by the lower lip, not just the upper.

  • If the existing teeth sit above the lower lip line, you can lengthen them and they will still show.
  • If they already sit at the lower lip line, lengthening them creates a "lip trap" — they hide rather than display.
  • Always assess at the smile, the speech "F/V" line and rest position.
Daughter test: Some clinicians use "would you do this on your daughter?" — but be careful. Your daughter has your values; the patient has theirs. Don't impose preferences. A patient who wants a gold incisor can have a gold incisor.
Slide 24 · Resin-bonded bridges (1)

Resin-bonded bridges — the conservative alternative

Modern enamel adhesion is so reliable that resin-bonded bridges have become a credible — sometimes preferable — alternative to single implant crowns, especially for missing anteriors and where future implant placement is being considered.

Key principles

  • Cantilever (one wing) beats fixed (two wings) for survival. Differential movement of two abutments dislodges fixed designs.
  • Minimally restored abutments with sufficient enamel for adhesion.
  • No prep / minimal prep — especially if a future implant is planned.
  • Use the right cement — a resin cement containing 10-MDP (e.g. Panavia).
Slide 25 · Resin-bonded bridges (2)

Survival vs retrievability

Survival rates

  • King paper: 81% at 5 years, 80% at 10 years for resin-bonded bridges.
  • Single implant crowns have higher raw survival.
  • Old composite restorations on the abutment = 3× higher failure — replace or polish before bonding.
  • Patient satisfaction with RBBs is consistently high (~91% would recommend).

The retrievability advantage

When an RBB fails, it just falls off — no biological damage. You re-cement and you are back to baseline.

When an implant fails, you may face screw loosening, peri-implantitis, porcelain fracture, or full implant loss with grafting needed.

Lower survival, but lower consequence

Slide 27 · Cementation tips

Bonding protocol — small details, big difference

  • Sandblast the metal or zirconia wing intaglio surface.
  • 10-MDP primer on metal/zirconia (built into Panavia).
  • Etch enamel 30s phosphoric acid, rinse, dry.
  • Bonding agent on enamel as per manufacturer's IFU.
  • Resin cement — never zinc phosphate or GIC.
  • Use a location hook or singulum rest — ensures full seating during cementation; trim afterwards.
  • Read the laminated instruction card every time. The biggest evolution in dental cements has been those instruction cards.

Avoiding the grey-out

Metal wings can show through translucent incisal enamel. Three solutions:

  • Use a thicker tooth (canine to replace lateral) so the wing doesn't reach incisal enamel.
  • Score a line on the palatal surface — tell the lab to finish short of that line.
  • Trial the grey-out using green stick compound behind the tooth before final fabrication.
  • Consider zirconia bridges as an alternative.
Slide 28 · Knowledge check

Quick self-check

Spell out PECOFA. In which direction do you stabilise, and in which direction do you plan?
Periodontal → Endodontic → Coronal → Occlusal → Functional → Aesthetic. Stabilise P → A (perio first). Plan A → P (start with where the teeth need to be aesthetically and work backward).
What is the threshold for periodontal stability?
Pocket depths ≤ 4 mm, BoP < 10%, no bleeding at 4 mm sites. Above 10% BoP with shallow pockets = remission. Pockets ≥ 5 mm or active bleeding = unstable — do not place implants.
Why must you not use chlorhexidine as an endodontic irrigant when also using sodium hypochlorite?
They react to form parachloroaniline — a precipitate with carcinogenic potential. EDTA is the correct adjunct to NaOCl (smear layer removal).
Approximately how much does a good coronal seal improve endodontic success?
About 11×. Many studies suggest a good coronal restoration with mediocre endo outperforms a perfect endo with poor coronal seal. Aquilino & Caplan: crowned RCT teeth had ~6× higher likelihood of survival.
Why prefer a one-wing cantilever resin-bonded bridge over a two-wing fixed design?
Differential movement of the two abutments stresses the bond and dislodges one wing. The remaining wing then traps caries. Cantilevers move with their single abutment and survive longer in most anterior situations.
What is the minimum ferrule height for a posterior tooth?
Approximately 4 mm. Anteriors can sometimes manage with 2–3 mm. Width matters as well as height — and no ferrule should always prompt re-evaluation of restorability.
Slide 29 · Take-home messages

Take-home messages from Lecture 2

🎯 The big ideas

  • PECOFA — stabilise in this order, plan in reverse.
  • Perio is the foundation — non-negotiable.
  • The coronal seal matters more than the endo.
  • A post on a tooth = last chance saloon.
  • Resin-bonded bridges trade survival for retrievability.

🛠 The clinical habits

  • Plaque + bleeding scores at every visit.
  • OHI is coaching — bring your tools, sit upright, eye-to-eye.
  • No rubber dam = no endo.
  • Provisionalise before reorganising occlusion.
  • Cantilever, not fixed, for most RBBs.
