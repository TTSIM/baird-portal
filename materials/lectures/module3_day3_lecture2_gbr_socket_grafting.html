Implant Types

Tissue-Level vs Bone-Level Implants

Bone-Level Implant

Implant platform sits at or below the alveolar crest. Entire implant body is rough titanium — bone integrates across the full surface.

  • Full rough surface — maximum osseointegration area
  • Custom abutment contours emergence profile
  • Platform switching available: 1.5mm horizontal offset max
  • Works with all phenotypes
  • No risk of metal showing with normal recession

Tissue-Level Implant

Lower portion is rough (osseointegration zone); upper 1.8mm is polished (transmucosal collar). The platform exits at the soft tissue level.

  • Vertical platform switching — abutment-implant junction further from bone
  • Leakage zone is 1.8mm above bone (Straumann)
  • Abutment fitted at gingival level — not subcrestal
  • Less margin for angular correction (only ~1mm vs 4mm)

"Tissue-level was a great solution 25 years ago when all bone-level implants were leaking. Nowadays we have implants that give zero bone loss — tissue-level is overengineering a problem that no longer exists."

Implant Types

Tissue-Level — Limitations

Thin Phenotype Risk

In thin biotype patients the polished collar may show through the gingiva, creating a grey shadow — a significant aesthetic problem in the anterior zone.

  • Metal colour visible through thin tissue
  • If implant placed slightly tilted — only 1mm available for correction (vs 4mm with bone-level)
  • Any gum recession → metal exposed immediately
  • Less forgiving of non-ideal implant positioning
Evidence

GBR Membrane Fixation — The Evidence

A landmark study (published 2004–2024, European journal) definitively showed that membrane fixation method is the single greatest determinant of GBR outcome quality.

①

No Fixation

Worst results

Particles scatter on suturing

②

Sutures Only

Poor results

Fiddly, loses hold by day 5

③

Apical Pins

Moderate results

Better but coronal moves

④

Apical + Coronal

Best results

Pins + 4-corner sutures

Methodology: CBCT taken on Day 1 and Day 180. Coronal 5mm zone evaluated for horizontal and vertical bone stability.

"If someone's arm is broken, we put a cast. If someone's jaw is broken, we put plates. Bone healing requires stability. If the membrane moves, fibroblasts invade — it's that simple."

Modern Protocol

Modern GBR — Four Non-Negotiables

🚫

No Cover Screws

Cover screws press on the graft, compressing particles. Use healing abutments to tent the soft tissue over the graft instead.

📌

Stabilise the Membrane

Apical and coronal fixation. Pins (My Zinger) placed at 4 corners. Non-resorbable sutures supplement the pins.

🏗️

Build Convexity

Overfill the space deliberately. Creates dead space that guides bone volume and supports soft tissue contour long-term.

🔒

Proper Seal

Hydrophilic membrane must create a complete barrier. No connective tissue invasion into the graft space.

Violating even one of these four principles compromises the entire graft. They are not optional — they are the protocol.

Critical Terminology

Socket Grafting vs Ridge Preservation

"Socket preservation does not exist. The socket will always collapse. You either have socket grafting or ridge preservation. If I hear 'socket preservation' I will pour cold water on you."

Socket Grafting

Placing graft material into a socket at the time of extraction. The socket is the container.

  • Done simultaneously with extraction
  • Socket walls provide 2–4 wall containment
  • Minimises collapse of existing architecture
  • First described by Chen (2009)

Ridge Preservation

The broader goal: maintaining ridge volume for future implant placement, which may involve grafting, membranes, or both.

  • Outcome-focused term (what we're preserving)
  • May or may not include a membrane
  • Applies to post-extraction management strategy
  • Can be combined with staged GBR planning

Note: GBR and GTR were historically invented before ridge preservation became mainstream — though they are often perceived as newer by non-specialists.

Pre-Conditions

Requirements for Successful Socket Grafting

Three clinical pre-conditions must be confirmed before attempting socket grafting. Violating any one predicts failure.

✓

1. No Acute Infection

Cellulitis, pus discharge, aggressive inflammation → do NOT attempt socket grafting. Clean granuloma/granulation tissue: acceptable after thorough debridement.

✓

2. Intact Labial Plate

A compromised labial plate means a 2-wall defect at best — the socket will collapse. If absent: raise a flap and add a membrane labially to rebuild the wall.

✓

3. Intact Interseptal Bone

Not essential, but significantly improves outcomes. Preserving interproximal bone maintains the peaks that support papillae and provides blood supply.

"If you broken or separated the labial bone during extraction, conventional ridge preservation is not going to work — you'll have a 2-wall defect and everything collapses."

Step-by-Step

Socket Grafting — 10 Steps

1

Atraumatic Extraction

Preserve labial plate — sectioning roots if needed. No forceful levering.

2

Check Labial Plate

Confirm walls are intact before committing to socket grafting.

3

Degranulation

Burs at 800RPM + irrigation from apex → coronal. No fibrotic tissue left.

4

Bone Sounding

Confirm all bony walls present. Assess inter-proximal peaks.

5

Tunnelling / Flap

Anterior: labial tunnel (trapezoid) past mucogingival line. Posterior: radial tunnel or envelope flap.

6

Membrane Preparation

Mucoderm hydrated 2–3 min (not 5). Cut to inverted sandwich shape — narrow coronal, wider base.

7

Palatal / Lingual Fixation

Stabilise membrane palatally first. Deflects from cross-contamination. Keeps socket accessible.

8

Pack Graft Material

Apical third first, then middle, then coronal — only to level of mesial/distal peaks. Do not overfill.

9

Tuck Membrane Labially

6mm away from keratinised margin. First suture with longer ends, second parallel, then pull together.

10

X-Suture for Closure

Non-resorbable. Prevents mucoderm expansion and escape between stitches.

Biomaterial

Mucoderm — What It Is & How to Use It

What is Mucoderm?

A porcine pericardium-derived acellular collagen matrix. Very dense, elastic — acts as a connective tissue substitute.

  • Can be left exposed (unlike standard collagen membranes)
  • Thicker than standard membranes — adds soft tissue volume
  • As it heals: expands as blood supply enters (gets softer)
  • Dual benefit: graft cover + soft tissue augmentation

Hydration Protocol

  • Guidelines say: 5 minutes in saline
  • Clinical recommendation: 2–3 minutes maximum
  • Over-hydration → too soft → difficult to suture, expands further
  • Under-hydration → harder to suture through but controllable
  • Under-hydration is better than over-hydration

Shape & Placement

  • Cut to inverted sandwich shape
  • Wider base goes into trapezoid flap (labial)
  • Narrow coronal part matches socket diameter
  • Measure socket width before cutting — cut exactly to size
  • Tuck 1/3 palatally, 1/3 centrally over socket, 1/3 labially

"As it heals, mucoderm starts to expand. Use an X-suture — otherwise it escapes between interrupted sutures and the patient can accidentally pull it out with their tongue."

Alternative: Dense PTFE (Permamem)

Also exposed-compatible. Does NOT provide soft tissue volume boost. Must be removed later. Choose Mucoderm when soft tissue augmentation is desired simultaneously.

Technique Detail

Tunnelling — The Trapezoid Technique

The trapezoid tunnel is essential for creating a pocket that will hold the membrane without visible tension or incision lines in the aesthetic zone.

How to Create It

  • Tunnelling instrument: flat and blunt (not sharp like elevator)
  • Enter at midpoint of socket margin
  • Separate mucosa and periosteum together (full thickness)
  • Rotate medially then distally to widen the base
  • Cross the mucogingival line — creates wider trapezoid base
  • Test: pull socket edge gently — if it moves easily, tunnel is done

The trapezoid = wider base, narrower neck at socket. This allows membrane to sit flat — any internal fold creates a dead space that collapses.

Anterior vs Posterior Approach

Anterior: labial tunnel only (preserve papillae). Posterior: radial tunnel or small envelope flap (better visualisation of bony walls).

Grafting Technique

Packing the Graft Material

How you pack the graft is as important as what you pack. Incorrect compression or fill level causes collapse or displacement.

Packing Rules

  • Fill in thirds — apical first, then middle, then coronal
  • Compact each layer loosely — not tight like amalgam
  • Blood supply must penetrate between particles
  • Overpacking = particles suffocate, no vascularisation
  • Fill only to level of mesial/distal peaks — not above
  • Overfilling leaves no space for soft tissue closure in the centre

"I actually like using an amalgam plugger — but I'm NOT compacting like amalgam. Loose packing, all the way to the apex."

Material Choice for Socket Grafting

  • Cerabone — xenograft, volume stability, slow resorption. Best for pontic sites, pure volume maintenance
  • MaxGraph — allograft. Mix cancellous (vitality) + cortical (volume)
  • MaxGraph+ — with HA, sticky/malleable; better handling
  • Alloplast — least predictable for large sockets

Sinus Proximity Warning

If sinus floor visible after extraction: check if membrane is intact. If intact: place haemostatic sponge as barrier before grafting. If perforated: socket is NOT suitable for grafting.

Suturing Strategy

Why Non-Resorbable Sutures Are Essential

Non-Resorbable (Nylon) — Preferred

  • Monofilament → less plaque trapping than braided
  • Maintains strength for full 2 weeks until removal
  • Does NOT gradually lose tension during healing
  • Reduces risk of membrane escape during first 10 days
  • Removed at 2-week review

X-Suture Specifically

  • Prevents mucoderm from expanding and escaping
  • Keeps membrane flat — no internal folds
  • If corners are unstable: add X-suture on top of interrupted
  • For mandible: stabilise both labially AND lingually

Resorbable Sutures — Risks

  • Braided construction traps more plaque
  • Strength degrades from day 1 — not 100→0, but gradual
  • By day 5: significantly reduced tension
  • Materials still regenerating but suture already failing
  • Higher risk of membrane escape and graft contamination

"Resorbable sutures go 100, 90, 80 — they don't go from 100 to 0. By day 5, they're no longer reliable for holding a membrane."

Suture Placement Distance

Labial sutures: placed ≥6mm from keratinised margin. Leave those margins for final closure integrity.

Decision Tool

Socket Grafting — Decision Tree

Step 1: Check the Labial Plate

Intact labial plate?

  • YES + good mesio-distal peaks → proceed to conventional socket grafting (flapless possible)
  • NO → raise flap, place membrane labially to rebuild wall, then graft

Step 2: Anterior vs Posterior

  • Anterior: labial tunnel, soft tissue grafting consideration, high aesthetic demand
  • Posterior: radial tunnel or envelope, sinus check if upper, lingual release for mandible

When to Do Socket Grafting

  • Implant planned 9–12+ months after extraction
  • Adjacent implant at risk if left ungrafted
  • Planned bridge pontic site (long-term volume maintenance)
  • Class IV root positioning (Kahn) — always graft
  • Thin phenotype at high aesthetic risk
  • Patient has high smile line

When NOT to Socket Graft

  • Acute cellulitis or active infection present
  • Early placement can give equal or better result
  • Extra procedure: extra antibiotics, healing time, cost, risk
  • 2-wall defect (collapsed labial) — staged GBR instead
Anatomy

The Kahn Classification — Root Positioning

Described by Joseph Kahn (Professor, Loma Linda University), published 2011. Defines anterior root-to-bone relationships that determine implant strategy.

Type I

🟢

Good labial AND palatal bone. Most common: 86% of cases. Immediate placement possible.

Type II

🔵

Limited labial AND limited palatal bone. Both still present. ~5% of cases. Adjust implant angle — immediate possible.

Type III

🟡

Very little palatal bone, thick labial. Very rare: 0.5% of cases. Unusual angulation — plan carefully.

Type IV

🔴

Thin palatal AND thin/absent labial. Always ridge preserve. Immediate placement contraindicated — labial collapse inevitable.

"Type IV is absolutely predetermined to have ridge preservation. If you place an implant without grafting, you will have vertical and horizontal diffusion — guaranteed."

Kahn Classification Applied

Type IV — Management Strategy

Why Type IV Fails Without Grafting

  • Thin labial plate often fractures on extraction
  • Even if plate survives: too thin to support mucosa
  • Blood clot alone → immediate collapse labially
  • Implant placed without grafting → exposed labial threads
  • Dehiscence inevitable within healing period

Correct Management

  • Extract atraumatically — preserve whatever remains
  • Assess walls immediately post-extraction
  • Socket grafting (ridge preservation) always indicated
  • If labial wall absent: place membrane labially to rebuild
  • Wait 3–6 months for bone maturation
  • Re-enter with CBCT confirmation before implant

"Even if the CBCT shows bone, the real situation at extraction may show fractures and dehiscences not visible on the scan. Always be prepared."

New 2025 Technique

Magnesium Membrane — Replacing the Labial Wall

A major advance: using rigid magnesium membrane as an artificial labial wall during socket grafting — avoiding the need for staged bone grafting in some cases.

The Technique

  • Rigid Novamat membrane adapted to labial surface
  • Contoured to recreate labial wall shape
  • Seated against mesio-distal peaks for stability
  • No pins needed — tunnel holds it (if tunnel is tight)
  • Socket grafting steps then proceed normally
  • Membrane resorbs to Mg salts → bone strengthening

Why This Matters

Previously, absent labial plate meant staged GBR (a separate second procedure). This technique creates the wall at extraction → potentially avoids second surgery.

Case Example (2025)

  • Patient: cracked tooth under microscope → extraction indicated
  • CBCT: no labial plate already visible pre-extraction
  • On extraction: labial plate confirmed absent
  • Rigid magnesium shaped → adapted to labial contour
  • MaxGraph+ packed into socket sockets (3 separate sausages)
  • Mucoderm placed as trapdoor with X-suture
  • 16-week result: ridge volume and bone stability maintained

"Novamat membrane: so rigid and contoured — it sits on the mesial and distal peaks. If tunnel is good, it won't move without extra stabilisers."

Indications

When to Do Ridge Preservation

Do Ridge Preservation When

  • Implant planned — 9–12+ months away
  • Adjacent implant present — extraction collapses bone around it
  • Planned pontic site on a bridge (volume maintenance for esthetics)
  • Class IV root positioning — always
  • High smile line with aesthetic demands
  • Limited available bone — every millimetre counts

Skip Socket Grafting When

  • Acute infection (cellulitis, pus) — graft will fail
  • Early placement is feasible and gives same result
  • Patient doesn't want any future treatment there
  • Alloplast likely to be used — unpredictable for larger sockets

"Every time you do ridge preservation, post-operative antibiotics are required. Every time. GBR = antibiotics. No exceptions."

Adjacent Implant Rule

Any time an implant exists next to a tooth being extracted: always consider ridge preservation. The soft tissue papillae and bone around the implant are at risk without it.

Antibiotics

Post-Operative Antibiotic Protocol

Ridge preservation and GBR procedures are not simple extractions. Antibiotic prophylaxis is a non-negotiable part of the protocol.

Always Give Antibiotics After

  • Any ridge preservation procedure
  • Any GBR (guided bone regeneration)
  • Any bone grafting with membrane
  • Any socket grafting with alloplast/xenograft

Why It Matters with Xenograft

Xenograft behaves like "gunpowder" — a spark of bacteria on it and it ignites rapidly. Infection spreads faster and more aggressively than with autogenous grafts. If xenograft graft fails:

  • Remove everything
  • Debride thoroughly
  • Allow natural healing before any new graft

Alloplast Timing

Alloplast remodels faster — possibly viewable at 8–10 weeks — but quality is less predictable. New bone may be too soft for immediate implant. Trust the X-ray and clinical feel.

Degranulation

Degranulation — The Underrated Step

Thorough socket debridement is as important as the graft material itself. Fibrotic tissue remnants cause chronic inflammation that destroys grafts.

Using Degranulation Burs

  • 4 different sizes (wider → narrower toward apex)
  • Start with widest, progress to smallest
  • 800 RPM with constant irrigation
  • Clean from coronal to apex — not just walls
  • Like electric vs manual toothbrush: far more effective than curettes
  • Check all walls — no fibrotic tissue should remain

"Little pieces of fibrotic tissue or degranulation can cause inflammation to your graft — all that hard work goes to waste."

Manual Degranulation (When Burs Absent)

  • Use Lucas curette and straight elevator
  • Work methodically around all 4 walls
  • Check for any chronic granuloma — scoop en bloc
  • More time-consuming and less thorough

Cross-Contamination Prevention

  • Have 2 suction tips during socket grafting
  • Be aware of what is in the patient's saliva
  • Stabilise membrane away from socket when packing
  • Avoid cross-contamination during particle placement
  • Xenograft + bacteria = rapid catastrophic failure
Post-Op Appearance

What Normal Looks Like at 2 Weeks

The appearance of socket grafts at 2 weeks alarms many clinicians. Understanding what is normal prevents premature interference.

Normal Findings at 2 Weeks

  • Yellowish appearance — normal, not infection
  • Slimy, slippery texture — early healing response
  • Slightly swollen tissue over graft
  • Membrane looks "nasty" from outside

What to Do

  • Remove sutures only — do NOT disturb the healing membrane
  • Do NOT wipe or clean the surface aggressively
  • Do NOT pull on apparent membrane edges
  • Things look worse before they look better
  • Review again at 8–10 weeks

Critical Warning

If the clinician mistakes the normal 2-week appearance for infection and removes or disrupts the membrane:

  • Bone is exposed underneath
  • Risk of osteitis and total graft failure
  • Everything that was preserved is lost

"If at 2 weeks you think this is an infection and you wipe it off — your bone is going to get exposed. Don't worry. This is how it should look when it starts healing."

Case Study

Ridge Preservation — 8-Year Follow-Up

1

Presentation

Patient with very thin labial plate, high smile line. Previous extraction site — thin phenotype, high aesthetic demand.

2

Ridge Preservation

Flap raised (thin labial plate). Compact with maxgraft. Membrane labially. Let bone and gum heal. Height and width maintained at 6 months.

3

Implant Placement + GBR

Implant placed with simultaneous GBR for final contouring. ~2mm bone around implant confirmed. No second-stage GBR needed (sufficient bone frame).

4

Restoration + Long-Term

Temporary crown for soft tissue sculpting. Final crown placed. 8-year review: still stable, healthy, good keratinized tissue. Patient very satisfied.

"By taking our time with the gum and soft tissue contour, this patient developed really nice soft tissue. 8 years down the line — still very stable and very good control."

Pre-Existing Implants

Extracting Next to Existing Implants

This scenario requires special consideration — the adjacent implant's bone, papillae, and emergence profile are all at risk when a neighbouring tooth is extracted.

What Can Go Wrong

  • Papillae formed around implant immediately collapse
  • Bone around implant is lost in remodelling wave
  • Implant threads may become exposed distally
  • Cosmetic and functional compromise of existing restoration

"Any time you have an implant and you have to take the tooth out next door — your first thought needs to be: I might do ridge preservation there."

Management Strategy

  • Even if patient refuses further implants: still graft to protect existing implant
  • Cerabone in socket = volume maintenance for adjacent bone
  • Free gingival graft as cover — sutured as trapflap over socket
  • Protect papillae on implant side throughout

Case Shown

Patient with existing implant on premolar, failing molar next door. Even without plans for replacement: cerabone placed, free gingival graft stitched on top. 10 weeks later: bone volume and papilla maintained despite no tooth in contact.

Full Arch Scenario

Multiple Adjacent Teeth — Sequential Strategy

When multiple anterior teeth need replacement, the staging and sequencing of extractions, grafting and implant placement is critical to preserving papillae and tissue contours.

1

Extract Laterals First, Place Implants

Place lateral incisor implants with GBR. Allow osseointegration (12+ weeks). Keep central incisors in place — they maintain papillae and bone.

2

Temporise on Laterals

Once lateral implants are integrated, connect temporaries. This creates the foundation for papilla reconstruction.

3

Extract Centrals — Now Load Laterals

Remove central incisors only once lateral implant temporaries are in place. The temporaries now maintain the papillae. Graft central sockets.

4

Place Central Implants

After socket healing, place central implants with GBR. Papilla is preserved by the lateral temporary bridge throughout.

"When you temporise and you're ready to load — that's a good time to extract the central teeth. Papilla is stable because the temporaries are there."

Patient Communication

Informed Consent for Ridge Preservation

Always Warn Patients

  • Ridge preservation does NOT guarantee avoiding GBR at implant stage
  • Many ridge preservation cases still need GBR when implant placed
  • Post-operative antibiotics always required
  • 2-week review for suture removal mandatory
  • Normal healing looks alarming at 2 weeks — advise in advance
  • Extra procedure = extra healing time, cost, risk

Regarding Material Preference

If patients express concerns about certain materials (religious, ethical, or personal):

  • Explain openly what is available and why each is recommended
  • Offer alternatives but be honest about the trade-offs
  • If using an inferior material due to patient preference: charge more (more work to achieve same result, if achievable)
  • Document patient's informed decision clearly

"I would recommend this material because that's going to give you the best result. If you have any negativity towards these materials, we can use alternatives — but the result cannot be guaranteed to be the same."

GBR Workshop Review

Keratinised Mucosa — Managing the Exposure

At stage 2 (implant exposure), managing where the keratinised mucosa goes is critical to long-term peri-implant health.

Standard Exposure Technique

  • Move keratinised mucosa from crestal → labial
  • Leave ectoderm in sulcus — re-epithelialises into good epithelium
  • Healing abutments placed through re-positioned keratinised tissue
  • Result: keratinised mucosa on labial → better long-term stability

Free Gingival Graft — When Needed

  • After excessive periosteal releases during GBR (keratinised lost)
  • Thin phenotype patients with inadequate tissue
  • Split-thickness graft from palate
  • Stitched to labial surface
  • Adds 2+ mm of keratinised width

"Every time you do a periosteal release, you move the flap — keratinised to non-keratinised. If you do it 3 times in one surgery, you've destroyed your keratinised mucosa. Free gingival graft becomes necessary."

Workshop Technique

Socket Grafting — Practical Tips

Graft Consistency Tips

  • Cerabone + saline → 1:1 ratio → chewing gum consistency
  • Cerabone+ (HA added) → even stickier, easier handling
  • Model particles into small sausages — feeds into each socket
  • One socket at a time — don't try to fill all at once
  • MaxGraph+ → same handling, better for large sockets

Palatal Flap Behaviour

  • If palatal mucosa is very dense — no suturing needed (pushes over membrane)
  • In mandible: must stabilise both labially AND lingually
  • Mid-crestal incision splits keratinised mucosa equally on both sides

Tunnel Troubleshooting

  • If mucosa won't move: need more release, not more force
  • Very thin upper anterior mucosa: small microsurgical blade
  • Test the tunnel: grab margin with tweezers, pull gently — should move easily
  • Over-tunnelling: membrane will move left/right — add stabilisers
  • First few cases: lift a small flap for better visibility

"For your first few experiences: lift the flap. It's actually nicer to see what you're doing. Then you progress to tunnelling without cutting."

2025 Update

The New Decision Tree — What Changed?

The arrival of rigid resorbable magnesium membranes in 2025 changed the decision tree for cases with absent or severely compromised labial plates.

Pre-2025 (Classic)

  • Intact labial plate → socket grafting → heal → implant
  • Absent labial plate → staged GBR → re-entry → socket graft → heal → implant
  • At least 2 separate surgical procedures for deficient cases

2025 — Magnesium Membrane Approach

  • Absent labial plate → rigid Mg membrane placed as wall replacement at extraction
  • Socket grafting proceeds normally with artificial wall in place
  • Mg resorbs to Mg salts → strengthens bone as it heals
  • Potential to avoid staged bone grafting for selected cases
  • Still early — but published research emerging

"A new player visited the market — something they've been developing for 6 years. A tool to help us regenerate the labial bone without having to do a second re-entry."

Complication Management

When Socket Grafting Fails

Signs of Failure

  • Sudden increase in swelling (beyond expected) after week 2
  • Purulent discharge from socket
  • Pain escalating rather than improving
  • Membrane completely displaced
  • Graft material visible and contaminated

Management Protocol

  • Remove ALL graft material — do not leave contaminated xenograft
  • Thorough debridement and irrigation
  • Allow natural healing of the empty socket
  • Antibiotics if infection present
  • Wait minimum 8–12 weeks before re-grafting
  • Review aetiology — was it infection, exposure, stabilisation failure?

"Xenograft is like gunpowder. As soon as a spark of bacteria goes on it, it flares up really fast. You can destroy a socket you thought you were helping."

Prevention Strategy

  • Thorough degranulation before grafting
  • Antibiotic coverage post-operatively
  • Proper membrane stabilisation (won't expose)
  • Good oral hygiene pre-operatively
  • Avoid grafting in infected sockets
  • Chlorhexidine rinse post-operatively

Key Takeaways

1. Terminology Matters

Socket grafting or ridge preservation. Never "socket preservation." The socket always collapses.

2. Stabilise the Membrane

Apical + coronal pins and sutures. This is the single most important determinant of GBR outcome.

3. Kahn Type IV = Always Graft

Thin labial + thin palatal. No exceptions. Plan for membrane labially if wall is absent.

Yellow, slimy, slippery — this is normal. Do NOT disturb. Remove sutures only.

Every GBR and ridge preservation procedure requires post-operative antibiotic cover. Always.

Rigid resorbable Mg can rebuild the labial wall at extraction — a potential game-changer for Type IV cases.
