Core Principle

The 2mm Rule

One of the most important numbers in implant dentistry. Every clinical decision about whether to graft flows from this principle.

Why 2mm?

  • Bacterial microleakage: The implant-abutment junction has a microgap. Bacteria enter and exit this junction continuously.
  • Bone response: Research shows that if the bone labially is less than 2mm, bacteria reaching the bone at 100% concentration will dissolve it.
  • Blood supply deprivation: Drilling the osteotomy removes 50% of the blood supply to the thin labial bone — leaving periosteum as the only source.
  • With 2mm+: Bacteria arrive diluted. Even with some saucerisation, enough bone survives long-term.
Aesthetic Principle

Building Convexity

Even when 2mm bone exists, this may not be enough. Convexity is what makes an implant restoration indistinguishable from natural dentition.

What is Convexity?

  • The natural labial curvature of the alveolar ridge and overlying mucosa
  • Without convexity, the soft tissue looks flat or concave — clearly prosthetic
  • The bone provides the scaffold for the overlying mucosa's shape
  • Convexity makes the "pink" match the neighbouring teeth

The Pink-White Principle: "To get the white right, you must first get the pink right. And to get the pink right, you need sufficient bone underneath."

Two Options to Build Convexity

  • Xenograft (outer layer): Provides long-term volume stability. Preferred by the ITI (Buser) school.
  • Connective tissue graft: Builds soft tissue convexity directly. Preferred by Zucchelli (Bologna) school.
  • Both approaches are valid — the key is you must do SOMETHING.

Decision Framework

The Three Questions Before Every GBR Decision

Answer these three questions in sequence. Any "No" answer redirects you to staged bone grafting.

1

Can I place the implant in the correct prosthetic position?

YES → Proceed to Q2  |  NO → Staged bone grafting required. Build bone first, then place implant.

2

Can I get adequate primary stability (≥25 Ncm)?

YES → Proceed to Q3  |  NO → Staged approach. Cannot perform simultaneous GBR without implant stability.

3

Does the defect have ≥2 bony walls remaining?

YES → Simultaneous GBR is appropriate  |  NO → Staged approach. No walls = no blood supply, no stability for graft particles.

All three YES: Place implant + simultaneous GBR (particles + membrane). This is your "bread and butter" — every good implantologist must master this.

Normal Surgical Finding

Fenestration & Dehiscence — Don't Panic

Exposed threads on the labial aspect of an anterior implant are expected and normal. This is not an error — it is anatomy.

Why Anterior Implants Are ALWAYS Exposed

  • Prosthetic envelope requires the implant to be slightly more upright than the natural root angle
  • The premaxilla always has a concavity — worst at the lateral incisor region
  • Following the bone = labially inclined implant = bad prosthetics
  • Following the prosthetic position = fenestration or dehiscence = CORRECT placement

Old School Error: Tilting the implant palatally to follow bone and avoid exposure. Result — the implant shoulder comes out labially, puts pressure on the cortical plate, which then resorbs. Long clinical crown or metal exposure follows.

Fenestration vs Dehiscence

  • Fenestration: Isolated window of exposed threads — apical region — while coronal bone is intact
  • Dehiscence: Exposed threads extend from the shoulder of the implant apically — entire labial surface exposed
  • Both are managed identically: GBR with bone particles + membrane

Clinical Rule: If you place an anterior implant and do NOT see fenestration or dehiscence — either you are in the wrong position, OR you have a very thick ridge (lucky 3%). Expect exposure and plan for GBR accordingly.

GBR Biology

What GBR Requires: Stability + Angiogenesis

Successful guided bone regeneration depends on two non-negotiable biological principles. Miss either one and the GBR will fail.

1. Clot Stability

  • A stable blood clot must form and be maintained under the membrane
  • Movement disrupts clot organisation → connective tissue infiltrates instead of bone
  • Implant must be immobile (adequate primary stability)
  • Membrane must be immobilised (pins, sutures)
  • Bone particles provide scaffold for clot stability

2. Angiogenesis (Blood Supply)

  • New blood vessel formation is essential for bone cell migration
  • Blood supply comes from: bony walls, periosteum, and bone marrow
  • No walls = no blood supply = GBR cannot work
  • Decortication (perforating the cortical plate) in the mandible helps initiate blood flow

The Bridge Analogy: Building a bridge requires two mountains to anchor from. Bony walls are those mountains. They provide both blood supply and mechanical stability. "You cannot build a bridge between a mountain and infinity."

Decortication

  • Perforation of the cortical plate to stimulate bleeding and growth factors
  • Especially important in the mandible — very dense cortical bone limits blood flow
  • In the maxilla: less critical (cancellous bone naturally more vascular)
  • Use round bur or piezo to create multiple small perforations

Material Detail

Autogenous Bone — Collection Techniques

Autogenous bone is the gold standard. The challenge is how to collect enough with minimal patient morbidity.

Slow Drilling Technique

  • Only applicable to the 2nd, 3rd, and 4th drills (NOT the first drill)
  • Reduce speed to 50–100 rpm and switch off saline irrigation
  • Bone collects on the drill flutes
  • Red bone: Good quality — use it
  • White bone: Overheated, necrotic — discard, stop the technique
  • Yields small amounts — ideal for small defects only

Bone Scraper

  • Manual scraper used on the exposed cortical bone after flap reflection
  • Scrape from superior to inferior, with firm pressure
  • Sharpen yearly or use disposable safe scrapers
  • Better quality than drill shavings
  • Used anywhere within the flap: chin, ramus, body of mandible

Drill shavingsLowest (cells crushed)Small defects, first layer
Bone scraperGoodGBR inner layer, stage grafts

Material Detail

Allograft — The Practical Alternative

Allograft is processed human cadaver bone. When autogenous is unavailable, insufficient, or undesirable, allograft delivers comparable clinical results.

Clinical Advantages

  • Order as much as needed — no harvesting limitations
  • Available as cancellous, cortical, or corticocancellous
  • Small or large particle sizes available
  • No second surgical site → lower patient morbidity
  • 30 seconds to prepare vs 30 minutes for autogenous collection

Recommended: Max graft (from Botiss) — considered the highest-quality allograft available. Available as cortical, cancellous, or corticocancellous.

Best Applications for Allograft

  • Inner layer of the layering technique (replacing autogenous)
  • Staged bone grafting (large volume needs)
  • Sinus grafting — preferred over xenograft (biodegrades, doesn't block ostium if perforated)
  • Cases where autogenous collection is not justified

Remodelling Time (Staged Grafts): Allograft requires 24 weeks before implant placement — longer than autogenous (12–16 weeks) but shorter than xenograft (8–9 months).

Material Detail

Xenograft — Volume Stability

Xenograft's key virtue is its long-term volume stability. It resorbs very slowly, maintaining the contour created at surgery for years.

Cerabone (Botiss) — Preferred Xenograft

  • Heated to 1200°C — highest temperature processing available
  • High temperature: removes all organics, prions, viruses, bacteria
  • Creates high hardness hydroxyapatite particles
  • Rough surface attracts osteoblasts to grow on top
  • Hydrophilic — absorbs blood and saline uniformly
  • At 90° on a spatula, bone stays in place — at 180°, still adheres

Hydrophilicity Advantage

  • Cerabone's hydrophilic property allows homogeneous blood/saline absorption
  • Other xenografts absorb unevenly — require manual mixing
  • Result: uniform particle hydration → better initial handling and integration

⚠ Xenograft Limitation: On biopsy — large remaining particles, little vital bone. The particles are volume-fillers. Do NOT use xenograft as the layer directly against the implant surface. Reserve it for the outer layer / convexity building.

Long-term Study: Hannover University — Cerabone shows ~10% volume loss at 4 years. Superior to other xenografts due to high-temperature processing and hydrophilic nature.

Core Clinical Technique

The Layering Technique

The most important GBR technique to master. Uses different materials in different zones to maximise both biological and aesthetic outcomes.

Why Layer?

  • Different zones around the implant have different requirements
  • Near the implant: need vital bone formation, osteoconductivity
  • Outer layer: need volume stability and convexity maintenance
  • No single material excels at both
  • Layering combines the best properties of multiple materials

The Two Layers

  • Inner layer (around implant): Autogenous bone OR allograft (Max graft). This layer directly protects the implant and promotes vital bone formation.
  • Outer layer (surface/convexity): Xenograft (Cerabone). This layer builds and maintains the ridge contour long-term. Volume stability is the priority here.
Layering Technique

Layering Rules — What Goes Where

Five clinical rules that guide the layering technique. Mastering these puts you in the top 25% of implantologists.

1

Always graft — even with 2mm bone

2mm bone = protection, not convexity. You still need to build the ridge profile for aesthetic and hygienic reasons. The question is HOW (xenograft vs CTG) — not WHETHER to graft.

2

NEVER put xenograft directly on exposed implant threads

Exposed threads need vital bone formation. Autogenous or allograft goes against the implant. Xenograft goes on top as the outer layer. This distinguishes you from most clinicians in the UK.

3

Collect autogenous from slow drilling (2nd/3rd drills only, <100rpm, no saline)

Red bone = use it. White bone = discard. Never use slow drilling on the first drill — too much heat generation without the benefit of irrigation.

4

If no autogenous: allograft is the inner layer substitute

Allograft (Max graft) is the nearest replacement to autogenous — in larger quantities and at far lower burden to the patient. Use it confidently when collection isn't feasible.

5

Xenograft outer layer: prefer high-temperature cerabone; mix 50:50 with allograft

Mixing xenograft with allograft (50:50, 60:40, or 70:30) "adds life to it." Not 100% xenograft needed for contour — a mix is sufficient and improves remodelling.

Advanced Material

Cerabone Plus (7+) — Hyaluronic Acid

Launched by Botiss in 2021 — cerabone combined with sodium hyaluronate. Changes handling and accelerates the healing biology.

What is Sodium Hyaluronate?

  • Powder form of hyaluronic acid — used in orthopaedics for decades
  • Improves healing and controls inflammation
  • Improves angiogenesis and new capillary formation
  • Gives a "kick-start" to the healing process
  • 80% cerabone + 20% sodium hyaluronate in each sachet

Handling Properties: Sodium hyaluronate absorbs saline FASTER than cerabone alone. This means you must use LESS saline when mixing — otherwise the material becomes over-hydrated (like floating vegetables in soup).

The 40% Saline Rule

  • Old recommendation: 50% saline → nice sticky ball in the bowl
  • Problem: placing in socket (which already has blood) → overhydration → floats out
  • New recommendation: 40% saline
  • Mix result: chewing gum consistency
  • When placed in socket with blood → perfect "sticky bone" effect
  • Resist the urge to add more saline — keep mixing and it hydrates further

Uses for Cerabone Plus: Socket grafting, pontic grafting, ridge contouring, sinus grafting (mix 50:50 with allograft for sinus). The sticky ball effect is especially useful in sockets.

GBR Equipment

Membranes — Role and Requirements

The membrane is the barrier that creates the protected space in which bone can regenerate. It must meet several biological and mechanical requirements.

Why Do We Need a Membrane?

  • Without a membrane, fibroblasts invade bone graft material
  • Fibroblast invasion → GBR becomes "mushy" — no bone quality
  • Membrane's job: exclude fast-growing connective tissue cells
  • Allow slower-growing osteoblasts to populate the space undisturbed

What a Membrane Must Do

  • Occlude: block fibroblasts (connective tissue cells)
  • Permeable: allow nutrient diffusion to graft particles
  • Integrate: allow soft tissue to grow onto it (stability)
  • Space-maintain: keep the tent over the graft
  • Biocompatible: no inflammatory response on degradation
  • Handleable: clinician can manipulate, cut, suture, adapt
Membrane Types

Non-Resorbable Membranes

ePTFE (Expanded PTFE)

  • The original barrier membrane — gold standard historically
  • Biologically inert — no allergic or chemical reactions
  • Hydrophobic — doesn't adapt well to margins
  • Hard to handle and manipulate
  • If exposed → bacteria trap → game over. Must remove and sacrifice graft.
  • Must always stay 1.5mm away from neighbouring teeth

d-PTFE (Dense PTFE)

  • More dense than ePTFE → bacteria cannot penetrate even if exposed
  • Perineum (Osteogenics): highly polished d-PTFE — biofilm does not adhere
  • Epithelium grows underneath it if exposed
  • Requires support: titanium tenting screws or bone particles
  • Trade-off: prevents periosteum contributing to bone healing for months
  • Bone quality underneath can be lower than with resorbable

Titanium-Reinforced PTFE

  • Metal framework inside the PTFE provides rigidity
  • Excellent space maintenance — the tent holds its shape
  • Used for large-volume GBR defects
  • Requires second surgery for removal
  • Must be kept 1.5mm from neighbouring teeth
  • Risk of membrane perforation through soft tissue

Key Rule for All Non-Resorbable: Always trim edges smooth and polished. Sharp margins cause tissue dehiscence and recession over neighbouring teeth.

Practical Note: Non-resorbable membranes require a second surgery. If they dehisce — ePTFE is game over; d-PTFE can survive with careful management.

Membrane Types

Resorbable Membranes

Collagen Membranes

Type I collagen or type I+III combination. Biodegrades via enzymatic reaction — no inflammation.

✓ No second surgery required
✓ Easy handling, can be stretched
✓ If exposed: saliva resorbs it quickly — no biofilm platform
✓ Lower complication risk if exposed
✗ Cross-linked type: slower, chemically treated
✗ Non-cross-linked (e.g. Bio-Gide): resorbs faster, tears easily

Synthetic Resorbable (e.g. Tezos)

Synthetic polymer. Slow resorption. Suitable for patients who decline animal products.

✓ Slow resorption = longer barrier effect
✓ No animal-derived material
✓ Produced in controlled conditions
✗ On resorption: chemical inflammation
✗ Multinucleate giant cells triggered
✗ Bone loss expected during resorption phase
✗ High risk of false membrane formation

Novel Technology

Magnesium Membrane — Novel Mag

A relatively recent innovation — a rigid, resorbable membrane made of magnesium alloy. Combines the space-maintenance of titanium with the biological benefit of resorbability.

Properties

  • Rigid and stiff — excellent space maintenance without collapse
  • Resorbable — dissolves via biodegradation (releases hydrogen gas)
  • Does NOT require second surgery for removal
  • Can be perforated with titanium pins for stabilisation
  • Magnesium screws also available (grade 5 alloy — resorb slightly slower)

Best Applications

  • Moderate-to-large GBR defects needing rigid space maintenance
  • Combined with autogenous bone scraping (ramus/chin)
  • Cases where titanium mesh is too rigid/perforating risk

⚠ Caution: As the membrane resorbs, it releases hydrogen gas. If the flap is not perfectly sutured with excellent tension-free closure — wound dehiscence can occur.

Clinical Technique

Membrane Stabilisation & Flap Closure

A membrane that moves is a membrane that fails. Stabilisation is critical. And tension-free closure is non-negotiable.

Membrane Stabilisation Options

  • Titanium pins: Most reliable. Place apically to hold membrane against bone.
  • Tenting screws: Elevate the membrane above the bone to create the "tent" space.
  • Sutures coronally: Suture the coronal membrane edge to papillae or flap.
  • Bone particles: Weight of bone particles beneath helps hold position.
  • Titanium reinforced: Built-in rigidity maintains shape without external pins.

Pin Placement: Pins only need to go in the coronal third of the membrane — you want the convexity there, not all the way to the apex. Think about where you're building the ridge, not just containing the graft.

1.5mm Margin Rule

  • For non-resorbable membranes: ALWAYS leave 1.5mm from neighbouring teeth
  • The papilla and flap need vital bone for blood supply
  • Covering the root surface blocks blood supply to the papilla → dehiscence → recession on neighbouring tooth
  • For resorbable membranes: less critical (resorbs if exposed), but still good practice

Press Release — Tension-Free Closure

  • Must be done BEFORE suturing any GBR case
  • Blade parallel to the flap, pointing toward bone, 5° angle
  • One 1mm notch → stretches to 10mm+
  • Blunt dissection adds further mobility if needed
  • Edge-to-edge suture with no tension = success
Surgical Enhancement

Decortication — Stimulating Blood Flow

Decortication is the deliberate perforation of the cortical plate to open up the marrow space and stimulate blood flow into the GBR site.

Why Decorticate?

  • Cortical bone = dense, avascular. Very little blood supply available for graft.
  • Perforations through cortical → access to medullary cavity
  • Blood, growth factors, and stem cells flow up into the graft
  • Improves angiogenesis and bone cell migration

When to Decorticate

  • Mandible: Always decorticate — very dense D1/D2 cortical bone with limited natural vascularisation
  • Maxilla: Less critical — naturally more cancellous and vascular. If forgotten: not a major concern.
  • Use a round bur or piezo at low speed
  • Multiple small perforations preferable to one large one

Vascularity — Why it Matters: Bone regeneration requires incoming blood vessels (angiogenesis). Without blood supply: graft particles sit as inert filler, osteoblasts cannot migrate in, and healing is poor. Decortication solves this in dense bone.

Clinical Timelines

GBR Healing Timelines

How long to wait before re-entering a grafted site depends on the material used, the membrane type, and patient factors.

Graft Material Simultaneous GBR Staged Grafting (re-entry) Notes
Autogenous 8 weeks (exposure) 12–16 weeks Fastest — all three biological properties active
Allograft 12 weeks (exposure) 24 weeks Second fastest — good for sinus, staged ridge
Xenograft 12 weeks (exposure) 8–9 months Slowest remodelling — only use for outer layer in staged cases

⚠ PTFE Membrane Effect: Even autogenous bone under a non-resorbable PTFE membrane needs 24 weeks — the PTFE prevents periosteal blood supply contribution, slowing all healing regardless of graft type.

Checking Readiness: Take a CBCT and assess bone density before re-entry. These timelines are ranges — patient age, medical history, defect size, and membrane type all modify the actual healing time.

H-Incision for Exposure in Bone-Deficient Areas

  • When limited keratinized mucosa exists buccally — DO NOT use a punch (wastes keratinized tissue)
  • H-incision moves keratinized tissue from the crest to buccal AND lingual aspects
  • Results in ≥2mm of keratinized mucosa buccally around the healing abutment
  • Especially critical in the mandible where keratinized tissue is limited
Posterior Implants

Posterior GBR — Why It Matters Too

GBR is not just an anterior aesthetic concern. Posterior implants with inadequate ridge width lead to restorative problems that are difficult to correct.

The Posterior Ridge Problem

  • Narrow posterior ridge → implant placed lingually → buccal cusp over-angulated
  • Patient bites cheek (upper) or tongue (lower)
  • Lab builds wide crown → big undercut under buccal → food trap
  • Lab makes it look better with ridge-lap → impossible to clean

⚠ Lower Posterior: Lingual cusp cannot be moved inward (tongue injury). Upper posterior: buccal cusp must be kept outward (cheek deflection). Narrow ridge compromises both.

Solution: Graft Before You Restore

  • Build a wide, convex ridge with GBR
  • Place implant in centre of the PROSTHETIC envelope
  • Restoration emerges naturally through the ridge
  • No undercuts, no ridge-lap, easy to clean

Peri-implantitis Risk: Large angle between implant axis and crown axis = elevated peri-implantitis risk. Studies confirm: greater the angle, higher the risk. Build the bone, eliminate the angle.

Remember: "You do not place implants just to restore them. You place them in the right position — even if that means building bone first — so the restoration has a chance of succeeding."

Complete Workflow

Simultaneous GBR — Step-by-Step

A standardised workflow for simultaneous guided bone regeneration — applicable to immediate, early, or delayed implant placement.

01

Flap Elevation

Three-sided flap with golf-club incisions. Full-thickness mucoperiosteal elevation. Expose bone fully around the defect.

02

Debridement

Thorough degranulation at 800 rpm. Clean the socket. Remove all granulation tissue. No residual infection.

03

Decortication

Multiple small perforations through cortical plate (especially mandible). Stimulate blood flow and growth factors.

04

Implant Placement

Correct prosthetic position. Verify torque. Decide transmucosal vs submerge based on torque achieved.

05

Layered Grafting

Inner: autogenous or allograft (around exposed threads). Outer: xenograft or xenograft+allograft mix (convexity).

06

Membrane Placement

Cut membrane to size (1.5mm from neighbours). Lay over graft. Stabilise with Ti pins (apical) and sutures (coronal).

07

Press Release

Periosteal releasing incision on the flap. 1mm notch → 10mm release. Check: flap must close with zero tension.

08

Tension-Free Closure

Interrupted + mattress sutures as needed. No tension across the wound. Good seal especially coronal margin.

09

Review + Exposure

8–12 weeks (material-dependent). Exposure with H-incision if possible. Stage 2 + soft tissue augmentation as needed.

Evidence Base

ITI Consensus 2023 — GBR Recommendations

The International Team for Implantology (ITI) published updated consensus findings in September 2023, validating the modern approach to GBR.

Key Consensus Points

  • Thick buccal bone at placement → predictable stable ridge over time
  • Thin buccal bone + NO GBR → predictable exposed threads and peri-implantitis
  • Thin buccal bone + correct GBR → predictable protection and stable regeneration
  • Xenograft (cerabone) for convexity provides long-term volume stability
  • ITI diagrams now include healing abutment in delayed position AND layering technique as standard

What this Means Clinically

  • Doing GBR correctly is now evidence-based and consensus-supported
  • Not doing GBR when indicated = below the standard of care
  • The layering technique with xenograft outer layer = the ITI-recommended approach
  • Internal sinus lift + GBR combination = appropriate scope for trained clinicians

Key Reference: ITI Consensus Conference September 2023 — guidance on buccal bone thickness management and GBR protocols for implant placement across all timing categories.

Socket Preservation

Socket Grafting — Material Selection

Victoria's topic in detail — but the key principle of material selection based on re-entry timing is essential to understand now.

Material Choice by Re-entry Plan

Re-entry timingMaterial
Implant in 6 monthsAllograft
Implant in 2–5 yearsXenograft
No implant / Pontic onlyXenograft (volume)

Why not xenograft for early re-entry? Xenograft doesn't fully integrate into vital bone. After 6 months, you'll find large xenograft particles and little natural bone. Not optimal for immediate implant stability.

Socket Grafting for Bridges

  • Even for non-implant cases — socket grafting improves bridge pontic design
  • Without grafting: pontic site resorbs → concavity → ridge-lap design
  • With xenograft: maintains volume → ovate pontic → hygienic design
  • Worth suggesting to associates doing extractions + bridges

Principle: "Ideally, every tooth you take out should be socket grafted." The downside is cost and time — but if the patient may want an implant in the future, or is having a bridge, grafting nearly always improves the outcome.

Risk Management

Why GBR Fails

Understanding failure modes is as important as knowing the technique. Most GBR failures are avoidable with correct diagnosis and technique selection.

Common Failure Causes

  • Wrong case selection: GBR attempted on a no-wall defect — no stability, no blood supply
  • Membrane instability: Membrane not pinned securely — movement disrupts the clot
  • Wound dehiscence: Sutured under tension — flap opens, membrane exposed, infection
  • Wrong material: Xenograft only against exposed threads — no vital bone formation
  • Insufficient blood supply: Cortical bone not decorticated (especially mandible)

What To Do If Membrane is Exposed

  • Collagen: Saliva resorbs it → no scaffold for biofilm → localized bone loss only — manageable
  • d-PTFE: Keep clean, epithelium grows under it — can survive with good hygiene
  • ePTFE: Bacteria colonise it immediately → MUST remove membrane and graft

⚠ Most Common Error: Placing the wrong technique for the wrong defect. GBR has an excellent evidence base — failures reflect technique or case selection, not the procedure itself. "GBR is not a bad technique — people choose the wrong technique for the wrong patient."
