Chapter 1

Cement vs Screw-Retained Crowns

This is the single most debated topic in implant restorations. The answer isn't always the same — it depends on the clinical scenario.

Cemented Crown

Concept: An abutment is screwed into the implant. Then a crown is cemented onto the abutment — just like cementing onto a natural tooth.

+
Easier to achieve passive fit
+
Better aesthetics (no screw hole)
+
Simpler lab workflow
−
Excess cement → peri-implantitis
−
NOT retrievable without destruction
−
Cannot remove for maintenance

Screw-Retained Crown

Concept: Crown is screwed directly through to the implant. No cement involved. Screw hole is sealed with composite.

+
Fully retrievable — unscrew and remove
+
No cement risk to soft tissue
+
Can maintain, repair, and modify
−
Screw access hole visible (aesthetic zone)
−
Requires precise implant angulation
−
More demanding lab fabrication

Chapter 1

The Cement Problem

Excess cement is one of the leading causes of peri-implantitis. Unlike natural teeth, implants have no natural barrier to prevent cement from tracking deep into the sulcus.

Cement cannot be seen on X-ray. It's radiolucent. A patient can have cement lodged subgingivally for years without anyone knowing — until bone destruction is advanced.

Why Cement Is So Dangerous

No Periodontal Ligament

Natural teeth have a PDL that creates a seal. Implants have direct bone-to-titanium contact — cement tracks freely down to bone level.

Foreign Body Response

The body treats cement like a splinter. Chronic inflammation starts. Bacteria colonise the cement surface. Bone destruction follows.

Invisible on Radiographs

Composite resin cements are radiolucent. You cannot detect excess cement on a standard periapical or OPG. It's a hidden time bomb.

Chapter 1

Screw-Retained — The Default Choice

Dr Hassan's philosophy: "Everything screw-retained unless there's a specific reason for cement."

"Unbreakable doesn't exist. Unrepairable is what you and I should always avoid." — Screw-retained crowns can always be unscrewed, repaired, modified, or replaced without destroying the underlying implant or abutment.

Retrievability Matters Because:

1
Porcelain chips — you can unscrew and repair
2
Soft tissue changes — you can modify the emergence
3
Screw loosens — you can re-torque without damage
4
Peri-implantitis — you can remove crown to clean
5
Patient's bite changes — you can adjust occlusion

When Cement Might Be Acceptable

Severe Angulation Problem

When the implant angulation puts the screw access hole through the labial surface of the crown. An angled abutment may help, but sometimes cement is the only option.

Cement Level Protocol

If you must cement, place the crown margin no deeper than 1mm subgingival. Use a radiopaque cement. Take a post-cementation radiograph. Explore with a probe.

Best practice when cementing: Use a custom abutment with the cement margin at or just below the gingival margin. Apply cement to the crown (not the abutment). Seat firmly and remove ALL excess immediately. Verify with radiograph.

Chapter 2

Emergence Profile — The Science of Shape

The emergence profile is the shape of the restoration as it emerges from the soft tissue. It dictates how the crown looks and how healthy the peri-implant tissue stays.

Think of it as the transition zone from the narrow implant platform to the wider crown contour.

Why Angle Matters

Gradual Emergence (<30°)

Soft tissue can adapt and seal around the restoration. Easy for the patient to clean. Brush bristles can reach the sulcus. Low peri-implantitis risk.

Steep Emergence (>30°)

Creates a "cliff" that traps plaque. Brush bristles bounce off and can't reach the sulcus. Chronic food trapping. High peri-implantitis risk — like a house with no guttering.

The emergence angle is determined by two factors: (1) Implant depth — too shallow = steep emergence to reach crown width. (2) Implant position — too palatal = long labial overhang.

Designing the Ideal Emergence

The modern approach starts with the virtual crown position and works backwards to determine implant position:

Dr Hassan's rule: "If the screw access hole doesn't come out through the cingulum area of the crown, the implant is in the wrong position. Full stop."

Chapter 2

Shaping Soft Tissue With Provisionals

The emergence profile isn't just designed on a computer — it's physically sculpted with provisional restorations.

In the anterior zone, a provisional screw-retained crown is used to gradually shape the soft tissue over weeks before the final crown.

Week 1

Implant uncovered. Provisional placed with slightly narrow contour.

Weeks 2–4

Provisional modified — slightly widened to push tissue into ideal position.

Week 6

Tissue has adapted. Provisional shape matches ideal emergence.

Week 8+

Final impression captures the shaped tissue profile. Final crown replicates exactly.

Why not just use a healing abutment? A standard cylindrical healing abutment creates a round tissue opening. But teeth aren't round — they're often triangular or oval. The provisional sculpts the tissue into a tooth-like shape that the final crown replicates.

The Copy Technique

When the provisional has achieved the ideal soft tissue shape, take the final impression with the provisional in place. The lab creates the final crown with the exact same emergence profile as the shaped provisional.

This technique is especially important for single implants in the smile zone where tissue drape determines the aesthetic result.

Time investment: Yes, provisionals add 6–8 weeks. But the aesthetic result is dramatically better than placing a final crown on a round healing abutment and hoping the tissue adapts.

Chapter 3

Probing Around Implants

One of the most controversial topics in implant dentistry: should you probe around implants?

YES — You Should Probe

Modern consensus: Probing is essential for monitoring peri-implant health.

Why:

✓
Detects bleeding on probing (BOP) — first sign of mucositis
✓
Tracks pocket depth changes over time
✓
Identifies suppuration (pus) before bone loss
✓
Baseline at crown delivery → compare at maintenance visits

But — Probe Gently

Critical difference from teeth:

Natural teeth have a connective tissue seal that resists probing. Implants have a weaker hemidesmosomes-only attachment.

Maximum probing force: 0.25 N (about 25 grams). Standard probing force for teeth is 0.5 N. With implants, use HALF the force or you'll probe straight through the weak attachment.

Record baseline pocket depths on the day the crown is delivered. Then compare at every maintenance visit. It's the change that matters, not the absolute number.

Mucositis is REVERSIBLE. It's the gingivitis equivalent for implants. If you catch it here, you can prevent progression to peri-implantitis. This is why regular probing matters — it's your early warning system.

5. Why use less probing force around implants than teeth?

Correct! Implant soft tissue attachment is weaker than around natural teeth. The peri-implant seal relies on hemidesmosomes rather than the stronger connective tissue fibres. Use 0.25N, not the standard 0.5N.

Correct! Mucositis is reversible inflammation without bone loss. It's the implant equivalent of gingivitis. Catching it early through regular probing prevents progression to irreversible peri-implantitis.

Chapter 4

Peri-Implantitis — The Biggest Threat

Peri-implantitis is irreversible progressive bone loss around an integrated implant caused by bacterial infection of the peri-implant tissues.

22%
of implants affected by peri-implantitis
(Derks & Tomasi, systematic review)

Why It's So Difficult to Treat

Remember Micro-Roughness?

The SLA surface that gives implants their excellent integration has mountains, caves, and tunnels. When exposed to bacteria, these become impossible to decontaminate.

No Periodontal Ligament

Teeth have PDL fibres that provide a barrier and some regenerative potential. Implants have direct bone contact — once lost, it's gone.

Causes of Peri-Implantitis

1
Excess cement — foreign body response, chronic inflammation
2
Poor oral hygiene — bacterial biofilm accumulation
3
Steep emergence profile — uncleanable contours trap plaque
4
Smoking — impaired immune response, reduced vascularity
5
History of periodontitis — same bacteria, same risk profile
6
Occlusal overload — mechanical stress on bone
7
Uncontrolled diabetes — impaired healing cascade

Prevention is 10× easier than treatment. Every factor above is either preventable or manageable through patient selection, surgical technique, and maintenance.

Chapter 4

Peri-Implantitis Treatment Protocol

Treatment follows a staged approach — escalating from non-surgical to surgical based on severity:

Stage 1: Non-Surgical

1
Remove crown/abutment (if screw-retained)
2
Mechanical debridement with titanium curettes
3
Chemical decontamination (chlorhexidine, citric acid)
4
Systemic antibiotics if active infection
5
Intensive oral hygiene instruction
6
Review in 4–6 weeks

If improved: Continue maintenance. If not: Escalate to Stage 2.

Stage 2: Surgical

1
Raise a flap to access the defect
2
Remove all granulation tissue
3
Decontaminate implant surface (laser, air abrasion, chemical)
4
Bone graft if containable defect
5
Membrane if needed (GBR approach)
6
Close and wait 6+ months

Honest truth: Surgical treatment of peri-implantitis has a success rate of only ~50–60%. The micro-rough surface is extremely difficult to fully decontaminate. This is why prevention is paramount.

Chapter 4

The Surface Paradox

The same micro-roughness that makes modern implants integrate so well is exactly what makes peri-implantitis so difficult to treat.

When Submerged in Bone

The mountains, caves, and tunnels give osteoblasts massive surface area to attach. Integration is fast and strong. This is GOOD.

When Exposed to Oral Environment

Bacteria colonise the same caves and tunnels. They burrow deep inside where no cleaning instrument, laser, or chemical can reach. This is BAD.

"You can clean the outside of the mountain, but you'll never reach the bacteria hiding in the caves." This is why even surgical debridement with the most aggressive protocols only achieves ~50–60% resolution.

Implications

1
PREVENT exposure — maintain bone levels
2
DETECT early — regular probing and radiographs
3
ACT quickly — mucositis is reversible
4
DESIGN for cleanability — gentle emergence profiles
5
EDUCATE patients — they are the daily defence

Chapter 5

Occlusion — Mechanical Complications

Natural teeth have a periodontal ligament that absorbs forces and provides proprioception (the ability to sense bite forces). Implants have neither.

8–10×
More force needed for implant patients to sense contact
vs natural teeth (no proprioception)

Patients with implants bite harder without realising it. They can't feel when they're overloading the implant the way they could with natural teeth. This is why occlusal design is critical.

Occlusal Design Rules

1
Narrow the occlusal table — reduce the bucket size catching forces
2
Central fossa contacts only — forces directed axially through the implant
3
No cantilever contacts — forces should never lever off the implant
4
Exclude from lateral excursions — canine guidance on natural teeth if possible
5
Night guard for bruxers — protect the investment while sleeping

Dr Hassan's analogy: "Making the occlusal table wider catches more force, like holding a bigger bucket in the rain. Narrow the bucket — catch less force."

Chapter 5

Complication Rates — The Real Numbers

Honest discussion of complication rates builds trust with patients and prepares you mentally:

Restoration Type5-Year10-Year
Single implant crown~40%~50%
Small bridge (2–4 units)~40%~50%
Full-arch fixed~65%~90%

These are complication rates, not failure rates. Most complications are manageable: porcelain chips, screw loosening, soft tissue issues. True implant loss is far lower (~2–5% at 10 years for single implants).

Most Common Complications

Porcelain Chipping

Most common mechanical complication. Usually repairable intraorally with composite (if screw-retained and removable).

Screw Loosening

Second most common. Usually indicates occlusal overload or manufacturing tolerance issues. Re-torque to specification.

Soft Tissue Recession

Particularly in the aesthetic zone. Often due to thin phenotype, labial implant position, or cortical bone resorption. Prevention is key.

Quick Quiz — Occlusion & Complications

9. Why do implant patients bite harder without knowing?

Correct! Natural teeth have PDL fibres that sense pressure. Implants go directly into bone with no sensory feedback. Patients need 8–10× more force to sense contact on an implant vs a natural tooth.
Implants lack a periodontal ligament, so there's no proprioception. Patients can't sense when they're overloading. They need 8–10× more force to feel contact compared to natural teeth.

10. A 50% complication rate at 10 years means:

Correct! Complications ≠ failures. Most are minor and manageable: porcelain chips, screw loosening, soft tissue issues. True implant loss is only 2–5% at 10 years. The key is designing for retrievability.

Chapter 6

Patient Education & Pricing

What Patients Must Understand

Implants Need MORE Maintenance, Not Less

Patients often think implants are "fit and forget." The opposite is true. They require structured maintenance visits, specific cleaning tools, and regular monitoring.

Complications Will Happen

Educate about the 40% complication rate at 5 years. When patients understand this upfront, they come to YOU when something happens — not to a lawyer.

Lifestyle Changes

Smoking cessation. Diabetes control. Oral hygiene commitment. Night guard compliance. These aren't optional — they're part of the treatment.

Pricing Philosophy

"Never match your patient's financial urgency." If a patient pushes for the cheapest option or fastest result, don't compromise. The cost of failure is always higher than the cost of doing it right.

What Should Be Included in the Fee

• Initial consultation and CBCT planning
• Surgical placement and materials
• Healing abutments and provisionals
• Final restoration (crown/bridge)
• First year of maintenance visits
• Complications budget (at least 10% margin)

If you're pricing without a complications margin, every repair comes out of your pocket and your goodwill. Factor it in from the start.

Explaining Implants to Patients

"How long will my implant last?"

"The implant itself is titanium and won't decay. But the bone around it is living tissue that changes with your health. If you maintain excellent hygiene, attend regular check-ups, and your health stays good, implants can last decades. But I can't guarantee a specific number of years because I can't predict your future health."

"Why is it so expensive?"

"The implant component is actually a small part of the cost. What you're paying for is the planning (CBCT scans, digital design), the surgical expertise, the laboratory-made custom restoration, and the ongoing maintenance. Think of it like buying a car — the purchase price is one thing, but servicing and insurance are part of the deal."

"Will it hurt?"

"The surgery is done under local anaesthetic — you won't feel pain during the procedure. Afterwards, there's typically some swelling and discomfort for 3–5 days, manageable with standard painkillers. Most patients say it was better than they expected. The bone healing period of 12 weeks is painless."

"Can't you just do it faster?"

"Think of it like placing a fence post in concrete. If you push against it while the concrete is still setting, it'll always be wobbly. We need to let the bone 'set' around the implant — that takes about 12 weeks. Rushing this step is the most common reason implants fail."

Chapter 7

The Four Levels of Competence

Every clinician goes through these stages. Knowing where you are helps you make better decisions:

Level 1: Unconscious Incompetence

"You don't know what you don't know." You've never placed an implant. You don't understand the risks, the biology, or the complications. This is where everyone starts — and it's the most dangerous level because you can't assess your own risk.

Level 2: Conscious Incompetence

"You know what you don't know." You've taken a course. You understand the theory. You can see the complications and know you're not yet skilled enough to handle them all. This is uncomfortable but safe — because you know to ask for help.

Level 3: Conscious Competence

"You can do it, but you have to think about every step." You're placing implants successfully, but each case requires careful deliberation. You follow checklists. You plan meticulously. This is the productive phase where experience builds.

Level 4: Unconscious Competence

"You do it naturally." The steps are second nature. You anticipate problems before they happen. You have Plan B ready without thinking. You teach others. This takes years of focused practice and continuous learning.

Chapter 8

Evidence-Based Decision Making

Dr Hassan's approach: "If someone tells you something works, ask for the evidence. If there is none, it's an opinion — not a fact."

Hierarchy of Evidence

Why This Matters

Course presenters, company reps, and colleagues will all share techniques and products. Ask: "Show me the data." If they can't, it's an opinion — and opinions don't belong in someone's jawbone.

Red Flags in Claims

• "In my experience..." (anecdote, not evidence)
• "Everyone's using it" (popularity ≠ validity)
• "The rep told me..." (sales pitch, not science)
• "It worked for my last 5 patients" (case series, lowest evidence)

Problem-based education: When you encounter a clinical problem, search for evidence yourself. Read the paper, not the abstract. Look at the methodology. Check who funded the study. This is how you build genuine expertise.

Chapter 8

The Chosen Outcome

Dr Hassan's core philosophy: "The chosen outcome is the number one complication in implant dentistry."

Most implant failures aren't caused by bad luck. They're caused by a predictable outcome that the clinician chose — either through planning decisions or through ignorance.

Examples of chosen outcomes:

• Placing too wide an implant → cortical pressure → resorption
• Choosing cement-retained when screw was possible → peri-implantitis
• Not grafting a thin phenotype socket → massive bone loss
• Loading during weeks 1–5 → fibrous healing
• Using the cheapest implant system → component failure

Every Decision Has a Consequence

Position Determines Outcome

Where you place the implant determines the emergence profile, the force distribution, the aesthetic result, and the long-term prognosis. A millimetre matters.

Restoration Determines Maintenance

Screw-retained = maintainable. Cement-retained = risky. Steep emergence = uncleanable. Every prosthetic choice has a maintenance consequence.

Patient Selection Determines Risk

Uncontrolled diabetic + smoker + thin phenotype + poor hygiene = you haven't chosen a patient, you've chosen a complication.

The goal of this course: To help you see the outcome before it happens. Every slide, every principle, every rule is about changing your "chosen outcome" from complication to success.

Clinical Scenarios — Test Yourself

Click each scenario to reveal the recommended approach:

Scenario A: 45-year-old, thin phenotype, upper central incisor extraction needed

Approach: Socket graft on extraction day (convert high risk → low risk). Wait 4–5 months for graft integration. Place implant palatally with stability from apical cancellous bone. Gap graft labially. Heal 12 weeks. Provisional to sculpt tissue 6–8 weeks. Final screw-retained crown with <30° emergence.

Scenario B: Diabetic patient, HbA1c 8.5%, needs lower molar implant

Approach: Refer to GP first to optimise diabetes control. Aim for HbA1c <7.5% before surgery. Once controlled: standard placement, extended healing (24–36 weeks minimum). Monitor ISQ at insertion and before loading. Screw-retained molar crown with narrow occlusal table.

Scenario C: Implant placed, torque reads only 12 Ncm

Approach: Below 15 Ncm = rocking = guaranteed failure. Remove immediately. Place wider implant (Plan B) into the same osteotomy without further drilling. The existing hole becomes the undersized preparation. Check torque again. If still <15 Ncm with Plan B, remove and allow healing.

Scenario D: Crown delivered 2 years ago, now BOP with 6mm probing depth

Approach: Take radiograph — compare to baseline. If bone loss visible: peri-implantitis. If no bone loss: mucositis. For mucositis: intensive hygiene, chlorhexidine, review 4–6 weeks. If screw-retained: remove crown, clean implant surface, modify emergence if steep. If cemented: investigate for excess cement.

Scenario E: Patient aged 19 wants implant for missing upper lateral incisor

Approach: Too young. Facial growth likely not complete. Explain that an implant is ankylosed and won't move with the rest of the teeth. Provide temporary bonded restoration (Maryland bridge or removable). Reassess at 21+ with facial growth evaluation. If placing at 21–25, consent explicitly for potential facial growth discrepancy.

Scenario F: 70-year-old with osteoporosis on alendronic acid

Approach: Risk assessment: how long on bisphosphonates? Oral bisphosphonates <3 years = manageable risk. Liaise with GP/specialist. If proceeding: extended healing (24+ weeks), gentle surgery, minimise trauma. Antibiotic cover. Consent for MRONJ risk. Some clinicians request a drug holiday — discuss with prescriber.
