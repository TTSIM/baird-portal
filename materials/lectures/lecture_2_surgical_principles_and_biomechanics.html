Chapter 1

The Press-Fit Effect

Press-fit is the controlled compression of cancellous bone when an implant slightly wider than the osteotomy is placed. This micro-damage is not a complication — it's the trigger for osteointegration.

No press-fit = no osteointegration. Without controlled pressure on cancellous bone, the body defaults to fibrous healing instead of bony healing. The implant becomes encapsulated in scar tissue.

The critical distinction:

✓
Press-fit on cancellous bone → osteointegration
✗
Press-fit on cortical bone → micro-cracks → resorption

Chapter 2

Osteotomy Sizing — Why 3.5 for a 4.0 Implant?

You're placing a 4.0 mm diameter implant. What diameter osteotomy do you drill?

4.5 mm — TOO WIDE ✗

Implant rocks in the hole. No compression. No press-fit. Mobility stimulates fibroblasts.

Result: Fibrous healing guaranteed. Like a broken jaw left untreated — motion = scar tissue.

4.0 mm — TOO PASSIVE ✗

Implant sits passively with no compression on cancellous bone. Just a passive touch.

Result: No osteocyte stimulation. No biological cascade. Weak or no osteointegration.

3.5 mm — CORRECT ✓

0.5 mm compression on each side. Press-fit on cancellous bone. Osteocytes activated.

Result: Biological cascade triggered. Osteoblast activity. Osteointegration.

Posterior maxilla exception: In very soft bone (type IV), drill only 3.0 mm for a 4.0 mm implant. Do not use the profile drill. More compression needed because the cancellous bone is less dense.

Chapter 2

Why Bone Healing Requires Stability

The fundamental principle: osteoblasts need stability, fibroblasts thrive on motion.

Fracture Analogy

When someone breaks their arm → cast. Broken jaw → mini-plate. Why? Because bone healing requires immobilisation.

Untreated Fracture

If a fractured jaw isn't stabilised within days, fibrous healing occurs. After 2 weeks untreated, the fracture site fills with scar tissue instead of bone — mobility stimulated fibroblasts.

Same principle for implants: If micro-motion exceeds 150 micrometres, fibroblasts are stimulated instead of osteoblasts. The threshold is absolute.

150 μm
Maximum micro-motion threshold
Above this → fibrous healing guaranteed
50–150 μm
Acceptable micro-motion range
Below this → osteointegration possible

This is why a 4.5 mm osteotomy for a 4.0 mm implant fails: The implant rocks, creating motion >150 μm, and fibroblasts take over. Conversely, a 3.5 mm osteotomy creates compression (not motion) — which stimulates osteocytes, not fibroblasts.

The Radar

Embedded in mature bone. Detect mechanical forces (pressure from press-fit). Signal the brain to dispatch osteoclasts. Found primarily in cancellous bone — which is why press-fit on cancellous triggers the response.

🧹 Osteoclasts

The Cleaners

Remove any bone under abnormal circumstances: crushed, infected, or compressed. They are non-selective within their target zone. After cleaning, they signal stem cells to differentiate into osteoblasts (in healthy patients).

The Builders

Derived from mesenchymal stem cells. Deposit osteoid matrix (collagen + growth factors), then mineralise it. First produce woven bone (random, weak), which is later remodelled into lamellar bone (organised, strong).

The Unwanted Alternative

When stem cells receive incorrect signals (from disease, infection, or excess motion), they become fibroblasts instead of osteoblasts. Fibroblasts produce granulation/scar tissue — not bone. This means implant failure.

Chapter 3

From Woven Bone to Lamellar Bone

After the osteoblasts fill the gap with new bone, the process doesn't stop. The first bone deposited is woven bone — random, weak structure. Through remodelling, this converts to lamellar bone.

Woven Bone

Random collagen fibre orientation. Weak but forms quickly. Provides initial biological stability. Present from weeks 2–8 approximately.

Lamellar Bone

Organised collagen fibres perpendicular to implant surface. Strong, mature bone. The fibres are aligned perpendicular to the implant — increasing stability. Full maturation at 12–24 weeks.

Remodelling Cycle

1
Osteoblasts deposit osteoid matrix (collagen + growth factors)
2
Calcium phosphate minerals deposited → woven bone
3
Osteoblasts mature into osteocytes
4
Osteoclasts reactivate → remove weak woven bone
5
Osteoblasts deposit organised lamellar bone
6
Bone structure perpendicular to implant → maximum strength

This is why patience matters. At 4 weeks, you have weak woven bone. At 12+ weeks, you have strong lamellar bone. Loading too early interrupts this remodelling process.

Chapter 4

Torque — Measuring Rotational Stability

Torque measures the resistance to rotation (spinning) as the implant is placed into bone. It's measured in Newton centimetres (Ncm).

Two Measurement Methods

Motor Sensors (Easy)

The implant handpiece has internal sensors that display real-time torque on the motor screen. Downside: Accuracy degrades without annual calibration — which most people don't do.

Manual Torque Wrench (Accurate)

Physical wrench with numbered increments. Test at 30 — if implant doesn't move, torque is >30. Test at 35 — if it moves, torque is between 30–35. Gold standard for accuracy.

15 Ncm
MINIMUM implant torque threshold
Below this → implant is rocking → must act

Below 15 Ncm: The implant is rocking and will fail. Options: (1) Remove and place a wider implant. (2) Remove and allow healing before trying again. This is why you always have a Plan B implant ready.

Is there a maximum torque? The old theory of "pressure necrosis" above 50 Ncm is no longer accepted. What matters is where the pressure goes. 100 Ncm on cancellous bone = fine. 30 Ncm on cortical bone = resorption.

Chapter 4

Pressure Necrosis — Debunked

Old teaching said that torque above 50 Ncm causes pressure necrosis — bone death from excessive compression. This theory is no longer accepted.

Modern understanding: It's not about HOW MUCH pressure, it's about WHERE the pressure is applied. The same force has completely different effects on cortical vs cancellous bone.

ScenarioTorqueResult
30 Ncm on cortical bone30Micro-cracks → resorption
50 Ncm on cortical bone50More cracks → faster resorption
50 Ncm on cancellous bone50Press-fit → osteointegration
100 Ncm on cancellous bone100Strong press-fit → excellent integration

Most implant systems recommend not exceeding 70 Ncm — not because of bone necrosis, but because the implant or its components may break at higher forces.

Chapter 5

ISQ — Implant Stability Quotient

Torque measures rotational resistance. ISQ measures lateral movement using magnetic resonance technology.

A proper assessment of mechanical stability needs both measurements.

How ISQ Works

A magnetic transducer is attached to the implant. The device sends a signal and measures the resonance frequency. Higher frequency = less lateral movement = higher ISQ value.

ISQ Scale

Measured on a scale from 1 to 100. Values below ~60 suggest poor stability. Values above 65–70 indicate good integration. The key is the trend over time, not a single measurement.

The Ideal Protocol

1
Measure ISQ day of insertion (baseline)
2
Measure ISQ at 12 weeks (review)
3
If ISQ improved → implant integrated ✓
4
If ISQ same or decreased → integration failing ✗

If you can only choose one: Prefer torque. If torque is high, ISQ is usually high too. But ideally, check both. Many clinicians only check torque, which can miss lateral instability.

Don't check mid-healing! ISQ at weeks 2–4 will show a dip (mechanical stability decreasing, biological not yet established). This is normal — not a sign of failure.

Chapter 6

Understanding the Stability Dip

The stability curve reveals the most vulnerable period for any dental implant:

Mechanical Stability (Declining)

The initial grip from press-fit compression. Declines as osteoclasts remove the compressed bone — this is normal and necessary. It means the biological cascade is working.

Biological Stability (Rising)

New bone forming around the implant. Starts near zero, gradually increases as osteoblasts deposit woven bone, then lamellar bone. Takes 8–12+ weeks to reach full strength.

Total Stability (The U-Curve)

The sum of both. Dips during weeks 1–5 when mechanical is declining and biological hasn't yet compensated. This is when loading is most dangerous.

The Danger Zone (Weeks 1–5): Mechanical stability is declining rapidly. Biological stability is still minimal. Total stability is at its lowest. Do NOT load the implant during this period. It's like loading a post in soft concrete — it will move.

ISQ during the dip: If you measure ISQ at weeks 2–3, it will be lower than day 1. This is expected and normal. It does NOT mean failure — it means the biological process is working. Only worry if ISQ hasn't recovered by week 12.

Wk 1–5
Never load during this period
Micro-motion > 150μm = fibroblasts win

Quick Quiz — The Stability Curve

5. Why does ISQ drop at weeks 2–4?

Correct! The dip is expected. Osteoclasts are removing the press-fit compressed bone (reducing mechanical stability) while osteoblasts haven't yet deposited enough new bone (biological stability still low).
The ISQ dip at weeks 2–4 is normal. It reflects the transition from mechanical to biological stability. Osteoclasts are removing compressed bone — which is exactly what should happen.

6. When is the implant most vulnerable to loading failure?

Correct! Weeks 1–5 is the danger zone. Mechanical stability is declining, biological stability is minimal. Loading during this period creates micro-motion that stimulates fibroblasts.
The danger zone is weeks 1–5. At day 1, mechanical stability is at its peak. By weeks 8–12, biological stability has largely compensated. But weeks 1–5 is where both are compromised.

Chapter 7

Clinical Analogy — Concrete Post

Dr Hassan's analogy for patients and students:

The Concrete Post

Imagine placing a fence post in wet concrete. At the moment of insertion, the post feels stable — the wet concrete provides some initial grip (mechanical stability).

Now, if you push against the post while the concrete is still setting (weeks 1–5), it moves. The concrete never sets properly around it. The post will always be wobbly.

But if you leave it alone until the concrete fully sets (12 weeks), it's rock solid. The concrete has cured and bonded to the post.

This is exactly what happens with implants:

1
Day 0: Implant placed with press-fit (wet concrete grip)
2
Weeks 1–5: Bone remodelling (concrete setting — don't touch!)
3
Weeks 6–12: New bone hardening around implant
4
Week 12+: Lamellar bone — safe to load

Patient explanation: "We need to wait for the bone to cure around your implant, just like concrete needs time to set. If we rush it, the implant won't bond properly and we'll have to start over."

Chapter 8

Implant Torque vs Screw Torque

Critical distinction: Do NOT confuse these two. They are completely different measurements for different purposes.

Implant Torque (Variable)

Force measured when placing the implant into bone.

Depends on: Bone density, implant design, osteotomy size, bone type.

Minimum: 15 Ncm. No maximum (as long as from cancellous bone).

What it tells you: How much primary mechanical stability you've achieved.

Screw Torque (Fixed per System)

Force used to tighten the abutment screw into the implant.

Prescribed by manufacturer: e.g. Megagen = 35 Ncm, Astra = 20–25 Ncm, Nobel = 32 Ncm.

Must follow exactly. Under-torque = loosening. Over-torque = screw fracture or stripped hex.

Removal torque is always higher than insertion torque.

Chapter 8

Screw Torque — Dangers of Getting It Wrong

Under-Torque Problems

!
Screw rocks when patient bites
!
Gap forms → bacteria leak into connection
!
Mucosa traumatised by rocking
!
Screw eventually fractures from fatigue
!
Broken screw = nightmare to remove

Finger tightening alone gives approximately 15 Ncm — not enough for any system. Always use the torque wrench.

Over-Torque Problems

!
Screw may fracture immediately
!
Hex head may strip → becomes circular
!
Screwdriver won't grip → can't remove
!
Removal torque > insertion torque

Example: Megagen recommends 35 Ncm. If you over-torque to 45, you need ~50+ Ncm to remove it. The screw is designed to break around 50. You've created a ticking time bomb.

Knowledge Check

Quick Quiz — Screw Torque

7. Megagen recommends 35 Ncm screw torque. Why not just finger-tighten?

Correct! Human finger strength achieves approximately 15 Ncm regardless of fitness. The torque wrench is a force multiplier — it's the only way to reach the manufacturer's specified torque.
Finger tightening only achieves ~15 Ncm, regardless of hand strength. Megagen needs 35 Ncm. The torque wrench is essential, not optional.

8. Why is removal torque higher than insertion torque?

Correct! The screw settles into the connection under load, and friction increases. If you torque at 35 Ncm, you typically need ~40 Ncm to remove. Over-torquing compounds this problem.
Friction and settling under load increase the removal force. Torque at 35 → need ~40 to remove. Over-torque to 45 → need ~50+ to remove → risk of fracture.

Chapter 9

Plan B Philosophy

Every case should have a Plan A and a Plan B. This applies to implant size, position, and even the restorative approach.

"Unbreakable doesn't exist. Unrepairable is what you and I should always avoid."
— Gallucci

The most common Plan B scenario: your implant doesn't achieve sufficient torque.

Typical Plan B Example

Plan A: 4.0 × 10 mm implant
Plan B: 4.5 × 10 mm implant

If the 4.0 achieves <15 Ncm, remove it. Place the 4.5 without further drilling — the existing osteotomy becomes the undersized preparation for the wider implant.

Why It Matters

Scenario without Plan B: You place your widest implant first. It rocks. You remove it. Now what? Tell the patient "sorry, can't do it today, come back in 4 months"? Extremely embarrassing and destroys patient confidence.

Plan B Principles

1
Never use your widest implant as Plan A
2
Keep a wider implant as the emergency option
3
Consider whether cemented could work if screw-retained fails
4
Always have bone graft material available
5
Think about retrievability at every stage

Chapter 9

Choosing an Implant System

Dr Hassan's experience: free implants from a Turkish company → fractured after 2 years → costly to fix → paid double the trip from his own pocket.

The lesson: If you think you're saving £50 per implant on a cheaper system, you'll pay triple when things go wrong. We're investing in a patient's human body. You have one shot.

What to Look For

1
Track record: At least 5+ years of published follow-up data
2
Connection design: Tight seal that minimises bacterial leakage
3
Coronal design: Minimal or no threads at the crestal zone
4
Support: Responsive customer service, representative visits, proper training

All modern premium implant systems will integrate — that's no longer the differentiator. What separates good from bad is maintaining bone level long-term — and that comes down to connection design, surface treatment, and coronal geometry.

Red Flags

• "Free trips" in exchange for using their system
• No published long-term data
• Poor or non-existent customer support
• Sales team focused on price, not outcomes
• "They do break — don't worry, we'll send a new one"

Chapter 10

Golden Rules of Implant Position

Every position error creates a specific clinical problem:

ErrorConsequence
Too labialCortical plate pressure → mid-labial recession → metal showing
Too lingualBulky cingulum → phonetic problems → food trapping
Too mesial/distalDisrupted emergence profile → porcelain chipping → papilla damage
Too shallowSteep emergence angle → peri-implantitis risk → poor aesthetics
Too deepDifficult restoration access → but rarely the biggest problem

The Evolution of Position Philosophy

Old School (1990s)

"Follow the bone" — place the implant wherever there IS bone. Get stability, don't worry about restoration.

Better (2000s–2010s)

"Follow the prosthetic envelope" — position the implant where the crown should be. Build bone if needed.

Current Best Practice

"Follow the ideal emergence profile" — start with the virtual crown, design the perfect emergence profile, THEN decide implant position. Build everything else around it.
