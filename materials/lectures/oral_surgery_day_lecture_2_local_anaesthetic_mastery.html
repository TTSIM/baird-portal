Why LA Matters So Much

Local anaesthetic in your hands is a very important part of gaining the confidence for your patient. If you're not good at your local anaesthetic, pack up and go home.

— Shankar
🎯

Patient Confidence

A painless injection is the first step to a trusting relationship. Get this wrong and everything else fails.

⏱️

Efficiency

Good LA = comfortable patient = you can work properly without fighting them.

💉

Sedation ≠ LA

They're separate processes. Sedation doesn't replace LA. If someone says "LA failed, so I'll sedate" — they don't understand either.

The Articaine Revolution

"Chuck all your guns out.
Get your articaine.
Use it for everything."

Why Articaine Wins

  • Higher tissue affinity — penetrates bone better than lignocaine
  • More potent — 4% concentration vs 2% lignocaine
  • Buccal infiltration replaces ID blocks in many cases
  • Faster onset for infiltrations

Shankar's Practice

  • Uses only articaine — no lignocaine at all
  • Uses it for ID blocks (9 years, no issues)
  • Buccal + lingual infiltrations for posterior teeth
  • Periodontal ligament injections with articaine
  • No more ID blocks for many posterior cases

The Evidence: Articaine + ID Block

Practical takeaway: If you won't use articaine for ID blocks, that's fine. But always give articaine buccal (and lingual) infiltration as well. This alone makes the biggest difference.

The Adrenaline Myth

❌ Common Myths

  • "Don't use adrenaline with hypertensive patients"
  • "It will spike their blood pressure dangerously"
  • "Patients are allergic to adrenaline"
  • "I had palpitations = adrenaline allergy"

✅ The Reality

  • Endogenous adrenaline from stress = ~300× what you inject
  • Tachycardia comes from intravascular injection, not the drug itself
  • Patient's BP is already up from attending the appointment
  • A painful extraction without good LA causes MORE adrenaline release
Shankar uses adrenaline on cardiac patients: At Queen Elizabeth Hospital near Papworth (cardiac centre), all cardiac patients awaiting valve replacement get dental assessments. "We still use articaine. If you don't give it intravascularly, there is no problem."

Nerve Supply — Know Your Blocks

Mental Nerve Block

Technique

Locate: Between the premolars, stretch the sulcus with a finger
Angle: Almost vertical, aim past the apex of the premolars
Depth: Approximately two-thirds of the root length beyond the crown
Deposit: One cartridge, then massage the area to help it dissipate and spread along the nerve

What It Gives You

  • Labial tissues from premolars forward
  • Useful for lip procedures (SCC, BCC)
  • Bilateral mental blocks work well for lower anterior work
  • Simple, effective, easy to learn
Don't worry about hitting bone or the nerve — the mental nerve faces backwards before exiting, and the foramen is well below the apices in normal mandibles.

Akinosi Technique — The Rescue Block

★ GO TRY THIS MONDAY MORNING

Step-by-Step

Patient bites together — closed mouth technique. Works even with limited opening!
Find the mucogingival junction of the upper molar teeth
Retract with mirror or finger — expose the injection site
Syringe parallel to occlusal plane — needle goes into the space between posterior maxilla and mandible
Deposit 2ml of local anaesthetic into this space

Key Points

  • No bony landmark — unlike ID block, you don't hit bone (this feels strange at first)
  • Catches ID nerve at a higher level
  • Gives you: ID + lingual + long buccal + mental — one injection, everything
  • May take slightly longer to onset
  • Works for patients with limited mouth opening (unlike Gow-Gates)
  • As effective as Gow-Gates but much simpler

Gow-Gates vs Akinosi

Gow-Gates

  • Requires: Good mouth opening
  • Target: Palatal cusp of upper 7, aim upward
  • Landmark: Hit bone = condylar neck
  • Catches: Mandibular nerve before division
  • Approach: From opposite side
  • Difficulty: Harder to perform
  • Shankar: "I find it harder to do"

✅ Akinosi (Preferred)

  • Requires: Teeth in occlusion (closed mouth!)
  • Target: Mucogingival junction of upper molars
  • Landmark: No bony endpoint
  • Catches: Same nerves — ID + lingual + buccal
  • Approach: Parallel to occlusal plane
  • Difficulty: Simple and reliable
  • Shankar: "Go try it Monday morning"

Diagnostic Power

🔎

Pain Source Elimination

95% of dental/facial pain is diagnosable. For the 5% that's not:

  • Give PDL injection to the suspected tooth
  • If numb but pain persists → pain is NOT from that tooth
  • Prove it to the patient: "This is not a tooth problem"
  • Move them on: refer to neurologist or pain specialist

Why ID Blocks Fail

❌ Too Lateral

→ Into parotid gland → temporary facial palsy (can't close eye on that side)

❌ Too Lateral (muscle)

→ Into masseter muscle → post-operative trismus lasting weeks

⚠️ Too Anterior

→ Hit oblique ridge → just change angulation, go more inwards

⚠️ Too Medial

→ Into medial pterygoid → Shankar: "Hard to do — the tongue is in the way"

Modern understanding of failure: Tissue acidosis theory is outdated. The current theory involves TTX-resistant sodium channels in A-delta and C fibres. These respond to pre-emptive ibuprofen 600mg given 30–45 minutes before the procedure.

Pre-Emptive Analgesia

The concept of pre-emptive analgesia is used by anaesthetists. Somehow, even though we deal with pain every day, as dentists we haven't grabbed onto that concept.

— Shankar

Ibuprofen 600mg

30–45 minutes before procedure

Blocks prostaglandin E2 → blocks TTX-R sodium channels

Combined protocol for difficult cases: Ibuprofen 600mg beforehand + Lignocaine ID block + Articaine buccal/lingual infiltration = handles the vast majority of cases. If still struggling, add Akinosi or intrapulpal drops.

Intrapulpal — The Wisdom Tooth Trick

📍 The Scenario

You're splitting a wisdom tooth. LA is working, but as you get close to the pulp with your bur, the patient starts jumping. You have analgesia but not surgical anaesthesia.

Stop drilling — don't push through the pain
Drop a few drops of LA directly into the split/cut you've made, close to the exposed pulp
Wait one minute
Resume drilling — the tooth goes numb and you complete the section
Same principle as intrapulpal in endodontics. Works especially well when the pulp chamber is severely decayed and difficult to anaesthetise through conventional blocks.

Safety Plus Syringe

Why Shankar Switched

  • Transparency: Blood flashback is visible through the plastic — metal syringes hide it behind a ledge
  • Aspiration: Has a ring to pull back and actively aspirate — better than relying on self-aspiration
  • Safety: One team had 6 needle-stick injuries in 4 years, all from metal syringe disassembly

The Catch

  • Designed for single use per cartridge
  • Reusing for multiple cartridges (NHS reality): the plastic lock sleeve can fatigue
  • Under pressure, the needle bends and LA sprays everywhere
  • "That's the company making sure you don't use it more than once"
One needle policy: Shankar uses only one needle — the long (yellow) one — for everything. No switching between short and long. "There's only one needle to use."

LA Complications

Click to reveal management

Temporary facial palsy from upper infiltrations Rare▼
Shankar has had this twice — articaine for upper teeth caught the facial nerve. Likely from using a long needle at a higher level. Patient can't close their eye on one side. Resolves in 2–3 hours. Reassure the patient.
Palatal / injection site necrosis Uncommon▼
White dead tissue at injection site. Looks worse than an ulcer. Possibly related to concentration (4% articaine) or vascular ischaemia from the vasoconstrictor. Heals over, but initially alarming. No specific treatment needed — monitor.
Post-operative trismus Uncommon▼
From irritating the masseter (too lateral ID block) or haematoma from upper 8 extraction (loose pterygoid plexus). Takes 4–8 weeks of mouth exercises to resolve. Very frustrating for patients.
Temporary visual disturbance Very rare▼
LA reaching the pterygoid venous plexus → vasospasm of vessels supplying the optic nerve → temporary blindness. Lasts 2–3 hours. Recorded cases exist. "Scary for patients, even scarier for us."
Prolonged numbness (weeks–months) Rare▼
"Every 2–3 years we get one case we don't understand." One colleague's patient was numb for 2 months after a simple premolar filling, then it resolved suddenly. No explanation found. "Accept that sometimes things happen. It shouldn't stop you from practising."

LA Allergy — Rare but Real

Explore the history: "What happened?" — Most are vasovagal, anxiety, or intravascular injection. Not allergy.
Respect the claim: If there's any chance it's genuine, stop. Don't proceed.
Refer to immunologist: Pinprick tests of various LA agents. May take 2–3 years through NHS.
Meanwhile: General anaesthetic for urgent procedures. No local of any kind.
Get it in writing: Before you touch the patient with any LA, have the immunologist's report on paper.
Remember: Most dental LA agents are amides. Topical LA agents are esters. We don't have ester injectable alternatives for dental work in the UK. True amide allergy is exceptionally rare — but physicians routinely give test doses (we don't, but they do).

Bupivacaine — The Long Player

⏰

Duration: 6–8 hours

Patients leave hospital still comfortable. Excellent for post-surgical pain control. The "send them home smiling" drug.

💉

Not in Cartridges

Comes in vials — must be drawn up with a syringe. Available in some dental practices.

🏥

Shankar's Use

Given at the end of every GA case. Not used for children or patients with learning difficulties (lip-biting risk for extended period).

📊

Same Max Dose

7mg/kg with adrenaline — same as lignocaine and articaine. Easy to remember: one number for all.

QuickSleeper — The Game Changer

— Shankar on trying QuickSleeper

What It Is

  • Makes a "funny noise" when it hits — minimal pain
  • Instantaneous anaesthesia
  • Can give buccally and lingually

Practical Notes

  • Bulky handpiece — lingual access can be tricky

Intraosseous Injections

Advantages

  • Very effective — direct delivery to bone
  • Fast onset
  • Useful when conventional methods fail
  • QuickSleeper makes it painless

Concerns

  • Older methods (Stabident) — painful bone puncture
  • Hard to find the pre-made hole with the LA needle
  • Risk of localised osteonecrosis
  • These two factors explain why intraosseous never fully took off

Sedation for Implant Dentistry

Why Get Sedation Trained?

If you're going down the implant route, Shankar strongly encourages getting sedation training. It's hard to get (20 mentored cases needed), but it's very valuable for your implant journey.

What Sedation Does

  • Calms the anxious patient
  • Helps them accept uncomfortable procedures
  • Midazolam = "like having a lollipop" for anaesthetists
  • Works well for anxious patients

What Sedation Does NOT Do

  • Replace local anaesthetic — ever
  • Make the tooth numb
  • Work reliably for true dental phobics
  • "Deep sedation so they don't need LA" = you're giving a GA. You'll kill the patient.

Shankar's LA Protocol

Upper Teeth

Articaine buccal infiltration only. Most times no palatal injection needed. The articaine penetrates through to the palatal side.

Simple

Lower Anteriors

Articaine buccal + lingual infiltrations. Bilateral mental blocks work well. No ID block needed.

No ID block

Lower Posteriors

Moving towards: articaine buccal + lingual + PDL only. Alternatively: lignocaine ID block + articaine buccal infiltration (3.6× more effective).

Transitioning

When It Fails

Akinosi technique first. If still failing: intrapulpal drops during sectioning. Pre-emptive ibuprofen 600mg for predicted difficult cases.

Rescue
Always: Adrenaline. One needle (long/yellow). Safety Plus syringe. Aspirate before depositing.

Managing Gagging

Upper Teeth

  • Gaggers are more problematic when working on uppers
  • Palatal injection actually helps with gagging — the local suppresses the gag reflex
  • Topical spray can help but often makes it worse — the taste is horrible and it drips down the throat, irritating them more

Lower Teeth

  • Less problematic — you don't need to put your hands deep into the mouth
  • Keep instruments to a minimum in the mouth
  • Work around it — quick, efficient movements
  • Tongue size matters — supine position makes large tongues flow everywhere
Tongue management: A Lack's tongue depressor helps control the tongue in supine patients, especially those with high BMI. Not pleasant but necessary for visibility and safety.

The "Pamper Your Patient" Protocol

Ibuprofen 600mg — 30–45 minutes before the appointment. Pre-emptive analgesia.
Topical LA gel — apply to the injection site before needling. Give it time to work.
Articaine with adrenaline — your workhorse. Buccal infiltration as standard.
Aspirate — always. Use Safety Plus for visibility. No intravascular injection.
Wait for onset — Shankar gives the LA first, then goes to scrub up. By the time he's back, it's working.
Bupivacaine at the end — for post-surgical comfort (hospital setting). Patient goes home still comfortable.

Lecture 2 — Key Takeaways

Articaine is king. Higher penetration, more potent. Use it for everything. Buccal infiltration = biggest impact.
ID blocks may become obsolete. Articaine buccal + lingual + PDL is increasingly replacing them for posteriors.
Learn Akinosi. Closed-mouth technique, no bony landmark, catches everything. Your rescue block when ID fails.
7mg/kg. One number for all agents with adrenaline. Know your cartridge concentrations.
Pre-emptive ibuprofen 600mg. Blocks TTX-R sodium channels. Give 30–45 minutes before.
Adrenaline fears are overblown. Endogenous release from pain is 300× worse. Aspirate properly.
Sedation ≠ LA. They're complementary, not interchangeable. Get sedation trained for your implant career.
