Shankar's Surgical Philosophy

If you've got some red stuff coming, it's good — the patient is alive. Bleeding is good, bleeding can be controlled. All bleeding will eventually stop.

— Shankar on overcoming fear of surgery

✅ Surgery is learnable

"If I can do it, all of you can do it. Surgery is simple — there are basic steps you have to learn."

⚠️ Stay humble

"If you get cocky, surgery will teach you well. Keep to the basics all the time."

🔁 Practice is everything

"I did 36 years of oral surgery. I got good only because I do only this — the same things over and over again."

The Three C's of Good Practice

Key insight: "Even if you're not competent, if you have the other two — caring and compassion — the number of complaints you get will be less." Communication skill helps overcome complications.

Assessment is Everything

If you fail in your assessment, you will fail in your treatment.

— The central message of this course
Observe the patient — gait, anxiety, mouth opening, tongue size, BMI. Start gathering information before you touch anything.
Examine the tooth — colour (darker = more carious = more fragile), crown integrity, adjacent restorations, periapical status.
Read the radiograph — root morphology, curvature direction, proximity to vital structures, bone levels, periapical pathology.
Predict the difficulty — which roots will break? Will a flap be needed? What instruments will you require?
Set expectations — tell the patient frankly what to expect. There are no magicians. Build them up before you begin.

Managing Patient Expectations

✅ Do Tell Patients

  • "This tooth may break during extraction — that's normal and we'll manage it"
  • "You will have some swelling and bruising afterwards"
  • "Adjacent fillings or crowns could be affected"
  • "Pain afterwards is part of the healing process"
  • "This is not a simple procedure — I'm going to take my time to do it right"

❌ Don't Accept

  • "My last dentist did it much better"
  • "It should be simple" — nothing is simple about any extraction
  • "Can you just pull it out quickly?"
  • Being pressured by time — if it takes 30 minutes, so be it
  • Rudeness — be firm, polite, but give it back if needed
Shankar's rule: "One thing about patients — just because they are a patient does not mean you should take all the grief they give you. Be firm. Be polite. But give it back to them."

The Golden Rule

"The tooth is yours.
The bone is the patient's."

What this means in practice

  • Section the tooth freely — use your drill, split the crown, break it into pieces
  • Preserve every millimetre of bone — especially labial and interradicular bone
  • For implant patients: bone preservation is the single most important goal

Why this matters now

  • Traditional "alveolar purchase" technique (fracturing buccal bone to extract) is obsolete in implant era
  • Every extraction you do is a potential future implant site
  • Your extraction today determines what's possible tomorrow

Force Direction — The Critical Concept

Apical pressure: Always push forceps towards the apex. Shankar's left thumb constantly pushes the forceps deeper. Never grip at the CEJ — go down the root.

Root Splitting — Direction Matters

Minimum cuts: You don't need to split all the way through. As long as you get a space to insert an instrument and it cracks — that fracture line will propagate. Do the minimum needed.

Bur Technique — See What You're Doing

My bur only goes to the point that I can see. If I can't see any more, I will stop. I don't work blindly.

— Shankar's golden rule for bur use

🔧 Instrument of Choice

Tapered fissure bur on a straight handpiece (not high speed — risk of surgical emphysema). The width of the fissure bur corresponds roughly to a Coupland's 1.

📏 How Far to Go

Only as far as you can see the working tip. Never sweep blindly. Your hand will naturally create a slightly wider cut than the bur — that's good, it gives room for your elevator.

🦴 Preserve Substance

Keep as much root substance as possible. You can put more pressure on a larger fragment. Minimum cut = maximum remaining structure to work with.

Implant-Minded Extractions

Your extractions are fundamentally different from hospital extractions. You're preserving for the future.

Preserve labial bone at all costs — this is the foundation for implant success. Without labial bone + soft tissue + implant, the system fails.
Preserve interradicular bone — it looks nice when intact post-extraction. Important for socket healing and future implant placement.
Manage soft tissue carefully — no mucosal tears, clean papillae, minimal tissue damage. Look at your extraction site — it should look beautiful.
Assess adjacent teeth — don't put an implant next to a failing tooth. A dodgy 7 beside your implant 6 will take the implant with it when it fails.
Consider biotype — thick biotype heals better, maintains bone, and gives better aesthetics. Thin biotype loses labial plate and height.

Biotype Comparison

Thick Biotype

  • Can't see probe through mucosa
  • Better bony form maintained
  • Superior crown aesthetics for upper anteriors
  • Less resorption post-extraction
  • Maintains labial plate height
  • More forgiving for implant placement

Thin Biotype

  • Almost transparent — probe visible underneath
  • Not much bone present
  • Labial plate resorbs quickly
  • Vertical bone loss occurs
  • Higher risk of peri-implantitis
  • Needs GBR / ridge augmentation
Timing matters: If you wait too long after extraction, you lose bone. Immediate implant, early implant, and delayed implant all have different advantages and restrictions. Get the timing right for your technique.

Post Crowns — The Implant Pipeline

💰 Good news for implant dentists

All the post crowns from the 1980s and 1990s are coming to you. Post crowns cause vertical root fractures that are invisible on radiographs. There's bone loss to the fracture point, and no labial bone left for you.

⏰ Don't Wait

If a patient has a failing post crown and you're planning an implant — don't sit on it. The faster you remove it, the more bone will be preserved. The longer you wait, the more bone is lost.

🔍 What to Look For

Vertical fracture lines (often invisible on X-ray), bone loss tracking down the fracture, and loss of labial plate. These teeth need removing before all the bone is gone.

Restorative-First Philosophy

Surgical skills are probably the lower end of skills that you need. Get your restorative skills going — surgical skills will follow.

— Shankar's advice to implant trainees

Why restorative matters more

  • Prosthetic envelope determines implant success — put it where the tooth needs to be, not where the bone is
  • Surgery is getting easier — companies are investing, materials are improving, guided surgery is advancing
  • Ideally, do both — place the implant and restore it yourself. Few teams do split surgery/prosthetics well

The old mistake

  • Maxillofacial surgeons placed implants where bone was, not where the tooth needed to be
  • They'd never worked on a tooth before
  • This is why implants got a bad name initially
  • Don't repeat this mistake — plan backwards from the prosthesis

Managing Retained Roots

Key Decision Points

Root breaks during extraction — what now?▼
Don't panic. Don't call the patient back next week. Wait 3–5 weeks. Granulation tissue forms around the root fragment, making it much easier to retrieve. This is the optimal window — too early is hard, too long and other problems develop.
H-file technique for palatal roots▼
Use an endodontic H-file slightly larger than the canal. Don't push — screw it in so it grooves into the root. Then rock it (don't pull). Rocking expands the socket. Then ease it downwards. This avoids pushing the root toward the sinus.
Orthodontic extraction with broken palatal root▼
Be very cautious about destroying buccal bone to retrieve it — the orthodontist needs that bone for tooth movement. Take a PA, consult the orthodontist. Most will say leave it. If you must retrieve, consider waiting for granulation tissue.
When to leave a root alone▼
Not every retained root needs removal. If it's small, deep, asymptomatic, and the cost of retrieval (bone destruction, nerve risk) outweighs the benefit — leave it. Monitor with radiographs.

Extraction as Confidence Builder

Nobody says: "This guy did a great extraction!" But they do say: "This guy did a great implant." Your extraction skills are the invisible foundation that earns you the £2,000–3,000 implant case.

Positioning & Ergonomics

🪑 Sit, Don't Stand

"You do all your dentistry sitting. Why stand up for extraction?" All patients go supine. Shankar works at 9 o'clock position for everything.

↔️ Same Side

Always work from the same side as the surgery. Left side work = sit on the left. Otherwise you cut off the light and your assistant can't see or help.

👐 Don't Hold the Maxilla

For upper extractions: don't hold the alveolus. If you need that much force, you're using abnormal force. Exception: labial finger on upper/lower anteriors for counter-traction.

😊 Brave Face

"70% of communication is nonverbal." Keep a relaxed face even when things go wrong. Create a calm environment — music, banter, distraction.

When and Why to Raise a Flap

Three Reasons to Remove Bone

Create a point of application — expose enough bone to get your elevator or forceps engaged
Clear the maximum convexity — the crown is wider than the root. Bone covering this bulge must be removed or the tooth won't budge
Clear the path of exit — bone blocking the tooth's only escape route must go

Flap Principles

  • Base on bone — your flap must rest on a bony surface, not over a hole
  • Adequate blood supply — base should be wider than the free margin
  • Avoid vital structures — especially mental nerve in premolar region
  • Implant thinking: traditional 3-sided flaps may not be appropriate — adapt your approach
  • Don't fear the flap — oral mucosa heals beautifully. Even raw tissue re-epithelialises in 3 weeks

Mental Nerve — Don't Panic

Shankar's view: "When you're doing a normal surgical extraction, raising a flap in the 4/5 region is very, very unlikely to catch the mental nerve. If your incision is going deep past the apex of a premolar, something is wrong — you shouldn't need to go that deep."

Think Like a Clinician

Don't turn into a technician. Be a clinician. Think when you're doing something. Don't do it because somebody else told you to.

— Shankar's message to experienced practitioners
🤔

Question Everything

"Why am I doing it this way? What would happen if I did it differently?" — even if you've done it the same way for 23 years.

🔄

Reflect and Adapt

It takes 3 years to see your failures come back. That cycle of reflection and adaptation is what makes you better.

Time Is Not the Metric

"If it takes 30 minutes, so be it. You get one chance at it. Sleekness and time will come with practice."

Suturing Basics — The X-Suture

Why the X-Suture Works

  • Compresses the socket — pulls soft tissue onto bone
  • Retains the haemostat/Surgicel — bleeding pushes it out; the suture resists this outward force
  • Four anchor points — buccal → palatal → buccal → palatal creates an X across the socket

Suture Choice

  • Vicryl Rapide is ideal — resorbs in the right timeframe (5–7 days is all you need)
  • Avoid Vicryl (standard) — resorbs too slowly, accumulates debris
  • Non-resorbable is fine if you plan to remove at 5–7 days

Suturing — Needle Orientation

Lower Suturing — "U" Shape

  • Needle curves naturally downward
  • Standard orientation for lower arch
  • Curve follows the natural arc into tissue

Upper Suturing — "N" Shape ↕️

  • Flip the needle upside down
  • When working buccal → palatal on uppers, the inverted curve brings the needle out toward you
  • Sometimes use it like a straight needle for lingual access
Two-handed technique: Needle holder in one hand, tissue forceps in the other. Always. The motion is in the wrist — no elbow movement. If two people standing beside you can see your elbows moving, you're doing it wrong.

The Extraction Skills Gap

Then (Chennai 1990s)

  • 200–300 extractions per outpatient session
  • Team of 7 postgrads: injections, extractions, surgical backup
  • "Finish by 10:30 before Ali escapes with the samosas"
  • 6 months of intensive hands-on practice
  • Boiling water steriliser (not ideal!)

Now (UK 2020s)

  • One trainee from Cardiff: 8 extractions total before starting DCT
  • Sheffield average: ~80 extractions during university
  • Less dental disease = fewer extractions needed
  • Good for population, challenging for training
  • Opportunity must be actively sought
The solution: Practice deliberately. Raise a flap even when you don't strictly need to. Suture every socket. Use every extraction as a training opportunity. In 2 years, you'll be excellent.

Human Factors in Surgery

Most mishaps happen not because of lack of skill, but because of the environment and state of mind.

🧠

Mental State

Nobody goes to work thinking "I'll harm this patient." But fatigue, stress, and pressure cause errors. Your wellbeing matters.

💬

Communication

70% is nonverbal. Patients read your face, your hesitation, your body language. Project calm control.

🏥

Environment

Right instruments, right lighting, right positioning, right assistance. Set up for success before you start.

⚖️

Dentistry is Hard

"Dentists get a very raw deal. You're isolated, everyone chases you for money, patient expectations are mismatched." — seek support systems.

Essential Instruments

🔧

Coupland's 1, 2, 3

Your workhorse. Wedging + lever principles. Goes into the socket, between root and PDL. Engages bone with its curved tip.

Lever principle
🔩

Cryer's (L + R)

For when one root is out — goes into the empty socket, rotates at 90° to lift the remaining root. Wheel and axle principle.

Rotation principle
🦜

Parrot Beak Forceps

One sharp beak engages the furcation lingually. Crushes and the tooth lifts. "It will change your molar extractions."

Game changer
🔭

Laster's Retractor

Goes behind upper 8s to prevent escape distally. Reflects light. Retracts cheek. Essential for tier 2 wisdom tooth work.

🪥

Rake Retractor

Hold with 2 fingers, other 3 support the mandible from below. Counter-pressure for elevator force from above.

Lower molars
📐

Straight Handpiece

With tapered fissure bur. Not high speed (emphysema risk). For sectioning teeth — see what your bur tip is doing at all times.

Safety first

Lecture 1 — Key Takeaways

Assessment first, always. Your treatment can only be as good as your assessment. Predict problems before they happen.
The tooth is yours, the bone is the patient's. Section freely, preserve bone absolutely.
Lower forceps on upper molars. Forces run parallel to roots. If it breaks, it breaks at the cervical margin — more to grab.
Split vertically, not horizontally. Pressure goes mesial/distal, not labial. Preserve the labial plate.
Restorative skills > surgical skills for implant dentistry. Plan from the prosthesis backwards.
Practice deliberately. Raise flaps, suture sockets, use every case as training. Time will make you fast.
Think, don't just do. Question why you do things a certain way. Be a clinician, not a technician.
