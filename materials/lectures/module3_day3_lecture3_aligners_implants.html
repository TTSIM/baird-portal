Why It Matters

The Multidisciplinary Imperative

Implants should be prosthetically driven and orthodontically prepared. Ignoring the orthodontic dimension leads to compromised positions, difficult restorations, and implant failure.

"We have no idea how many times we are going to need ortho — either aligners or fixed appliances. And with this: Do we do ortho first, in the middle, or at the end? That's the main question."

Consequences of a Single Missing Tooth (Untreated)

  • Occlusal instability — bite collapse over time
  • Pathological tooth migration — tipping, drifting, over-eruption
  • Malocclusion — spacing and alignment changes
  • Alveolar bone resorption — ridge diminishes without stimulation
  • Aesthetic and confidence impact
  • Functional consequences — reduced chewing efficiency
The Core Rule

Orthodontics Before Implants — Why?

Why Do Ortho First?

  • Determine if ridge preservation, sinus lift, or bone graft is needed before committing
  • Assess interocclusal space accurately BEFORE implant placement
  • Establish correct root positions for ideal implant positioning
  • Avoid orthodontic movement around an osseointegrated implant (causes resorption)
  • Sinus lift + fixed ortho = proven bone resorption risk

"If you're doing a graft and you're also doing orthodontic movements, you may lose part of your graft. Soft tissue surgery and ortho together — same risk."

Risks of Implant BEFORE Ortho

  • Ortho around implants creates osteal resorption near the implant
  • Soft tissue may retract if aligner puts pressure on gingival margin
  • Graft healing compromised by adjacent tooth movement
  • Implant position may be suboptimal for final orthodontic result
  • May need to remove and replace the implant — avoidable

Exception: Implant as Anchorage

If implant is placed in the molar/premolar area to serve as orthodontic anchorage for canine distalization or large rotations — acceptable. Must wait 3 months post-integration before loading for ortho.

Spatial Analysis

Space Requirements — Single Implant

Mesiodistal Space

  • Minimum: 6mm (some references)
  • Clinical recommendation: 6.5–7mm
  • Implant needs 1–1.5mm bone on each side
  • Tight at 6mm: increased peri-implant bone loss risk
  • Measure: contact point to contact point
  • Also measure: minimum available (near gingival level — bone level)

Interocclusal Space (Vertical)

  • Minimum: 8mm (crown only, not components)
  • Crown alone ≈ 4–5mm for posterior
  • Must also accommodate: abutment + components
  • Short abutment → low retention → crown keeps loosening
  • Short abutment → cleaning difficulty → periimplantitis risk
  • Scan patient and use digital articulator for precision

"'Mind the gap.' You need to know the distance — understand those spaces. Always leave 7mm of mesiodistal space in your orthodontic prescription."

Spatial Analysis

Space Requirements — Multiple Implants

Two Adjacent Implants

  • Minimum 3mm between implant platforms
  • 1–1.5mm bone on each side of each implant
  • Interradicular space needed: 5.5mm
  • Total mesiodistal space for two implants: 12–14mm
  • Plus: 11% root relapse risk — plan with this buffer

Bolton Analysis

Compares the mesiodistal width of teeth to their antagonists. Essential when planning implant crowns.

  • If implant crown is too different from antagonist width → looks malformed
  • Especially important for central incisors, canines, first premolars
  • Mesiodistal AND crown height must both match arch dimensions

Three-Unit Bridge: Two Implants

  • Space between: each implant to adjacent tooth
  • Space between: the two implants themselves
  • Emergency profile space for pontic
  • If insufficient space → single implant + cantilever (compromised)

"If you quote 3 implants and there's only space for 2 with a bridge — the patient is in finance and already paid extra. This creates compliance issues and liability. Plan the space precisely before quoting."

Two Measuring Points

  • Contact point to contact point — aesthetic planning
  • Minimum available at bone/gingival level — surgical space
  • Both measurements required for every planned implant case
Clear Aligners

How Clear Aligners Work

All clear aligner systems work on the same biomechanical principles. Brand differences exist but the physics doesn't change between systems.

Core Biomechanics

  • Apply light, controlled forces to crowns (not roots)
  • Forces are programmed into each aligner in sequence
  • Different movement types split across multiple aligners
  • Predictable execution — prescription tied to each aligner

What Aligners Do Well

  • Crown tipping movements
  • Space creation / closure
  • Rotation (mild–moderate)
  • Single tooth intrusion (up to 0.5mm for molars)
  • Spacing (may avoid an implant entirely)
  • Levelling the curve of Spee (mild)

What Aligners Do Poorly

  • Molar uprighting — requires TADs / auxiliaries
  • Root movement — forces act on crown, not root
  • Large rotations of molars
  • Significant intrusion of brachycephalic patients (strong masseter)
  • Extrusion — do at very end of treatment sequence

"The molar is a beast. Uprighting a molar with aligners alone? It doesn't matter which brand — it's just really hard for them. That's where TADs come in."

ClearCorrect Specific

Movement difficulty flagged by colour: purple = very difficult (needs elastics/buttons/auxiliaries), green = straightforward. Warns clinician about challenging movements pre-treatment.

Aligners + Implants

Benefits of Aligners for Implant Cases

Clinical Advantages

  • Predictable timeline — you know when to expect ideal space
  • Patient can see deadline for implant surgery
  • Less push-back than "2 years with orthodontist"
  • Integrates with digital workflow — technician uses final setup for surgical guide
  • Digital mockup showable to patient at consultation
  • Removable: better oral hygiene, less hygienist visits
  • Reduced peri-implant inflammation vs fixed brackets

Implant-Driven Specific Benefits

  • Prosthetically guides implant position
  • Facilitates ideal emergence profile
  • Improves aesthetic outcomes
  • Reduces risk of compromised implant placement angle
  • Root position on CBCT used to guide safe movement ranges

"When I say to the patient 'you'll need 2 years with an orthodontist first' — they say 'that long?'. With aligners: 'Here's your 10-aligner plan, implant in 3 months.' Much more acceptable."

Root Control

Managing Root Position & Relapse

Root movement is the hardest thing to control with aligners — and the most important for implant spacing.

11% Root Relapse Rate

After orthodontic root movement, 11% relapse is expected. For implant planning, this means:

  • Plan space with 11% buffer built in
  • If finishing with 7mm: ensure setup achieves 7–8mm
  • Root tips can close space before implant placed
  • Consider retainer with attachments after ortho

Managing Relapse

  • Apply 5° double-bend at end of treatment (equivalent to T-loop wire bend)
  • Helps root stay in correct angulation post-ortho
  • Apply second round at end — reduces relapse
  • Retainer with attachments on adjacent teeth holds root position
  • Without bone on root: retainer on crown only = crown doesn't move, root can

"At the end of treatment: leave your retainers with attachments, with gauges. They will hold the tooth in place until the surgery is done."

Anchorage

TADs — Temporary Anchorage Devices

Mini-implants (TADs) placed in bone provide skeletal anchorage for movements that aligners cannot achieve alone.

When TADs Are Needed

  • Molar uprighting — aligners cannot upright molars predictably
  • Molar intrusion >0.5mm — especially brachycephalic patients
  • Significant distalization
  • Large rotations of molar/premolar teeth
  • Any movement where aligner flag shows "purple" difficulty

Using Existing Implants as Anchorage

  • Integrated implants provide excellent skeletal anchorage
  • No periodontium — implant doesn't move under load
  • Can use for: large rotations, distalization, intrusion
  • Wait 3 months post-integration before using as anchorage
  • Buttons or composite engagers can be placed on crown surface
  • Rough crown surface (buff first) for better composite adhesion

"If the patient is fit and well and has an integrated implant — feel free to use it for big rotations, for distalization. The implant is rock solid in the bone."

Aligner Design

Trim Line Selection — High vs Low

The trim line (how far the aligner extends cervically) affects the force distribution and soft tissue pressure around the implant.

High Trim Line

Extends to cervical area of the crown. Forces applied near the gingival margin.

  • Better for root movement
  • Higher torque control
  • Recommended when implant will be placed after ortho
  • Forces near cervical = more root influence

Low Trim Line

Stops at incisal/occlusal edge. Forces applied near crown tip only.

  • Better for crown tipping
  • Recommended when implant is mid-treatment or already placed
  • Avoids pressure on gingival margin → prevents soft tissue recession around implant
  • If implant already in, high trim line risks soft tissue retraction

"If you did your implant before ortho or mid-treatment: choose the low trim line — you don't want pressure around the soft tissue. Your tissue could retract if you have a high trim line."

Clinical Workflow

The Correct Treatment Sequence

1

Comprehensive Digital Documentation

Intraoral scans, CBCT, full face/smile photos, digital smile design. Share everything with lab technician AND orthodontist from the start.

2

Digital Mockup + Treatment Plan

Technician produces mockup. Patient can visualise final outcome before starting. This alone dramatically improves acceptance and compliance.

3

Orthodontic Phase (Aligners)

Create ideal space for implants. Correct root angulations. Optimise occlusion. Pontic tooth in aligner maintains aesthetics during treatment.

4

Implant Surgery

Space confirmed. CBCT repeated if needed. Surgical guide based on final aligner setup. Place implant(s). Allow osseointegration.

5

Soft Tissue / Periodontal Surgery

Any gingival surgery goes LAST — after ortho and after implant integration. Avoids relapse from surgical trauma during active tooth movement.

6

Final Restoration + Retention

Crowns fitted. Ortho retainer (with attachments). Nightguard for implant protection. Start annual review cycle.

Provisionals

Managing the Missing Tooth During Treatment

Patients will not tolerate a gap for 12–18 months of ortho. Options for managing missing teeth aesthetically during the orthodontic phase.

🦷

Pontic in Aligner

Artificial tooth built into the aligner shell. Clean surface. Automatic update with each new aligner. Best for most cases.

🔗

Bonded Temp Crown

Composite or resin-bonded temporary. Patient eats, goes out confidently. Especially useful for anterior teeth.

📌

Unloaded Implant Crown

Implant placed but NOT loaded. Temp crown for aesthetics only. Final crown only after all treatment complete.

"By delaying the extraction to the very end, the patient will have something there to eat, to go out, to feel confident. This is the best strategy for most anterior cases."

Engagers on Implant Crowns

Aligner engagers CAN be placed on implant crown surfaces. Rough the surface with a buff, use monobond adhesive (not standard bond) for better adhesion to ceramic/acrylic. Works for rotation correction.

Retention

Retention After Implant + Ortho

Retainer Strategy

  • Always use retainer with attachments after ortho adjacent to implant site
  • Attachments on adjacent teeth hold root position (not just crown)
  • Without attachments: root relapse possible even with perfect crown position
  • Keep in place until surgery is completed and implant loaded
  • After final crown fitted: new retainer without attachments if stable

Nightguard — Always After Implants

  • Implant has no proprioception — patient cannot feel grinding
  • Daytime: conscious chewing, aware of forces
  • Night: unconscious loading → bone loss risk
  • Recommend Hawley retainer at night (acrylic plate grind surface)
  • Essix retainer NOT ideal for grinders (no occlusal coverage)

"Having an implant and just giving an Essix retainer is enough for ortho — but it's not good for the implant. If the patient grinds, I'd rather have the nightguard on the nightstand than the crown fractured in the first year."

Night Protection

Retainer vs Nightguard — Negotiating the Balance

Stage 1 (Active Ortho Phase)

  • Retainer: full time (day + night) for first 6 months
  • Patient is NOT grinding protection during this phase
  • If grinder: nightguard at night + retainer during day
  • Accept that there may be minor ortho relapse — the implant takes priority

Stage 2 (Long-Term)

  • Year 2+: nightguard (night) + retainer (day)
  • Non-grinders: retainer alone may suffice
  • Annual implant review: X-ray + clean + check nightguard
  • "Having an implant is like owning a car — MOT and service every year"

Patient Education Script

The key message to patients:

  • "The implant is in the bone — it doesn't move. If you grind, the force goes directly to the implant and bone."
  • "You won't know you're grinding at night."
  • "This nightguard protects your investment."
  • "Use retainer in the day, nightguard at night."

"I always recommend a nightguard when finishing an implant case — it doesn't matter if single unit or multiple units or All-on-X."

Special Indications

When Orthodontics is Mandatory

Some clinical situations make multidisciplinary ortho-implant planning non-negotiable, not optional.

Absolute Indications

  • Congenitally missing laterals — space created by peg lateral or agenesis needs calibration
  • Posterior tooth loss with drifting/tilting — especially upright molars (hardest movement)
  • Collapsing bite / increased overbite — vertical space creation required
  • Dento-alveolar trauma — fibrotic tissue + abnormal root positions

Periodontally Compromised Teeth

Counter-intuitive: ortho in perio patients can be beneficial before implants. Why?

  • Moving the tooth to a better position improves future implant anatomy
  • Even if the tooth is lost during treatment — the bone and soft tissue are better positioned for implant
  • Must obtain special consent — tooth may be lost during ortho
  • Ortho does not improve perio — but improves implant site post-extraction

"We are leaving the patient in a better condition for the implant and for the restorative site. That tooth is going to be lost at some point — we're just optimising what comes after."

Case Study

Trauma Case — Lost 11 & 12 at Age 14

1

Background

Car accident at 14. Upper right 1 & 2 (11, 12) lost. Fibrotic scarring in the area. Implant placement when 18–19 years old.

2

Pre-implant Orthodontics

Fixed braces placed BEFORE implants. Ortho established correct root positions and space. Fibrotic tissue managed with periodontal surgery (at the very end).

3

Implant Placement

At 18–19: sufficient bone confirmed. Implants placed in ideal position. Long healing with temp crown in aligner pontic throughout.

4

Scar Removal + Final Restoration

Plastic/periodontal surgery to remove scarring as LAST step. Final crowns after all ortho and tissue management complete. Patient aged 19 — much better outcome than if implant placed at 14.

"The accident was at 14. She was 19 when we started — already a lot of waiting. But by taking the time to plan properly, the outcome was dramatically better than rushing in."

Case Study

Periodontally Compromised — All-on-X + Aligners

Presentation

  • Upper arch: fully periodontally compromised
  • Lower arch: canine-to-canine work needed
  • Plan: All-on-X implants upper (molars + premolars)
  • Then: aligners lower arch to improve position

Clever Sequence

  • Upper All-on-X first — surgical guide based on final aligner setup position
  • Upper arch done: serves as stable reference for lower treatment
  • Lower: aligners with existing implants (5 & 6) as anchorage
  • Canine recession: move root slightly lingual → more space for perio graft at very end

Key Lesson

The upper arch All-on-X was planned with the lower final position in mind. The digital setup told the technician exactly where the lower arch would end up.

  • Surgical guide made for upper BEFORE lower ortho
  • Patient motivated: upper arch already done
  • Periodontal surgery at very end once everything positioned

"With the final position from the lower arch, the denture can do the surgical guide for the upper arch first — then do ortho on the lower. You actually have better control of what happens on the lower arch."

Case Study

Mid-Treatment Extraction — Replanning Protocol

The Situation

  • Patient in active aligner treatment
  • Compromised tooth becomes symptomatic mid-treatment
  • Tooth must be extracted before treatment is complete

Management Protocol

  • Rescan the patient after extraction
  • Replan with final position in mind
  • Request pontic in aligner at extracted tooth site
  • Finalise remaining space requirements
  • May place implant during this stage (unloaded)

Post-Treatment Retention

  • Because the implant is now in place: no need for retainer with attachments on that site
  • The implant crown itself maintains the space
  • Standard retainer post-ortho is sufficient for that segment
  • Nightguard still recommended for implant protection

"Once the implant is there — you also don't need to put a retainer with engager attachments on. The implant crown takes care of maintaining that space."

Refinements

If space is still insufficient after refinements: do another refinement round. It is better to leave the patient in more aligners than to deliver fewer implants than promised.

Medications

Medications Affecting Bone Response

Both orthodontic tooth movement and implant osseointegration depend on bone remodelling — which can be significantly impaired by common medications.

High-Risk Medications

  • Antidepressants — decrease bone response; slower ortho movement AND slower osseointegration
  • Immunosuppressants — avoid using implant as anchorage
  • Diabetes medications (especially if poorly controlled) — impaired healing
  • Bisphosphonates — significant risk of MRONJ

"Finding a patient who is fit and well with no medications for an implant — I think it's like gold. It's not what we see in daily practice."

Clinical Implications

  • Patient lost bone during graft surgery + on antidepressants + needing large tooth movements → be conservative
  • Delay placement if bone remodelling is needed adjacent to implant
  • Minimal movements near newly grafted areas
  • Check medications at every appointment

Heavy Grinders / Brachycephalic

Do NOT use implant as orthodontic anchorage in active grinders (until they have nightguard compliance proven). Excessive force from grinding + ortho loading = failure risk.

Communication

Talking to Your Orthodontic Team

Poor communication between the implant surgeon and the orthodontist is the most preventable cause of poor outcomes.

What to Send to Ortho / GP

  • How much space you need and where (in millimetres)
  • Whether it's a single implant, multiple, or bridge
  • Type of connection (tissue level/bone level)
  • Abutment type planned
  • Cement vs screw-retained
  • Overbite and overjet requirements
  • Your final crown vision — which "apple" do you want?

What to Receive from Ortho

  • Digital setup showing final root positions
  • Confirmation of space achieved (mesiodistal + vertical)
  • Alert if target space cannot be achieved — so you can adapt
  • Recommendation to use narrow implant if necessary
  • Final retainer type prescribed

"If you are just sending 'go to the tell your patient to do ortho' — they will go to the GP, but you didn't leave anything explicit. Be the best friend of your restorative colleagues."

Extrusion Strategy

Aligner Extrusion — Sequence Matters

When Extrusion is Needed

  • Restoring a compromised tooth before extraction (builds bone)
  • Improving crown-to-root ratio before restorative work
  • Establishing better vertical position for future implant
  • Correcting deep bite (relative extrusion of posteriors)

Why It Goes Last

Extrusion is the hardest movement to achieve with aligners. If programmed early, other movements are completed and the aligner runs out before full extrusion.

Correct Protocol

  • Request extrusion for the LAST steps of the aligner sequence
  • "Break the movements" — align first, then extrude
  • Higher chance of success because the aligner can focus on extrusion
  • Can still complete satisfactorily even if not 100% at end

"Always request extrusion for the last steps. When we break the movements — align the arch and extrude later — we have a higher chance of achieving the extrusion."

Molar Intrusion Limit

Maximum predictable molar intrusion with aligners alone: 0.5mm. Brachycephalic patients (strong masseter): even less predictable. Use TADs for >0.5mm intrusion.

Buccal Corridor

The Buccal Corridor — Don't Forget It

Once an implant is placed, it becomes a fixed point. Any subsequent attempt to expand the arch will be blocked by the implant — the teeth around it can move but the implant cannot.

The Risk

  • Placing an upper premolar implant in a narrow arch
  • Patient needs arch expansion 2 years later for aesthetics
  • The 5 cannot expand — everything around it moves but the implant is stuck
  • Leaves an obvious asymmetry in the smile

Assessment Protocol

  • Check buccal corridor at consultation smile photos
  • If arch is narrow: plan expansion BEFORE implant
  • Use upper study models in articulator — assess expansion potential
  • If expansion likely needed in future → do ortho first

"We look at the patient and say: bone OK, soft tissue OK, I can place the implant. But if that arch needs expansion in 2 years — you can't expand that 5 anymore. It's permanently fixed in position."

Space Measurement

Two Methods to Measure Space

Two distinct measurements must be made for every planned implant site — one for aesthetics, one for surgical safety.

Contact Point to Contact Point

The wider measurement — from where adjacent crowns touch occlusally.

  • Used for aesthetic crown planning
  • Determines visible space in the smile
  • If too small: crown will look narrow/malformed
  • If too large: dark triangle at emergence

Minimum Available (Bone Level)

The narrower measurement — at the level of the gingiva/bone, where root surfaces converge.

  • Used for surgical safety planning
  • Root surfaces converge here — less space than crown zone
  • If <6.5mm: implant risks root contact on either side
  • Both measurements needed: they are often quite different

Always measure BOTH. A case may look like it has enough contact-point space, but at bone level the roots may leave insufficient room for the implant body.

Implant as Anchorage

Using Existing Implants for Ortho Anchorage

When It Works Well

  • Patient fit and well — no immunosuppression, no uncontrolled diabetes
  • Implant fully integrated for ≥3 months
  • Not actively grinding/bruxing
  • Distalization, large rotation correction
  • Cases requiring skeletal anchorage

When NOT to Use as Anchorage

  • Diabetes (risk of implant failure + poor bone response)
  • Immunosuppressant medication
  • Heavy grinder/clencher (unless nightguard is compliant)
  • Fresh implant placement with bone grafting — remodelling area still active
  • Large movements adjacent to newly-grafted site

"The implant is rock solid in the bone. There is no periodontal ligament — it will not move. Feel free to use it for big rotations, for distalization — it's a good source of anchorage."

Digital Mockup — The Treatment Acceptance Tool

Why Digital Mockup Works

  • Patient can visualise the end goal on the first appointment
  • They take it home and show family — family now supports treatment
  • Patient remembers what they started with (vs 18 months later)
  • Reduces compliance issues around cost / duration
  • Creates shared accountability with the clinical team

"Charge up to £250 for a mockup — they will be grateful. They go home, show it to the family. If the family was not supporting before, they will see the final outcome."

What to Include in the Plan

  • Timeline: when ortho, when surgery, when crown

  • Animated soft-tissue video mockup (Smartcloud/ClearCorrect)

Prosthetically-Driven Mindset

Think backwards from the final restoration. Know what the crown will look like, what connection it needs, what space it requires, what abutment height is needed — BEFORE you plan the first aligner or place the first implant.

Ortho-Can-Help

Can Orthodontics Create Bone?

An emerging debate: can aligner-mediated tooth movement create new bone and improve the site before implant placement?

The Theory

  • Moving a tooth through an extraction site before implant placement may stimulate bone formation in the direction of movement
  • This is called orthodontically mediated bone generation
  • Theoretically could reduce need for bone grafting

"There is a lot of positive cases and maybe we will create some bone — but it's not 100% sure. And for a significant bone defect, I would still do step-by-step staging."

Current Consensus

  • Literature not yet in consensus
  • Some cases show benefit — but case selection is critical
  • Not reliable for large bone defects
  • May work for small amounts of additional bone
  • Do not use as substitute for staged bone grafting in deficient ridges

Practical Advice

If ortho can improve the site and the literature suggests it's possible for that defect size — consider it as an adjunct, not a replacement for bone grafting. Do not put all eggs in one basket.

Lecture 03 Complete

Key Takeaways

1. Ortho First

Establish correct root positions and space before placing implants. The implant is permanent — the ortho window is not.

2. Mind the Gap

6.5–7mm mesiodistal, 8mm vertical. Measure both contact-point AND bone-level space. Quote only what fits.

3. 11% Relapse

Root movement relapses. Use retainer with attachments and double-bend prescription. Build a buffer into your space plan.

4. Always Nightguard

Implant has no proprioception. Patient doesn't know they grind at night. Hawley retainer at night for all grinders.

5. Implants as Anchorage

Integrated, healthy implants are excellent orthodontic anchors. Use them for rotations and distalization (not in grinders or the immunocompromised).

6. Show to Accept

Digital mockup on day one. The patient sees the end goal. Family supports. Treatment is accepted and adhered to.
