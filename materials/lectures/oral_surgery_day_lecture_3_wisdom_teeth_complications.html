Wisdom Teeth
& Complications

Classification, Surgical Approach, Coronectomy, Nerve Injury, Bleeding & OAF Management

Based on the oral surgery masterclass by Mr Shankar

Winter's Classification

Point of Application — The Key

Universal rule: All impaction types → point of application is MESIAL… except distoangular, where it's DISTOBUCCAL. This is why differentiating vertical from distoangular matters.

Vertical

Mesial point of application. Use Coupland's or straight elevator. Twist distally and lingually. Sometimes forceps work.

Easiest

Mesioangular

Mesial application. If bone loss mesially → may elevate without sectioning. If tooth impaction against 7 → must section.

Check for bone loss

Horizontal

Always section. Split between crown and root (horizontal cut) or vertical cut. Crown out first, then roots.

Always section

Distoangular

Distobuccal application. Remove bone behind tooth. Try to keep tooth whole — sectioning makes it harder. Deliver distally and lingually.

Hardest

Surgical Approach — Horizontal Impaction

WHARFE Assessment

W — Winter's

Classification type (vertical, horizontal, mesio, disto). Determines surgical approach and difficulty.

Essential

H — Height of Mandible

0–30mm acceptable, 30–50mm harder. Shankar: "I don't believe it's worthwhile knowing."

Skip

A — Angulation

Redundant with Winter's classification.

Skip

R — Root Morphology

Favourable curve = comes with elevation path. Unfavourable curve = root will break. Critical to assess.

Essential

F — Follicle

If follicle present → space around tooth → advantageous for elevation. Cyst = even more space.

Helpful

E — Exit Point

Lingual = least resistance. Buccal = thick bone, more resistance. Most teeth delivered lingually.

Helpful
Shankar's summary: Focus on W (type), R (roots), F (follicle space), and E (exit path). Skip H and A — they add little practical value.

Coronectomy

When to Consider

  • ID nerve running through the tooth → extraction = anaesthesia (nerve severed)
  • Nerve beside or below → extraction = paraesthesia risk
  • Patient informed and chooses reduced risk option
  • CBCT shows intimate nerve relationship

The Root Migration Phenomenon

After coronectomy, roots tend to migrate away from the nerve over time, as if there's some eruptive force. This makes a second procedure safer if ever needed.

Important Caveats

  • Doesn't eliminate risk — drill vibration at 40,000 rpm still affects the nerve
  • Reduces risk significantly but paraesthesia still possible
  • Roots may need removal later if they become symptomatic
  • Tell patients: possible need for a second surgery
  • If roots become loose during coronectomy → you may have to remove them
Consent clearly: "I'm removing the top of the tooth and leaving the roots. This reduces the nerve risk but doesn't eliminate it. You may need another procedure later."

The CBCT Dilemma

The more cone beam CTs you do, the more you'll find the ID nerve is close to teeth. Cone beam CT is more for the patient to understand risks and for defensive practice — it's not changing what I do to that tooth.

— Shankar

⚠️ The Precedent

A speech therapist received £250,000 for lingual paraesthesia. The expert witness asked: "Why wasn't a CBCT done?" — for a lingual nerve injury. You can't see the lingual nerve on CBCT. But the precedent is now set.

7 OPG Signs

Look for signs of nerve proximity on OPG: darkening of root, deflection of canal, narrowing of canal, interruption of white line, diversion of canal, superimposition, narrowing of root. If any present → consider CBCT.

Tier 2 practitioners: "For £85–140, is it worth taking the risk? Oral surgery is not a practice builder. Don't take the hassle. If you feel it's not safe for your environment and skills — refer."

Upper Eights — Special Considerations

🪟

Laster's Retractor

Goes behind the upper 8 to prevent it escaping distally and upward. Reflects light for visibility. Retracts the cheek. Essential instrument.

🦷

Mouth Position

Don't open wide! When the mouth is wide open, the coronoid process moves forward and blocks the tooth's path. Ask patient to half-close or move jaw to the same side.

⚠️

Tuberosity Risk

Upper 8 extractions can fracture the tuberosity. Isolated tuberosity fragment → bin it and suture. Segment with 6/7 moving → splint and plan surgically.

🩸

Haematoma

You can't see an upper 8 haematoma because of the loose pterygoid plexus. Can cause trismus lasting 4–6 weeks.

When NOT to Extract a Wisdom Tooth

Just because there's a tooth that is deeply impacted doesn't mean it needs removing. If it's down there, keep it there. Don't go disturbing it if it's not giving symptoms.

— Shankar

Don't Extract If:

  • Deep vertical impaction, asymptomatic
  • No caries, no pericoronitis, no pathology
  • Extraction risk outweighs benefit (nerve proximity)
  • The cost of retrieval (bone, nerve) exceeds the gain

Watch Out For:

  • TMJ pain in 20-somethings being blamed on wisdom teeth — explore other causes first
  • Unerupted wisdom teeth normally don't cause pain
  • Orthodontists pushing for wisdom tooth removal — question the indication
  • Private orthodontics driving unnecessary referrals

Complications — By Timing

⏱️ Intraoperative

Vasovagal, panic attacks, LA failure, tooth/root fracture, adjacent tooth damage, tuberosity fracture, mandible fracture, root into sinus, bleeding

📅 Immediate Post-Op (same day)

Emotional response (crying), pain, wrong extraction realisation, reactionary bleeding (LA wears off)

📅 Intermediate (days)

Swelling, bruising, dry socket, trismus, infection → cervicofacial abscess

📅 Late (weeks–months)

Paraesthesia/anaesthesia, chronic infection (osteomyelitis), oroantral fistula, bisphosphonate-related issues

Shankar's approach: "All of us are going to get complications. Recognise it, act on it, own it. Patients are grateful when you say 'I'm sorry this happened, but here's what we're going to do about it.'"

Nerve Injury — Anaesthesia vs Paraesthesia

Anaesthesia (Total Numbness)

  • Nerve is severed — will NOT recover on its own
  • Needs URGENT referral for nerve repair
  • Window: 4–6 weeks for best outcomes
  • Sheffield = gold standard for nerve repair
  • "It's better to have anaesthesia — at least you know exactly what to do"

Paraesthesia (Altered Sensation)

  • Usually recovers — nerve is bruised, not severed
  • Map it: test pain, touch, temperature, 2-point discrimination
  • Re-map at follow-up to track improvement
  • May be from canal roof disruption or local inflammation
  • "The problem with paraesthesia is everybody's got their own idea about it"
Resource: Sheffield University has a nerve injury department website with a flow chart for managing altered sensations. Bookmark it.

Nerve Injury Assessment

The Four Tests — Click to Learn

1. Pain Sensation Pin prick▼
Use a dental probe or pin. Test affected area vs normal side. Does the patient feel sharp? Dull? Nothing? Map the boundary where sensation changes.
2. Light Touch Cotton wool▼
Gently brush with cotton wool. Can they feel it? Is it the same as the other side? This tests A-beta fibres — the first to recover.
3. Temperature Ethyl chloride▼
Use ethyl chloride spray or warm/cold instrument. Tests C-fibres and A-delta fibres. Temperature discrimination recovers later than touch.
4. Two-Point Discrimination Callipers▼
How close can two points be before they feel like one? Normal lip: ~5mm. Increased distance = nerve damage. This is the finest test and last to recover.
Document everything: Map the affected area on a diagram. Date it. Repeat at follow-up. Photographic documentation is valuable. Improvement over time → reassuring. No improvement → refer to Sheffield.

Bleeding Management

My professor used to say: three P's for bleeding — back Pressure, Prayer, and Patience. Take your swab, put it where it's bleeding, sit tight for 3 to 4 minutes.

— Shankar

Soft Tissue Bleeding

  • Suture it — pulls tissue onto bone, compresses, stops bleeding
  • X-suture over Surgicel holds the pack in place
  • Buccal advancement flap for socket closure if needed

Bony Bleeding

  • Crush it — take a Coupland's or Warwick James, press and crush the bleeding point on bone
  • Bone wax — works but contains beeswax → wax granuloma risk. Avoid at implant sites
  • Bipolar cautery — hospital luxury. A £80 pen cautery with disposable copper tip is a practice alternative

Oroantral Communication & Fistula

OAC (Communication)

  • Fresh opening between mouth and sinus
  • Will heal on its own if contamination is stopped
  • No epithelial lining yet

OAF (Fistula)

  • Epithelialised tract — will NOT heal spontaneously
  • Needs formal closure: excise the fistula, advance tissue
  • Develops weeks after an untreated OAC

What Causes Problems

Oral bacteria + respiratory bacteria don't like each other. When they mix through an OAC → infection. The key is stopping the contamination pathway as fast as possible.

Emergency Management

Try suction — if visible, use suction tip to retrieve the root
If not visible → close — buccal advancement flap or cross suture
Splint option — vacuum-formed splint worn 3 weeks. Place gauze over socket BEFORE taking impression!
Antibiotics + refer

Root Displaced into Sinus

Why it happens: Broken root + apical pressure from elevator = root pushed upward through thin antral floor. Upper 6s and 7s most at risk.
If visible and accessible: Attempt retrieval with suction. Don't push further.
If lost: Close the socket (buccal advancement flap or splint). Take post-op X-ray to locate the root.
Not every root needs removal: If you seal off the contamination pathway, it may remain asymptomatic. Monitor.
If it gets infected: CT sinus → ENT involvement → FESS → root retrieval. This process takes well over a year.
Document & refer: Record everything. Make the patient aware. Urgent referral (though hospital will call it routine).
Prevention: When elevating upper posterior roots, avoid apical pressure. Come from above and push down, or use an H-file screwed into the root canal. Be aware that the sinus floor may be paper-thin or absent.

Tuberosity Fracture

✅ No Problem (isolated)

  • Tuberosity has no function
  • Fragment completely detached → remove and bin
  • Fragment attached to tooth → comes off with it
  • Just need good soft tissue closure (buccal advancement flap)
  • "Everybody gets excited about tuberosity fracture. Don't."

❌ Big Problem (segment)

  • If 6, 7, or adjacent teeth are moving with the segment → dentoalveolar fracture
  • Stop. Don't complete the extraction.
  • Splint: vacuum-formed or figure-of-eight wire
  • Hold for 4 weeks to allow healing
  • Then plan surgical removal of the 8

Mandible Fracture

⚠️ Very Uncommon — But It Happens

Shankar's department had two mandible fractures in one year — the first he'd seen in his career. "It all comes down to sussing how much pressure you're able to put."

🦴

Bone Density Varies

African heritage: Very dense bone, harder to extract. Osteoporotic elderly: Fragile bone, may fracture with normal force. Know your patient's bone.

🔧

Rake Retractor

Support the mandible during lower extractions. Hold retractor with 2 fingers, other 3 under the jaw. Counter-pressure prevents excessive force transmission.

📋

Management

Recognised intraoperatively → immediate referral to OMFS. May need IMF (intermaxillary fixation) or ORIF (open reduction internal fixation).

Wrong Extraction — More Common Than You Think

Don't be led by patients: "Take this one out" is not your diagnosis. Confirm the source of pain yourself.
Radiographic evidence: Always have an X-ray that confirms pathology in the tooth you're extracting.
Mixed dentition danger: The most common mistake is confusing 4s and 5s, or deciduous and permanent canines in orthodontic cases.
Referred pain: A patient may come back 2 days later saying the pain is still there. If your assessment was wrong, this is a wrong extraction.
Orthodontic cases: Be extremely careful. Orthodontists will create havoc if you extract the wrong tooth — and rightly so.

Post-Operative Complications

🔥

Dry Socket

Blood clot lost or doesn't form. Exposed bone = intense pain 2–5 days post-op. Pack with Alvogyl or similar. Will resolve but miserable for patients.

🦠

Infection → Abscess

Simple extraction can brew into cervicofacial infection. Rare but serious. "The patient had a tooth removed, now they've got a neck abscess. It happens."

😬

Trismus

Three causes: muscle irritation from injection, haematoma organising, or infection. Takes 4–8 weeks. Mouth exercises essential.

🩸

Reactionary Bleeding

Occurs when LA vasoconstriction wears off. Patient gets home and starts bleeding. Usually controlled with pressure + bite on gauze 30 mins.

Bisphosphonates & Biologic Agents

Every other patient over the age of 60 will soon be on some biologic agent of some kind. It is such a nuisance. We do not have nationalised guidelines.

— Shankar

The Problem

Rheumatologists say one thing, oncologists say another — for the same drug. GPS have enough to manage. There's no consensus.

The Solution

Write directly to the prescribing consultant (not the GP). Ask: "I need to do an extraction. What do you want me to do?" This is the safest approach.

Risk Patients

Immunosuppressed, uncontrolled diabetics, chemotherapy patients, those on RANK-L inhibitors or bisphosphonates → risk of osteomyelitis and MRONJ.

Handling Complications — The Mindset

Recognise it — don't deny or minimise. The sooner you identify a complication, the sooner you can manage it.
Own it — "I'm sorry this has happened." Patients respect honesty. Nobody goes to work planning to harm patients.
Manage it — "Here's what we're going to do about it." Have a plan. Patients want to know you're in control.
Reflect on it — What happened? Why? Could it have been prevented? What will you do differently?
Learn from it — Some things will happen regardless. But put measures in place to minimise the risk next time.
Resilience matters: "Dentistry is probably one of the hardest fields to manage patients. You're isolated, everyone chases you for money, and patient expectations are mismatched. Build support systems around you."

Antibiotics — Shankar's Approach

Metronidazole for 3 days

When antibiotics are indicated, Shankar's preference is metronidazole for 3 days. Effective against anaerobic oral pathogens. Caveat: some patients get nausea and stomach cramps — warn them. No alcohol.

Not every extraction needs antibiotics. Use clinical judgement. Antibiotics are for infection, not prevention. If there's acute infection, give pre-operatively. Don't prescribe "just in case."

Wisdom Teeth — Decision Framework

Click through the decision points

Is the tooth symptomatic? ▼
No symptoms + deep impaction → leave it alone. Monitor. Don't extract teeth that aren't causing problems, especially if there's nerve risk. Symptomatic → continue assessment.
What classification is it? ▼
Vertical: Often straightforward — elevator, twist, deliver. Mesioangular: Check for bone loss mesially; may section if impacted against 7. Horizontal: Will need sectioning — plan for it. Distoangular: Most difficult — try to keep whole, deliver distally/lingually.
What does the OPG show about the nerve? ▼
Look for the 7 radiographic signs of proximity. If concerning → consider CBCT. If CBCT confirms intimate relationship → discuss coronectomy vs extraction with patient. Document the consent conversation thoroughly.
Can I manage this in practice? ▼
Assess your: skills (are you trained and confident?), environment (do you have the instruments, lighting, suction, assistance?), risk tolerance (for £85–140 tier 2 payment, is the risk worth it?). If any doubt → refer. "Oral surgery is not a practice builder. Don't take the hassle."

Tooth Displaced Into Tissue Spaces

Sublingual Space

Root or tooth pushed lingually during lower extraction. Dentist feels sudden loss of pressure — tooth has gone backwards and downwards into the sublingual space. Cannot be retrieved in practice.

What To Do

Own up. Explain to the patient. Close the socket. Take an X-ray to document the position. Refer urgently to OMFS for retrieval under GA.

Prevention: Support the mandible with your other hand (rake retractor + fingers underneath). Control the force direction. Be aware that excessive lingual pressure can push fragments into tissue spaces.

Disinfected vs Sterile — Know the Difference

Disinfected (Standard Dental)

  • Reduces microorganisms — doesn't eliminate all
  • Adequate for routine extractions
  • Examination gloves (not sterile)
  • Decontaminated instruments
  • Dental nurses trained in this level

✅ Sterile (Implant Level)

  • Free from all viable microorganisms
  • Required for implant placement
  • Sterile gloves, sterile drapes
  • Autoclave-sterilised instruments
  • Nurses need upskilling for this level
Critical for implant surgeons: When you ask a dental nurse for a forceps from a decontaminated tray, she'll hand it to you. For implant work, you need her to open the sterile pack and present the instrument without contaminating it. This is a different skill set.

Lecture 3 — Key Takeaways

Winter's classification determines your surgical approach. Distoangular = hardest. Horizontal = always section.
Point of application is mesial for everything except distoangular (distobuccal).
WHARFE: Focus on W, R, F, and E. Root morphology and follicle space are the most useful predictors.
Coronectomy reduces nerve risk but doesn't eliminate it. Roots migrate away over time.
Anaesthesia = urgent referral for nerve repair (4–6 weeks window). Paraesthesia = map, monitor, usually recovers.
OAC: close + seal + antibiotics + refer. Not every sinus root needs retrieval — seal the contamination path.
Complications will happen. Recognise, own, manage, reflect, learn. Build resilience and support systems.
