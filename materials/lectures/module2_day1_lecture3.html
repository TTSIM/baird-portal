Slide 2 · Plan the case

Plan the case — not the space

Early in your training you will be given relatively simple cases, focused on a single space. The temptation is to keep planning that way: see a gap, plan an implant. The successful clinician learns to do the opposite — to back out of the gap and look at the entire mouth, the whole patient, the whole life.

Snooker analogy: Snooker players plan four or five shots ahead. Golfers plan their second shot while playing the first. Why do we not think three or four appointments ahead when treating patients?

What "the case" includes

  • The chief complaint — and the unstated complaint behind it.
  • Adjacent and opposing teeth, not just the missing tooth.
  • The occlusal scheme as a whole.
  • Periodontal status of the mouth.
  • Future contingencies — what happens if a tooth or implant fails in five years?
  • Medical, social, psychological, financial, time and behavioural factors.
Slide 3 · What is "high risk"?

High risk in implant dentistry — the full picture

Implant failure

The classic worry. Marketed heavily by implant companies. But often quoted out of context — one company famously counted boxed implants in cupboards as "successes".

Peri-implantitis

By definition a late phenomenon. Real, but the literature has overstated its inevitability. Treated and untreated cases sometimes show similar tooth loss outcomes.

Aesthetic failure

Defined by the patient, not the dentist. A clinically perfect crown can still be a failure if the patient feels the result is wrong.

Phonetic failure

High-profile examples: Margaret Thatcher and Elton John reportedly never sounded the same after their implant work. Listen for S, F, V sounds during temporaries.

Litigation

Increasingly the dominant risk. A real or perceived adverse event + poor communication + arrogance = a complaint. Then a lawsuit.

Mortality

Around six known cases globally of patient death during implant treatment. Sedation risk, undiagnosed cardiovascular events, anaphylaxis. Implants are not minor surgery.

Slide 5 · Smoking

Smoking is declining — but still matters at high doses

The vascular effect

One cigarette cuts peripheral blood flow by ~40% for one hour. A pack-a-day smoker is essentially in a state of self-imposed peripheral vascular disease.

Smokers don't bleed easily on probing — which means BoP is a poor measure of perio control in heavy smokers.

Numbers from the Glasgow study

  • 2,118 implants placed by one private operator.
  • Failure rate: 4.76% in non-smokers vs 11.28% in smokers.
  • Anterior maxilla — close to 45% failure rate in heavy smokers.
Rough-surface implants have largely closed the gap between smokers and non-smokers in modern data. They should be the implant of choice for any patient who cannot or will not stop smoking.
Slide 6 · Cessation protocol

The cessation protocol that works

An early Canadian study of 226 patients and 638 implants tested a three-arm comparison: never-smokers, smokers who quit (1 week before, 8 weeks after placement), and smokers who continued.

Two patients from the lecturer's practice paid for all of their perio treatment plus a holiday within two years simply by diverting their cigarette money into a separate bank account. The financial framing converts more patients than the clinical one.
Slide 7 · Antidepressants

SSRIs and implant failure — a growing problem

Selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (e.g. citalopram, sertraline, fluoxetine) have an effect on bone metabolism. Multiple studies — increasing in size — show an increased implant failure rate.

StudySizeEffect
Early small study (~12 yrs ago)SmallApproximately doubled failure rate
2018 large study~2,000+ implantsTripled failure rate
Subsequent larger studyVery large~4× failure rate (84% vs 96% success)
Recent meta-analysisPooled~2.5× failure rate at both patient and implant level
SSRI use has become widespread in the general population. Document the medication, document the conversation, document that you informed the patient of the elevated risk. This is not an exclusion — but it is a consent issue.
Slide 8 · Proton pump inhibitors

PPIs — a newer concern

Proton pump inhibitors (e.g. omeprazole, lansoprazole, esomeprazole) interfere with calcium absorption and bone metabolism. A large retrospective British study of approximately 20,000 patients and 50,000 implants over 12 years found:

Failure rate: ~1.77× higher in PPI users compared to non-users.
Pantoprazole: appears not to increase failure — though this is a newer drug with less data.
PPI use has gone from "never heard of" in 1990 to ~20–30% of the adult population today. This is one of the fastest-growing consent issues in implant dentistry.
Slide 9 · Antibiotic allergy

Penicillin allergy — surprisingly impactful

A study of 5,500 implants in two systems treated over 10 years:

GroupFailure rate
Patients who could take penicillinBaseline
Patients allergic to penicillin (overall)~3.2× higher
Penicillin-allergic + immediate implant placement~10× higher failure
Clindamycin alternativeUp to 6× more risk of infection
Clinical rule: Do not place immediate implants in patients allergic to penicillin. The combination is one of the highest published failure scenarios in modern implant dentistry.

Tetracycline allergy does not necessarily increase implant loss but is associated with increased soft-tissue breakdown / mucositis-type problems.

Slide 10 · Bisphosphonates & vitamin D

Bisphosphonates and vitamin D — separating signal from noise

Oral bisphosphonates

Per consensus conference data, oral bisphosphonates do not significantly affect implant survival. Caution remains around IV bisphosphonates and other antiresorptives, where MRONJ risk is higher.

Oral: usually safe to proceed IV: refer / avoid

Vitamin D supplementation

A 2022 systematic review found no effect of vitamin D supplements on implant success in three studies; one outlier study suggested a relationship with deficiency, but on close reading the methodology was flawed (mixing implants and patients in the denominator with only 27 patients and 3 failures).

Evidence too weak to recommend routine supplementation
Reading lesson: When one study is an outlier, read it three or four times before believing it. You either count implants or you count patients — never mix and match.
Slide 11 · Peri-implantitis

The 90/10 rule — and why peri-implantitis is partly fake news

A famous quote from the periodontal microbiologist Jorgen Slots, opening a three-hour seminar with no slides:

"90% of peri-implantitis doesn't need treatment, 10% is untreatable. Discuss."

The data behind this provocation: in untreated cohorts, around 6.5–10% of implants are lost to peri-implantitis. In treated cohorts, around 9% are still lost. If doing nothing and doing everything produce similar outcomes, the value of the heroic intervention is questionable.

The Coley & Jemt insight

A history of marginal bone loss is not a reliable predictor of future bone loss. Most implants with 2–3 mm of early bone loss do not progress. Unnecessary alarmism leads to overtreatment that does not benefit the patient.

Don't ignore — but don't over-react. Monitor, image, and intervene if bone loss is genuinely progressive, not just present.
Slide 12 · Perio history

If you have to bet — bet on the implant or the tooth?

A 10-year retrospective study of 58 patients with periodontitis who also had implants:

  • Average annual tooth loss: ~0.07 per year (slightly more than half of those for periodontal reasons).
  • Average annual implant loss: ~0.4 per year.
  • Patients were 10× more likely to lose an implant than a tooth over the decade.
Counter-intuitive but important: In a maintained periodontal patient, teeth often outlast implants. This is one of the strongest arguments for preserving teeth aggressively rather than reaching for extraction-and-implant.

The exception: aggressive periodontitis (now stage IV grade C) — where rapid downhill progression of teeth makes implants the only viable long-term scaffold for prosthodontic stability.

Slide 13 · Short implants

Short implants are no longer "short"

Twenty-five years ago, the data was clear: 7 mm implants in the posterior maxilla had ~25–30% failure rates. The longer the implant, the higher the success rate. That data drove a generation of sinus lifts and complex grafting.

Then implant surfaces changed. Machined → micro-roughened (sandblasted, acid-etched, dual acid-etched). The surface area increased, the cellular response improved, and the relationship with length collapsed.

Modern findings

  • 4 mm "ultra-short" implants in the posterior jaw match longer implants for survival in trials.
  • Higher crown-to-implant ratio is paradoxically associated with less marginal bone loss.
  • The decision to graft and lengthen is no longer automatic — short can work.
Lesson: textbook risk factors have a shelf life. What was true in 1995 may be obsolete in 2025. Always read the surface chemistry and the publication date.
Slide 14 · Patient red flags

Red flags — the patients who will hurt you

In the lecturer's medical-legal caseload, certain patient profiles recur. None of these alone is a contraindication, but combinations should make you pause.

Aesthetics is the main reason for treatment "I just know you can do it" Pulls their lips back to show you the problem Has a hard deadline (wedding, holiday) A staff member dislikes them History of psychiatric illness or self-harm Wants the cheapest option but the best result Has shopped multiple clinicians already Won't bring family or partner to discussion Argues with the assistant before meeting you Wants to use the plan as a menu — picks and chooses
Two real cases: A 38-year-old single parent with three suicide attempts had cosmetic bonding sold to her on a sales call, used her life savings, and was unhappy with the result. A 70-year-old type-2 diabetic on anticoagulants signed an 8-page consent on the day of surgery and was hospitalised for hypovolaemic shock 12 days later. Both settled for tens of thousands. Both should never have been treated.
Slide 15 · Read the patient

Read the patient — not just the X-rays

Norman Friedman first described the apically positioned flap in 1962. When he was invited to give a career-spanning lecture decades later, he spoke not about flap design but about patient body language. At the time, the audience was disappointed. In retrospect, it was the most important lesson he could have taught.

Body language signals to learn

  • Where do they sit in the waiting room? How do they greet your reception staff?
  • Do they make eye contact, or look at the floor / their phone?
  • Do they crowd you when speaking, or hold themselves back?
  • Do they ask realistic questions, or lecture you on what they want?
  • Do they listen — or wait for you to finish so they can speak?
In the cosmetic dentistry capitals of the 1970s and 80s, the very best clinicians were known for screening patients out before they ever picked up a handpiece. The first appointment was an audition — both ways.
Slide 16 · Same-day teeth

Same-day teeth: an artificial expectation

Same-day teeth are not a treatment concept demanded by patients. They are an artificially created concept developed by dentists for financial and marketing reasons, then sold back to patients as if it were what they had always wanted.

The problem: Patients who go straight from extraction to immediate restoration to definitive bridge — without ever wearing a temporary denture — tend to be more critical of the final result. They have nothing to compare it to.
The solution: A period in a well-made temporary denture builds appreciation. They suffer a little, then they love the final result. Make the temporary thick — don't let them like it too much.
Errors in haste, repent at leisure. Aggressive same-day marketing has produced a generation of dissatisfied patients and a steady pipeline of medical-legal cases. Most of the time, slower is better.
Slide 17 · The litigation iceberg

The litigation iceberg

There are several chances along the way to head off a lawsuit. The visible negligence is the tip — most of the iceberg sits underwater as quiet dissatisfaction that could have been resolved with a phone call.
Slide 18 · Why patients sue

Why patients actually sue

It is rarely the clinical error

A famous Los Angeles case: a dentist removing a failed subperiosteal implant cut all four major vessels to the premaxilla. The premaxilla sloughed. The patient did not sue. Asked why, she said: "He's such a nice man. He keeps calling to see how I am."

It is the response

Patients sue when they feel ignored, dismissed, lied to or belittled. They do not sue when they feel cared for — even after catastrophic outcomes.

One simple secret: phone the patient that evening. Wait until half-time of the football match (otherwise they call you in the 85th minute). Average call length is about 75 seconds. It almost never costs you anything and it heads off the majority of complaints.

If you make a mistake: acknowledge it, apologise, fix it. Do not deny, do not belittle, do not blame somebody else. Patients will not sue if they think you are going to fix it.
Slide 19 · Non-defensible errors

Errors that are simply not defensible

Some clinical errors will lose any case in court. They are characterised by failure to act on information that was available at the time.

  • Implant contacting an adjacent tooth. Visible on the post-op radiograph. Inexcusable not to act.
  • Watching and waiting for post-operative neuropathic symptoms to resolve. If you can remove an implant touching the inferior dental nerve within 36–48 hours, sensation may return. Vitamin B and waiting will not help.
  • Cantilevers too long. The textbook maximum is ~14 mm. Some cases litigated involved 25 mm cantilevers — the implant nearest the cantilever fractures because the lever multiplies the load.
  • Excess cement. A leading cause of late peri-implantitis. Remove excess cement at delivery — every time.
  • Treating the saveable tooth as if it were unsaveable. Implants are not an alternative to a tooth that could have been saved. This is the single most common dento-legal theme.
Slide 20 · Communication & consent

Consent that actually counts

Consent is not a signed form. Consent is a documented conversation in which the patient understood the options, the risks, the alternatives, and chose freely.

Elements that matter

  • Clear written and verbal communication.
  • Time to reflect — not signed on the day of surgery under pressure.
  • Realistic, balanced presentation — both the advantages and disadvantages of each option.
  • Documentation of what was said, when, and what the patient said back.
Common failure: A consent document for an All-on-X case that lists every disadvantage of dentures and every advantage of implants — but none of the disadvantages of implants and none of the advantages of dentures. That is not consent. That is sales.
Slide 21 · Records & templates

Records, templates & honesty

If it isn't in the records, it didn't happen. Court will not accept "I always do that" as evidence.

The template trap

Pre-written templates are convenient but dangerous. Real cases the lecturer has reviewed:

  • Composite placed in a screw-retained implant bridge — patient noted as "advised of sensitivity from composite". Implants do not get sensitive from composite. The template was a copy-paste from another case.
  • Patient "advised of risk of inferior dental nerve damage" — for an upper central incisor. The IDN does not run anywhere near an upper central. The template betrayed the lack of personalised consent.
Rule: If you use templates, customise them every single time to the specific tooth, the specific patient, and the specific risks of that case. Generic notes can actively undermine your defence.
Slide 22 · The problem-list method

The problem-list method of treatment planning

"Now develop a treatment plan" is the worst instruction in dental school. All the data in the world isn't a treatment plan — it's a list of findings. The problem list is the bridge between findings and a plan.

How a problem list is built

  1. List every finding that requires a separate course of action.
  2. Each finding is a numbered line item.
  3. Mark each as Active or Inactive.
  4. An active problem must be addressed within the present treatment plan.
  5. An inactive problem is a deviation from normal that needs monitoring but not active treatment now.
Worked example: A patient with two carious lesions, generalised gingivitis, an unerupted asymptomatic third molar, and an over-erupted opposing tooth might generate four problems: two active (caries × 2 + gingivitis), one inactive (third molar to monitor), and one active (the over-erupted tooth — because you cannot fit an implant crown opposing it without addressing the over-eruption).
Slide 23 · Active vs inactive

Active vs inactive — how to decide

Active problem

  • Causes symptoms or harm.
  • Will worsen if untreated within the planning horizon.
  • Blocks another planned treatment from succeeding.
  • Patient is unhappy with it (aesthetic / functional).

Examples: caries, periodontal disease, missing teeth the patient wants replaced, parafunction in a planned restorative case, mobile teeth.

Inactive problem

  • Stable on serial review.
  • Not causing symptoms.
  • Not interfering with planned treatment.
  • Patient not concerned about it.

Examples: impacted asymptomatic wisdom tooth, long-standing diastema the patient likes, midline asymmetry the patient doesn't notice, healed root-treated tooth.

An inactive problem can become active later. Document it on the list and on the recall — that is your audit trail.
Slide 24 · From problems to plan

From the problem list to the treatment plan

Once the problem list is complete, draft the treatment plan and then go back and check that every active problem is addressed. This single discipline catches an enormous number of oversights.

The matrix workflow

#ProblemA/ITreatment item
1Missing UL2AResin-bonded bridge or implant
2Peg lateral UR2AComposite build-up or veneer
3Diastema mesial UL1ALimited orthodontics
4Wear facets caninesAComposite restorations + night guard

Every active item gets a treatment. Every inactive item gets a monitoring plan. Nothing is left orphaned.

Slide 25 · Patient letters

Communicating the plan in lay terms

The lecturer's standard approach is to write a letter to the patient after the consultation. The structure:

  1. Pleasure to meet you. Open warmly and recap their chief complaint in their own words.
  2. List the problems — translated into lay language. "Missing left lateral incisor — the small tooth next to the front tooth."
  3. State the treatment plan in plain English, sequenced.
  4. Caveats — what we cannot fix, what may go wrong, what depends on healing.
  5. Estimated timeline and costs.
  6. Invitation to ask questions before agreeing to proceed.
This letter is the consent process. It gives the patient time to reflect, share with family, and come back with questions. It also creates a contemporaneous record that protects both parties.
Slide 26 · Aesthetic risk & smile line

Aesthetic risk assessment

Some aesthetic situations are simply harder than others. Recognising them early is the only way to manage expectations honestly.

High aesthetic risk

  • High smile line (gum visible).
  • Thin gingival biotype.
  • Adjacent crowns or restorations.
  • Bone/soft tissue deficit at the site.
  • Asymmetry between sides.
  • Patient with very high aesthetic expectations.

Lower aesthetic risk

  • Low smile line (gum not shown).
  • Thick gingival biotype.
  • Healthy adjacent teeth.
  • Adequate bone and soft tissue.
  • Symmetric starting point.
  • Patient with realistic expectations.
The Mayo Clinic scale: Plastic surgeons there use a simple linear scale — the patient marks where they currently are (0 = ugliest, 100 = most beautiful) and where they hope to get to. The surgeon does the same independently. If the surgeon cannot meet the patient's hopes, the surgeon does not treat. If the patient's hopes are realistic and the surgeon thinks they can be exceeded, treatment proceeds.
Slide 27 · Knowledge check

Quick self-check

A patient on long-term citalopram (an SSRI) wants implants. What is your approach?
Treat — but document. SSRIs are associated with roughly 2–3× the failure rate. This is a consent issue, not an exclusion. Note the medication, explain the elevated risk in writing, and confirm acceptance. Liaise with the GP only if dose changes are clinically reasonable; do not stop antidepressants for a dental procedure.
Why is "watch and wait" the wrong approach to post-operative inferior dental nerve symptoms after implant placement?
Because the window of reversibility is very short. If an implant in contact with the inferior dental nerve is removed within 36–48 hours, sensation may return. After that, you have lost the chance. Vitamin B is not a treatment.
What is the maximum textbook cantilever length for an implant bridge — and what was wrong with the litigated case described in the lecture?
Approximately 14 mm is the textbook maximum. The litigated case used 25 mm cantilevers — almost double. The implant nearest the cantilever became the pivot and fractured. The case settled for £50,000 without going to court.
Why phone the patient on the evening of surgery?
Because it heads off the majority of complaints before they start. Average call length is about 75 seconds. Patients who feel cared for do not sue. Wait until half-time of the football, otherwise they will call you in the 85th minute.
What is the difference between an active and an inactive problem on a problem list?
An active problem must be addressed within the current treatment plan — it is symptomatic, harmful, will worsen, or blocks other treatment. An inactive problem is a deviation from normal that needs monitoring but not active treatment right now (e.g. asymptomatic impacted wisdom tooth).
Why are short implants no longer the risk factor they used to be?
Because implant surfaces have moved from machined to micro-roughened (sandblasted, acid-etched). The increased surface area improves the cellular response, and modern data shows 4 mm implants matching longer ones for survival. The 1990s data on length is now obsolete.
Slide 28 · Take-home messages

Take-home messages from Lecture 3

🎯 The big ideas

  • Plan the case, not the space.
  • Read the patient — body language matters more than radiographs.
  • Risk factors evolve — what was true in 1995 may be wrong now.
  • Implants are not an alternative to a saveable tooth.
  • Most lawsuits are about communication, not clinical skill.

🛠 The clinical habits

  • Build a problem list on every complex case.
  • Mark each problem A or I; address every A.
  • Send a plan letter in lay terms after consultation.
  • Customise templates — never copy-paste consent.
  • Phone post-op patients the same evening.
  • Six weeks to learn how to do an implant. Ten years to learn when not to.
"It takes me six weeks to teach you how to do surgery, and two years to teach you when not to."
— Walter Cohen, University of Pennsylvania
