Patient Selection & Treatment Planning for Implant Dentistry

Lecturer: Dr Sarah Jawad — Consultant in Restorative Dentistry, Guy's & St Thomas' NHS Foundation Trust / King's College London

This first lecture lays the strategic foundation for every implant case. Before any drill touches bone, we have to know who the patient is, what they want, what they expect, what they can tolerate medically and behaviourally, and where the teeth ultimately need to be. Slide 2 · Defining success

What does "success" mean in implant dentistry?

Ask three different people — surgeon, restorative dentist, patient — and you will get three different answers. Most academic papers report survival, which simply means the implant is still in the mouth. That is not the same as a happy patient with a working tooth.

Key insight: "Survival" includes implants with pus draining around them and bone disappearing. We need a richer language than "still there".

Slide 3 · The outcome hierarchy

Five tiers of outcome — and who cares about each

Lesson: When we describe results to patients, lead with the top of the pyramid — "you'll be able to chew comfortably and smile confidently." They do not care about peri-implant bone levels until something goes wrong.
Slide 4 · The human cost of tooth loss

Tooth loss & quality of life

What we know

  • Tooth loss is consistently linked to reduced oral health-related quality of life.
  • Impairment worsens with the extent and severity of tooth loss.
  • Location matters: losing a wisdom tooth barely registers; losing an upper central incisor is devastating.
  • Patients seeking implants report improved QoL after placement — largely because expectations were met.

The trauma of extraction

Almost every gap your implant patient presents with represents a previous loss event — and an emotional one. Imagine being told right now that your lower six is coming out. You would be upset before, during and after.

Take-home: If you take only one thing from this lecture: there is no replacement for a natural tooth. An implant comes close — but it is not the same.
Slide 5 · Removable vs fixed

Does our treatment actually restore quality of life?

Removable prostheses: Around 30–50% are not worn. They are only worn when they offer a clear aesthetic or functional gain. A poorly tolerated denture can actually worsen QoL.
Fixed prostheses: Improve QoL meaningfully — but never return the patient to the level of an intact natural dentition. Acceptance & adaptation are what convert a denture into "theirs".
Slide 6 · The first conversation

Listen to the complaint — really listen

Nothing has changed since dental school: presenting complaint → history → dental history → motivation → expectations. The structure is the same. The depth is what changes when you are planning implants.

Six questions that change everything

Why are you here today, in your own words?
Tells you the patient's chief concern — not the gap you can see, but the problem they feel. A patient who says "I can't smile in photos any more" is a different patient from one who says "I can't chew on the right side."
Why now? What changed?
Reveals the trigger — a new partner, a job, a wedding, a denture finally giving up. The trigger often dictates the timeline and the emotional stakes.
What have you been told about implants already?
Surfaces social-media myths, neighbour anecdotes and prior consultations you will have to gently re-set.
What does success look like to you?
If their answer is "perfect Hollywood teeth in two weeks", you know you have an expectations problem before you have a clinical problem.
What would make you walk away unhappy?
A more powerful question than the previous one — it surfaces deal-breakers (cost, time, multiple visits, dentures during healing).
Who else is involved in this decision?
Spouse, parent, employer — the people whose opinions will shape the patient's eventual satisfaction.
Slide 7 · Rapport

Building rapport — the under-rated clinical skill

Rapport is not the same as being friendly. It is the deliberate calibration of how you speak to this particular human in front of you. Two patients with the identical clinical picture may need wildly different communication styles.

Calibrating to the person

  • Match their vocabulary — don't say "edentulous" to a builder, don't say "no teeth" to a barrister.
  • Mirror their pace — anxious patients need slower delivery and more checking-in.
  • Use their words back to them: if they call it "the wobbly one", that is what you call it.

Why it matters for outcomes

  • Patients who feel heard accept longer, staged plans.
  • Trust early = fewer complaints late.
  • Rapport is the single biggest predictor of perceived success — independent of the radiographs.
Slide 8 · Expectations

Setting expectations before any treatment is offered

An expectation, once formed, is very hard to revise downward. If a patient walks in believing implants are quick, painless and permanent, the work of unpicking that belief has to happen at the consultation — not at the cementation.

The spectrum of expectations

Red flag: "I just know you can do it" — when the patient is more confident in you than you are in yourself. They will swing 180° when something goes wrong.
Slide 9 · Perceived value

"Why is dentistry so expensive?"

Patients will spend thousands on cars, holidays, kitchens, glasses and clothes without flinching — but flinch at a four-figure restorative quote. The honest answer is that dentistry suffers from a perception problem, not an actual value problem.

What we are actually selling

  • Years of training and ongoing CPD.
  • Strict regulation, GDC oversight and liability.
  • Sterile environments, materials science, lab work.
  • Time, attention and continuity of care.

How to frame value

  • Compare to other regulated professions, not to entertainment spend.
  • Itemise the plan so the patient sees what each fee buys.
  • Be transparent about why "Turkey teeth" carry hidden costs.
  • Talk about the cost of failure — not just the cost of treatment.
Slide 10 · Sources of expectation

Where do patients get their ideas from?

📱 Social media

TikTok, Instagram and YouTube show finished cases at six weeks — never the failures, never the maintenance. Patients arrive expecting that timeline.

👥 Anecdote

"My neighbour had hers done in a day." Single-case reports from non-clinicians become the patient's evidence base.

✈️ Dental tourism

Glossy clinics in Turkey, Hungary and beyond promise full-mouth rehabilitations in a week. The cost is real; so are the failures we see returning.

📰 Old textbooks

Some patients quote success rates from papers that are 20 years out of date. Numbers without context can be more dangerous than no numbers at all.

🏥 Previous clinicians

A previous consult may have over-promised or under-delivered. You may be inheriting an expectation you didn't create.

🎯 Their own desire

The most powerful source of all. Patients hear what they want to hear. Repeat key information back at the next visit.

Slide 11 · Medical history

Red flags & relevant systems review

A useful framework is to ask three questions about every medical condition:

  • Will this affect the management of the patient? (Will they collapse in the chair? Cardiovascular risk, ASA grade.)
  • Will this affect implant placement? (Bleeding tendency, anticoagulants, immune compromise.)
  • Will this affect implant survival? (Diabetes, radiotherapy, bisphosphonates, antidepressants, PPIs.)
The red flags paper: A useful summary exists in the literature listing the conditions clinicians should slow down for. Photograph the abstract — you will refer back to it.

Slide 13 · Smoking

Smoking — the dose-dependent killer of implants

Smoking is the most studied — and most under-acted-on — risk factor in implant dentistry. The data is unambiguous and the relationship is dose-dependent.

15.8%
Implant failure in smokers (overall)
10%
Failure in light/medium smokers
30.8%
Failure with >20 cigarettes/day
The NHS rule: NHS-funded implant treatment requires patients to be cigarette-free for at least six months. Limited public resource should not subsidise predictable failure.
For private patients: You cannot refuse, but you must inform. Document the conversation. Document the increased maintenance burden. Document that the patient accepted the risk in writing.
Slide 14 · Beyond cigarettes

Vaping, shisha, smokeless tobacco — what we know and don't

💨 Vaping / e-cigarettes

Increasingly common, especially in younger patients. Periodontal evidence is mounting — vapers respond less well to non-surgical therapy, with more sites >5 mm and more need for surgery. The implant-specific evidence base is still maturing but mechanistic data already shows interleukin patterns similar to tobacco.

Treat as smokers until proven otherwise

🌿 Shisha & hookah

Patients who smoke shisha rarely identify themselves as "smokers". The periodontal harm is well documented; peri-implant data is essentially absent. Ask the question explicitly.

Ask, document, counsel

🚬 Cigars

Less frequent inhalation than cigarettes but high local exposure. Peri-implant data is sparse. Apply the same caution as for cigarettes.

Treat as smokers

🟢 Betel quid & gutka

Common in South Asian populations and frequently combined with tobacco. Strong oral cancer association; peri-implant evidence is essentially absent. Counsel and screen.

Counsel + cancer screening
Slide 15 · Alcohol

Alcohol & bone — what the rabbits taught us

A famous experimental study fed dose-dependent alcohol to rabbits and looked at osseointegration. Two findings stood out:

  • Liver impact → bleeding tendency — indirect surgical risk.
  • Direct bone effect — significantly less bone density and reduced direct bone-to-implant contact in alcoholic rabbits. Polyfluorochrome-labelled bone areas were significantly reduced in the alcohol group compared to controls.
Mild consumption: Some studies suggest a paradoxical protective effect against late failures — possibly anti-inflammatory.
Heavy consumption: A roughly 200% increase in long-term failure. Ask about units per week; do not rely on the patient's self-perception.
Slide 16 · NHS eligibility

Who qualifies for NHS implants in the UK?

NHS-funded dental implants are reserved for specific clinical groups. Eligibility is also a "postcode lottery" — different dental hospitals and commissioning groups have slightly different acceptance criteria.

Generally eligible

  • Cleft lip and palate patients — congenital absence and hard-tissue deficits.
  • Head & neck cancer patients post-surgery / radiotherapy.
  • Hypodontia — significant developmental absence of teeth.
  • Major trauma with hard and soft tissue loss.

Generally not eligible

  • Routine adult tooth loss from caries or periodontal disease.
  • Active smokers (six-month cessation rule applies).
  • Aesthetic-only requests without functional indication.
Slide 17 · Three patient archetypes

Three groups you will plan very differently

1️⃣ Fully edentulous

No teeth, possibly an existing denture. The challenge is finding reference points for where the new teeth should go. Quality of life is already low — almost any improvement is welcome.

2️⃣ Partially dentate with gaps

Some teeth, some spaces. The decision is whether to fill the gaps, take more teeth out, or restore existing teeth before filling gaps. Treatment plan is genuinely a choice between several valid options.

3️⃣ Dentate but failing

All teeth still present, but the dentition is deteriorating — caries, perio, fractures. Stabilisation comes first; restoration comes later. The hardest group to plan because the trajectory is uncertain.

The unifying principle: The best implant patient is a stable implant patient. Whichever group your patient is in, the first job is to stabilise — not to drill.
Slide 18 · The edentulous patient

Planning when there are no teeth left

The fundamental problem with the fully edentulous patient is that there is no anatomical reference for where the new teeth should sit. Without a denture, the bone is not a reliable guide. Drill where the bone is best and you may end up with teeth in the wrong place — facially unsupported, prosthetically uncleansable, phonetically distorted.

Slide 19 · Why we need a denture first

"I'm not here for a denture" — but we need to make one anyway

Patients arriving for implants often resist the idea of a removable temporary stage. The job at consultation is to explain why the denture is not the destination — it is the planning tool.

What the denture gives you

  • AP (antero-posterior) tooth position — where the incisal edge sits relative to the lip.
  • Vertical dimension — the OVD that will support the new prosthesis.
  • Horizontal arch form — symmetry and curvature.
  • Lip support — how much pink the patient needs underneath the upper lip.
  • Phonetics — F, V, S sounds depend on incisal edge position and palatal contour.
  • Aesthetic preview — the patient sees the result before any irreversible step.
Practical: If the patient has an old denture they hate, ask them to bring it. You can either copy it and modify, or rebase the existing one. An old set you no longer wear is gold dust for planning.
Slide 20 · The flange try-in test

The flange-off try-in — preventing aesthetic disaster

This is one of the most useful clinical tests in edentulous planning. The flange of a denture provides facial support that an implant-borne fixed prosthesis cannot recreate. If the patient has been edentulous for years, removing that flange will cause the lip to sink in and the face to age.

The protocol

  1. Copy or duplicate the existing (or new) denture.
  2. Trim the labial flange off completely.
  3. Try it in. Show the patient in the mirror.
  4. Ask: "This is what your face will look like with fixed teeth on implants. Is that acceptable to you?"
Patient happy: Proceed with planning a fixed implant restoration. Use the duplicated denture as the radiographic and surgical guide.
Patient unhappy: Discuss an implant-retained removable overdenture instead. Pink acrylic in a removable prosthesis can recreate the lip support a fixed bridge cannot.

Slide 22 · Stability first

The "stable patient" principle

Before you place a single implant, the patient should be stable. The best implant patient is a stable implant patient. Stabilisation buys you three things:

⏱
Time to observe behaviour change
🦠
Reduced bacterial reservoirs
🎯
Clearer view of true restorability
Patients who do not engage with diet, oral hygiene and basic perio control are not going to engage with implant maintenance either. Use the stabilisation phase as a behavioural test as much as a clinical one.
Slide 23 · Predicting tooth loss

We are not very good at predicting which teeth will be lost

A long-running observation: dentists asked to predict which teeth would be lost over 20+ years are frequently wrong. Teeth they wrote off are still there; teeth they thought would survive have gone. The dominant variable turns out to be patient factors: hygiene, diet, engagement and immune response.

Hirschfeld & Wasserman: 17% of "questionable" teeth in well-maintained patients were eventually lost. In "extreme downhill" patients, almost all questionable teeth were lost. The patient phenotype matters more than the tooth.
Implication: Don't bet the case on a single tooth's prognosis. Stabilise, observe behaviour, then re-treatment-plan with fresh eyes. The patient at six months is not the same patient as the one who walked in.
Slide 24 · Knowledge check

Quick self-check

Tap each card to reveal the answer.

A patient who smokes 25 cigarettes a day asks about implants. What is the published failure rate range, and what is your conversation?
Around 30%+ failure at >20/day — roughly double the rate of light smokers. NHS would refuse until 6 months cigarette-free. Privately, you must inform fully, document, and emphasise increased maintenance and risk of late failure. Offer cessation support; some patients quit purely because the implant gave them a reason.
An edentulous patient hates her denture and demands "fixed teeth like my friend got". What is the very first clinical step?
Make (or copy) a denture, remove the labial flange, try it in, and let her see in a mirror what her face will look like without flange support. If she rejects the look, switch the conversation to an implant-retained removable overdenture rather than a fixed bridge.
Approximately what proportion of removable prostheses are not actually worn by their owners?
30–50%. They are only consistently worn when they offer a clear aesthetic or functional gain. This is part of the reason implant-retained options can dramatically improve acceptance.
List the three "lenses" through which to assess every medical condition before implant treatment.
(1) Will it affect management of the patient (collapse, cardiovascular event)? (2) Will it affect implant placement (bleeding, infection)? (3) Will it affect implant survival (diabetes, radiotherapy, bisphosphonates, antidepressants)?
Which patient groups have NHS eligibility for implants in the UK?
Cleft lip/palate, head & neck cancer, hypodontia and major trauma with hard/soft tissue loss. Subject to a regional postcode lottery and not for routine adult tooth loss.
Slide 25 · Take-home messages

Key take-home messages from Lecture 1

🎯 The big ideas

  • There is no replacement for a natural tooth.
  • Patients judge by quality of life — not by radiographs.
  • Teeth drive the implants — never the other way.
  • The best implant patient is a stable patient.

🛠 The clinical habits

  • Always do a flange-off try-in before promising fixed.
  • Run every medical condition through the three-lens test.
  • Document every smoking conversation in writing.
  • Treat the patient — not the gap.
