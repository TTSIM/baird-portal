Why Flap Design is Everything
The most important single surgical decision you will make

Wrong flap design causes the most embarrassing outcomes in implant dentistry. Failing implants are repairable — wrong flaps that cause recession on neighbouring teeth are not easily corrected.

The Worst Outcome

Patient comes for an implant. You use the wrong flap and cause recession or black triangles on the adjacent natural teeth. The patient says: "I came to fix one tooth and you ruined the others." This is career-damaging and very difficult to correct.

Failed Implant vs Wrong Flap

A failed implant is manageable — even reputable manufacturers replace failed implants. But tissue damage from poor flap design leaves permanent aesthetic consequences. Manufacturers cannot fix your flap choices.

The Solution

A scientific, evidence-based decision framework — not Instagram trends, coffee-and-habit choices, or copying what you saw on a weekend course. One size does NOT fit all. Every case demands individual analysis.

"One size does not fit all"
The principle that governs every flap design decision

Three Questions Framework
The scientific method for designing every flap

Break down every flap design into answers to these three questions. Answer them in order — the answers define your design completely.

Q1
Raise this papilla — or preserve it?
Use the Four Yeses system (next slides). If you get one NO: preserve. If four YESes: raise. This is the single most important question.
Q2
How big does my flap need to be?
Simple implant only: 1 unit each side. Implant + moderate GBR: 2 units each side. Complex GBR / vertical augmentation: consider extending further. Match size to surgical need.
Q3
Do I need vertical lines — and where do I place them?
If no GBR: envelope may suffice. If vertical lines needed: Golf Club technique rules apply. Three factors: smile line, keratinized vs mobile mucosa, root convexity position.
The Principle

Answer Q1 for each papilla individually — they can have different answers on the same case. Mix and match based on the biology of each specific anatomical site.

The Four Yeses System
Tap YES or NO on each factor — see if this papilla can be safely elevated

All four questions must be YES to safely raise a papilla. One NO = preserve it. Apply to each papilla individually.

Most Important: Mesial/Distal Bone Sounding
Bone sound the papilla base. Distance from mucosa crest to bone: ≤3mm = favourable (YES). >3mm = unfavourable (NO — stop here, preserve papilla regardless of other factors).
✓ Favourable — Continue to Q2
✗ STOP — Preserve this papilla. Bone cannot support the papilla coronal to 3mm. If elevated, it will not return.
2nd: Mid-Labial Bone Sounding of Adjacent Teeth
Bone sound the labial aspect of the neighbouring tooth. ≤3mm = YES. >3mm = NO. If unfavourable: recession on the neighbour will result from flap elevation even if the papilla itself survives.
✓ Favourable — Continue to Q3
✗ STOP — Raising flap here will cause mid-labial recession on the adjacent tooth.
3rd: Who Is My Neighbour?
Is the adjacent tooth/restoration healthy? Good crown with no leak = YES. Leaking crown / inflamed tissue = NO. Adjacent implant = NO (papilla = scar tissue, no blood supply from PDL).
✓ Continue to Q4
✗ STOP — Compromised neighbour means the papilla has poor healing capacity. Preserve.
Least Important: Phenotype
Thick phenotype = YES (fibrous, vascular, good healing). Thin phenotype = NO (fragile, minimal blood supply, high recession risk).
✓ FOUR YESES — Safe to elevate this papilla. Predictable recovery expected.
✗ Even with 3 other YESes: thin phenotype papilla is at risk. Consider preserving.

Bone Sounding Technique
The essential pre-surgical examination that guides flap design

Bone sounding is the measurement of distance from the soft tissue crest to the underlying bone by penetrating through the junctional epithelium with a probe.

Phenotype Assessment (No Anaesthesia)

Insert probe 1mm into sulcus — check if metal shadow visible through tissue. No anaesthesia needed. This only probes the tissue, not through it.

Bone Sounding (Anaesthesia Required)

Probe penetrates through junctional epithelium and connective tissue to contact bone. Patient must be anaesthetised. Measure from tissue crest to bone: ≤3mm = favourable, >3mm = unfavourable.

Two Sites to Measure

1. Mesial/distal papilla — tests whether papilla will survive elevation
2. Mid-labial of adjacent teeth — tests whether recession will occur on neighbouring teeth

CBCT as Alternative

CBCT can show bone levels and soft tissue thickness. Mesial/distal bony peaks visible on periapical radiograph. For complex cases: plan flap design from CBCT before surgery, refine at chairside.

Favourable vs Unfavourable Bone Sounding
Understanding why the 3mm threshold is biologically significant

The 3mm threshold is not arbitrary — it reflects the biological width principle. Bone can only supply blood to approximately 3mm of soft tissue above it.

Favourable — ≤3mm
  • After elevation, biological width re-establishes at same or similar level
  • Bone can supply blood to the full papilla height
  • Papilla heals and returns to original position
  • Safe to elevate — predictable result
Unfavourable — >3mm
  • The coronal tissue above 3mm from bone = minimal blood supply
  • Appears as papilla but is essentially scar tissue with poor vascularity
  • After elevation: biological width re-establishes at 3mm from bone
  • Coronal 2mm+ has no blood supply → necrosis → recession
Clinical Insight — Don't Be Deceived by Appearance

A papilla that looks healthy and full may have >3mm bone sounding. It looks like a papilla because it was never disturbed. The moment you elevate it, its apparent height will be lost. You cannot see the bone sounding without probing — probe before you plan.

The Most Common Clinical Mistake

Forgetting bone sounding on the mid-labial of adjacent teeth. This leads to recession on the neighbouring tooth — as experienced in the case where a carefully preserved implant outcome was ruined by unexpected recession on a lateral incisor.

The Neighbour Rule
Why the quality of adjacent teeth determines papilla fate

The neighbour determines blood supply to the papilla. A compromised neighbour means a compromised papilla — regardless of phenotype or bone sounding.

Good Neighbour

Healthy natural tooth with intact periodontal ligament. PDL provides blood supply to papilla. Well-seated, non-leaking crown. Papilla is genuinely vascularised and will heal after elevation.

Bad Neighbour

Leaking or poorly-designed crown. Inflamed periapical tissue. Inflammatory cells present in the papilla → reduced healing capacity. Elevating this papilla risks lasting recession on the compromised crown as well.

Implant Neighbour

Always NO. No PDL → no blood supply to papilla from the implant side. What appears to be a healthy papilla between two implants is scar tissue. Elevate it and you will lose it. Even if it was a perfectly placed implant.

Lesson from Clinical Experience

Case: placed implant in 2009, came back in 2012 to place adjacent implant. Thought "let me check my old bone graft" — raised a big flap, disturbed the papilla next to the old implant. Lost the papilla permanently. Same mistake that the four-yeses system was created to prevent. If the neighbour is an implant — always use papillary-preserving flap.

Phenotype & Flap Design
How tissue type guides your incision choices
Thick Phenotype — Flap Strategy
  • Papillae: robust, recover well after elevation
  • Envelope flap: safe — papillae will return
  • If vertical lines needed: expect visible scarring (acceptable vs black triangles)
  • Tell patient pre-operatively about scar risk
  • Scarring will fade over 5+ years
  • Priority: avoid black triangles > avoid scars
Thin Phenotype — Flap Strategy
  • Papillae: fragile, high recession risk
  • Papillary-preserving flap strongly preferred
  • Vertical lines: actually less of a problem (minimal scarring)
  • High risk: black triangles after papilla elevation
  • Priority: preserve papillae at all costs
  • If vertical lines needed: place them away from implant
Scarring vs Black Triangles — Which Is Worse?

Vertical scars in a high-smile-line patient: uncomfortable and visible, but will fade over 3–5 years. Black triangles: permanent, aesthetically devastating, extremely difficult to correct. When forced to choose — scars are preferable to black triangles. Always inform patient before surgery.

Extent of the Flap
Matching flap size to the surgical procedure — no more, no less

The second of the three questions. The flap must be large enough for the procedure but never unnecessarily large — each papilla disturbed is a risk.

Simple Implant (No GBR)

• 1 unit each side of implant site
• Minimum papillae elevated
• May use flapless approach if bone adequate
• Two sutures can close a minimal flap
• Don't over-extend "just to see"

GBR / Bone Augmentation

• 2 units each side — minimum
• Larger flap ensures membrane can be placed away from flap margins
• Flap margin must rest on vital bone — not on graft material
• Better vision = better GBR technique = better outcomes

GBR Flap Closure Principle

After GBR, the "belly" under the flap is larger — like a big shirt trying to close. You need: (1) bigger flap for more tissue, (2) periosteal release for stretch. The membrane must be completely covered by the flap without gaps.

Flapless Approach

When: simple implant, adequate bone, no GBR required, no aesthetic tissue concerns. Uses a tissue punch to expose bone. Zero flap = zero papilla disruption = zero recession risk. Best outcome but limited visibility and only appropriate for ideal bone cases.

Vertical Incisions — The Rules
Three factors govern where and how vertical lines should be placed

If you need a vertical releasing incision, three factors determine where to place it and whether to use a truly vertical or oblique line:

Factor 1: Smile Line

High smile line = visible scars. Use envelope flap where possible. If vertical lines unavoidable: place in less visible positions. Low smile line = more forgiving; scars not cosmetically significant.

Factor 2: Mucosa Type

No horizontal lines in keratinized (attached) mucosa — these scar badly. No true vertical lines in mobile mucosa — these also scar worse. At the MGJ: transition from vertical to oblique/curved as you cross into mobile mucosa.

Factor 3: Root Convexity

Never place a vertical incision over the maximum convexity of a root. This is the guaranteed position for mid-labial recession. Scars over convexities pull open under tension from the curved bone surface beneath.

The Scarring Rules Summary

✗ No horizontal lines in attached mucosa
✗ No true vertical lines in mobile mucosa
✗ No vertical lines over maximum root convexity
✓ Oblique transition at MGJ when going into mobile mucosa
✓ Vertical lines on flat surfaces between root convexities

Vertical Incision — Position Options
Comparing incision positions — from worst to best
❌ Worst — Splitting the Papilla

0/10. Cuts through the papilla base — the vascular supply. Guaranteed papilla loss, worst possible scarring, permanent aesthetic damage. Never do this.

❌ Poor — Base of Papilla (Yellow)

2/10. Still encroaches on papilla blood supply. Field too small. Suture under high tension from papilla pull. Avoid.

✗ Suboptimal — Mid-Labial (Green)

5/10. Over the maximum root convexity → mid-labial recession guaranteed. Common "textbook" position — actually wrong. Avoid.

✗ Acceptable — Traditional (Blue)

7/10. Away from papilla, often what older textbooks teach. Still may be over some convexity. Acceptable but not optimal.

✓ Best — Golf Club Position

10/10. On the flat surface between roots. Away from papilla base. Away from maximum convexity. Minimal tension on sutures. Scar hidden in the "crease" between roots. The correct choice.

The Golf Club Technique
Full anatomy of the incision — vertical component + oblique extension

The Golf Club incision has two components: a vertical line and an oblique extension. Together they form the "golf club" shape. Both must be correctly placed.

1
Vertical component — on the flat surface between two roots, at the middle of the mesio-distal width of the papilla base. Away from maximum root convexity. ≥3mm from implant margin and papilla base.
2
Oblique extension — from the base of the papilla, an oblique line runs toward the mesial tooth, parallel to the mesial curvature of the posterior tooth. This is the "club head" of the golf club shape.
3
Leave a minimum 3mm island of papilla behind the oblique line for easier suturing and adequate vascularisation of the retained tissue.
4
Cut toward yourself. At the MGJ: transition to oblique/curved — never continue straight vertical into mobile mucosa.
The Golf Club Shape

Vertical line (shaft) → at the flat MD midpoint between roots. Oblique extension (club head) → from papilla base toward mesial tooth, following the curvature of the adjacent posterior tooth. Result: minimal papilla disturbance, 3mm tissue island, scar in crease between roots.

Why the Oblique Extension?

A purely vertical line reaching the crestal incision would split the papilla base — the worst outcome. The oblique extension redirects the incision away from the papilla vascular supply and toward the mesial tooth where tissue is more robust.

Remote Palatal Incision
Five advantages of a palatally-offset crestal incision

Instead of a mid-crestal incision, an incision offset slightly toward the palate provides significant surgical and biological advantages — particularly for GBR cases and aesthetic zones.

Advantage 1

If wound dehiscence occurs, the opening is away from the implant and graft. Not directly over the surgical site — reducing contamination risk.

Advantage 2

Any dehiscence is flanked on both sides by keratinized mucosa (palate + buccal keratinized mucosa). The gap re-epithelialises rapidly with keratinized tissue.

Advantage 3

If a long healing abutment is placed at high torque (≥25Ncm), the palatal keratinized mucosa is pushed buccally — providing a natural free gingival graft effect. May eliminate the need for a separate FGG.

Advantage 4

When working in an early healing socket: a mid-crestal incision would fall on granulation tissue — knife drops in. The palatal offset places your incision on sound bone.

Advantage 5

The palatal flap elevation brings extra soft tissue thickness buccally — if the granulation tissue in the socket is kept attached to the flap, it increases the mucosal thickness on the buccal aspect.

Asymmetric Flap Design
Applying different designs to mesial and distal aspects of the same case

The four-yeses system may give different answers for the mesial and distal papillae of the same surgical site — and your flap design should reflect this asymmetry.

Real-World Example

Bone sounding: mesial papilla = 2.5mm (favourable). Distal papilla = 4mm (unfavourable). Correct flap: elevate mesial papilla, preserve distal papilla. The incision is asymmetric — not a mirror image on both sides. This is biologically correct, not aesthetically inconsistent.

Benefits of Asymmetric Design

• Minimises tissue trauma to compromised papilla
• Maximises access where bone sounding allows
• Scar line is offset from implant on at least one side
• Optimal tissue management at each individual anatomical site

Key Teaching Point

You are not doing symmetry for the sake of symmetry. Flap design follows biology. A flap that looks "tidy" and symmetrical but ignores bone sounding findings will produce a worse result than an asymmetric, biologically-justified design.

Core Principle

One size does not fit all — and the two sides of the same tooth's surgical site may not be the same either.

Envelope Flap — In Detail
When to use it, and why it is not always the answer

The envelope flap is the most conservative of the four designs — and is often defaulted to inappropriately. Know when it is right and when it is not.

Envelope Flap — Ideal When
  • Simple implant placement, no GBR needed
  • Thick phenotype (papillae will recover)
  • All four yeses confirmed for all involved papillae
  • Minimal bone augmentation planned
  • Adequate visibility expected through sulcular access
  • Aesthetically: no scarring risk from vertical lines
Envelope Flap — Wrong When
  • Thin phenotype — elevated papillae won't return → black triangles
  • Significant GBR needed — insufficient field
  • Adjacent implant — papilla between implants is scar tissue
  • Bone sounding unfavourable at papillae
  • Adjacent compromised crown with inflammation
Common Clinical Error

"I always do envelope flap — it's what I was taught" is not an acceptable rationale. The envelope flap that involves papillae belonging to a thin phenotype patient adjacent to a dodgy crown will consistently produce recession and black triangles. Apply the four-yeses system every time.

Papillary-Preserving Flap — In Detail
Trapezoid design for compromised papillae

The papillary-preserving flap intentionally leaves the interdental papillae untouched. It uses vertical releasing incisions and a horizontal crestal incision that leaves a minimum 3mm island of papilla at the base.

Minimum Clearance Rule — 3mm

Leave a minimum of 3mm of papilla island at the base (Baird course specification). With less than 3mm, blood supply to the retained papilla tissue is insufficient and recession still occurs. The 3mm island also provides adequate tissue width for suturing.

Ideal Indications

• Thin phenotype (protect fragile papillae)
• Adjacent implant neighbour
• Unfavourable bone sounding at papilla base
• Compromised adjacent crown
• Any case where 4 yeses cannot be obtained

Limitations — Accept These Trade-Offs

• Limited surgical field — harder for GBR
• Scarring in thick phenotype patients (dense fibrous tissue)
• Step-effect at suture line if thick tissue is not managed carefully
• Requires excellent suturing technique to achieve passive closure

Flapless Surgery
The most conservative approach — when it is and isn't appropriate

Flapless implant surgery — using a tissue punch rather than a scalpel flap — represents the gold standard of soft tissue preservation when bone conditions allow.

✓ Appropriate for Flapless When
  • Adequate bone volume (no GBR needed)
  • CBCT confirms ≥2mm bone on all surfaces
  • No aesthetic keratinized mucosa concerns
  • Simple single-tooth case
  • Good bone density for primary stability
  • Adjacent papillae not to be disturbed
✗ Flapless Contraindicated When
  • Bone grafting (GBR) required
  • Bone volume assessment unclear
  • Soft tissue grafting needed simultaneously
  • Thick soft tissue obscures bone landmarks
  • Complex implant positioning required
  • Risk factors for low primary stability
Case Application — Adjacent Implant with Failing Tooth

When placing an implant adjacent to an existing implant where the papilla is scar tissue and blood supply is absent: flapless placement, soft tissue graft via tunnel, no flap elevation = zero disruption to the precious remaining papilla.

Smile Line & Flap Design
How the patient's smile affects your incision choices

The smile line determines how visible any resulting scars or tissue changes will be — and should directly influence which flap design you choose.

High Smile Line

Full gingival display. Any scars, recession, or black triangles are clearly visible. Prioritise: avoid black triangles > avoid scars. Thin phenotype: papillary-preserving essential. Thick phenotype: carefully chosen vertical lines acceptable.

Average Smile Line

Papillae visible but not mid-labial mucosa. Papilla aesthetics still important. Some scar tolerance. Balance between papilla preservation and surgical access. Four-yeses system still strictly applied.

Low Smile Line

Minimal gingival display. Aesthetic demands lower — tissue health is priority over cosmetics. Black triangles acceptable if hygiene access is improved. Scars not visible.

Intentional Black Triangles

For a patient with a very low smile line, multiple implants, and poor oral hygiene potential — intentional black triangles can be the correct choice. They improve cleaning access and are not visible in the smile. Overengineering aesthetics for a low-smile patient at the expense of hygiene access is the wrong priority.

Case Walkthrough — Complex Anterior
Applying all three questions to a real anterior implant case

Upper central incisor missing (delayed healing, 3+ months). Young patient, high smile line, braces recently removed. Step-by-step flap design decision:

Q1
Raise papillae? — Bone sound mesial papilla: 2mm ✓. Bone sound distal papilla: 2.5mm ✓. Both neighbouring teeth healthy ✓. Thick phenotype confirmed ✓. → FOUR YESES — raise both papillae. Envelope flap appropriate.
Q2
Flap extent? — Bone defect present requiring GBR. → Extend 2 units each side. Bigger flap needed for membrane placement away from margins.
Q3
Vertical lines? — High smile line + thick phenotype. Scarring will be visible but acceptable (better than black triangles). Golf club position, 3mm from implant, oblique at MGJ. Periosteal release mandatory for GBR closure.
Outcome at 5+ Years

Both papillae preserved. GBR successful. Crown placed with concave subgingival profile. Soft tissue stable at 5-year review. Scar from vertical lines barely visible.

Case Walkthrough — Adjacent Implant
Managing the most challenging scenario: implant next to implant

Existing implant (UR3). Adjacent tooth (UR2) failing — needs extraction and new implant. Neighbour = existing implant.

Step 1
Fixed temporisation before extraction — Make 2-unit bridge on existing implant BEFORE removing failing tooth. Fit immediately post-extraction. Papilla supported throughout healing.
Step 2
Plan flap — The papilla between old implant and new implant site = scar tissue (old implant is the "neighbour"). → Always papillary-preserving flap on that side. Distal side: assess with four-yeses system.
Step 3
Surgery — Papillary-preserving mesially (implant side). GBR with bone graft + membrane. Close with tension-free sutures. Refit temporary bridge to support papilla and tissue contour during healing.
Step 4
Outcome — At 5+ years: both implants present with papilla preserved between them. No recession. Aesthetically excellent.
Common Flap Design Mistakes
Learning from what goes wrong
Mistake 1 — Ignoring Bone Sounding

Elevating flap without bone sounding the mid-labial of adjacent teeth. Result: recession on the adjacent tooth. The patient didn't consent to that. Career-defining embarrassment.

Mistake 2 — Raising Implant Neighbour Papilla

Elevating papilla adjacent to old implant without recognising it is scar tissue. Result: permanent papilla loss between the implants. Extremely difficult to correct.

Mistake 3 — Vertical Over Convexity

Placing vertical incision over the maximum convexity of the root. Result: mid-labial recession at every follow-up. Particularly damaging in high smile-line patients.

Mistake 4 — Horizontal in KM

Horizontal incision line in keratinized (attached) mucosa. Creates a permanent, visible "step" scar that takes 5–6 years to fade if it ever does fully.

Mistake 5 — No Periosteal Release

Closing a GBR flap without periosteal release → wound under tension → dehiscence → bacterial ingress → bone graft failure. The most preventable catastrophe in GBR surgery.

Mistake 6 — One-Size Approach

"I always do envelope flap" or "I always preserve papillae" — neither is universally correct. Only the biology of the individual case determines the right design.

Scarring Rules — Comprehensive Summary
The definitive guide to avoiding visible scars
The Two Absolute Don'ts

❌ No horizontal lines in attached mucosa / keratinized gingiva — these scar badly and permanently (step effect, 5+ years)

❌ No true vertical lines in mobile mucosa — mobile tissue scars differently, worse aesthetics

The Transition Rule

When a vertical incision reaches the MGJ — transition to oblique. Do not continue straight vertical into mobile mucosa. The angle change prevents the worst mobile-mucosa scarring while still providing adequate release.

Scarring Perspective

Thick phenotype scars more than thin phenotype. But scars in thick phenotype are still preferable to black triangles — and scars fade over years. Black triangles do not. Always weigh: will this scar be worse than the alternative tissue damage?

Long-Term Scar Fate

Vertical scars in attached mucosa: typically significant for 1–2 years, noticeably reduced by 3–4 years, often barely visible at 5 years. Horizontal scars in attached mucosa: slower to fade, may persist 5–7 years. Scars in thin phenotype: minimal — fibrous density low.

Flap Elevation Technique
Correct instrumentation and sequence for flap elevation

Correct flap elevation prevents perforation, minimises trauma, and maintains tissue integrity for primary closure.

1
Periosteal elevator — Begin elevation at the incision line, working subperiosteally. The Buzzer elevator for general mucosa, diamond-shaped tip for papilla region.
2
Full-thickness flap — Elevate periosteum with mucosa as one unit. Subperiosteal dissection keeps the plane clean and avoids trauma to vessels above the periosteum.
3
Maintain flap moisture — Keep flap covered with saline-soaked gauze during surgery. Dry flap = tissue death = wound healing problems.
4
Retraction — Use gentle, continuous retraction — not sharp pulling. The Minnesota retractor or Langenbeck for standard cases.
Palatal Flap Elevation

Do NOT raise the palatal/lingual flap for standard labial implant cases. Only raise the labial flap — measure the thickness of the lingual/palatal mucosa with a probe to assess tissue thickness without disrupting it.

Granulation Tissue Management

After extraction or in early healing sockets: keep granulation tissue attached to the flap (scraping off reduces flap thickness and blood supply). The granulation tissue will integrate and thicken the flap.

Implant Position — The T-Concept Revisited
Why correct 3D positioning underpins every soft tissue strategy

No amount of skilled flap design saves an implant in the wrong position. Position is the non-negotiable foundation on which soft tissue management rests.

Apicocoronal (Depth)

3–4mm below future gingival margin. Thin phenotype: 4mm. Thick phenotype: 3mm. Never compromise depth for bone concerns — use shorter implant or sinus lift instead.

Mesiodistal

≥1.5mm from adjacent tooth roots. ≥3mm between adjacent implants. Closer than this: interdental bone resorbs → papilla lost → black triangles.

Buccopalatal (T-Concept)

As palatal as possible — aimed at the cingulum. This protects the labial plate, preserves bone buccally, and creates the "jumping gap" for labial bone graft.

Wrong Position = Inevitable Failure

An implant placed at the bone crest with 2mm of thin mucosa above it will lose bone through biological width re-establishment. No flap design can prevent this. No graft can fix this. Correct position at implant placement is the only intervention that works.

Maintaining vs Obtaining
The hierarchy of soft tissue management

"Maintain rather than lose and obtain" — the principle that should guide every soft tissue decision in implant surgery.

MAINTAIN (Easiest)
  • Fixed temporisation before extraction
  • Correct flap design preserving papillae
  • Papillary-preserving flap for at-risk papillae
  • Tension-free closure to prevent dehiscence
  • Frenectomy before surgery if needed
OBTAIN (Harder, More Morbidity)
  • FGG to regain lost keratinized mucosa
  • CTG for lost tissue volume
  • Papilla reconstruction (very unpredictable)
  • GBR for lost bone support
  • Multiple staged procedures over months
The Cost of Poor Flap Design

Maintaining tissue takes 2 hours of careful planning and surgery. Attempting to recover lost tissue takes multiple procedures over 12–18 months — with no guarantee of a natural result. The investment in careful upfront decision-making is always worthwhile.
