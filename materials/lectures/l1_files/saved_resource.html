Core Concept

What Is Valid Consent?

Valid consent is not just a signature on a form. It requires that the patient understands what will happen, why it is being proposed, what the alternatives are, and what the risks are — and then freely agrees.

The Department of Health defines consent as: A patient giving permission for a healthcare professional to provide care or treatment. For consent to be valid, it must be given voluntarily by an appropriately informed person who has the capacity to make the decision.

Consent is a process, not a one-time event
Core Concept

The Three Pillars of Valid Consent

Capacity
Tap to reveal

The patient must have the mental ability to understand, retain, use, and weigh the information to make a decision. This can fluctuate — reassess each time.

Information
Tap to reveal

The patient must receive sufficient information: what the treatment involves, risks, benefits, alternatives, and consequences of doing nothing — tailored to them.

Voluntariness
Tap to reveal

The decision must be free from coercion or undue pressure. No financial incentives, time pressure, or manipulation. The patient must feel able to say no.

Gray Area

Emergency Treatment and Consent

What happens when a patient arrives in pain but refuses treatment? This is one of the most challenging gray areas in dental practice.

Clinical Scenario

A patient presents with a large abscess and severe pain. They say: "I don't want any treatment. Just give me antibiotics." They are fearful and anxious.

Key principle: If a patient has capacity and refuses treatment — even emergency treatment — you cannot proceed. Doing so would constitute battery. You must respect their autonomous decision.

In practice: Most patients who present in pain want help. The skill is in communicating calmly: "I understand your fear. To resolve the pain, I need to drain the abscess. Here's exactly what I'll do…" This often leads to informed acceptance.

Framework

The Best Interests Framework

When a patient lacks capacity, treatment decisions must be made in their best interests. This is not simply what you think is clinically best — it is a holistic assessment.

Clinical Factors

Diagnosis, prognosis, treatment options, likely outcomes, and risks of inaction.

Patient's Wishes

Any previously expressed preferences, values, and beliefs when they had capacity.

Carer Views

Input from those who know the patient well — but their views are not determinative.

Least Restrictive Option

Choose the option that interferes least with the patient's rights and freedoms.

Critical Distinction

Carers, Legal Guardians and Your Role

A common misconception: if you disagree with a legal guardian's decision, can you override it?

No — you cannot override a legal guardian or someone with Lasting Power of Attorney. If they have legal authority, the decision is theirs. Your role is to advise, urge reconsideration, and document — not to impose your clinical judgment.

What You Can Do

Step 1: Clearly state your disagreement and clinical reasoning.
Step 2: Urge them to reconsider based on the patient's best interests.
Step 3: Document everything — your recommendation, their decision, your advice against it.
Step 4: If they insist on something clinically inappropriate, you can refuse to perform it — but you cannot force treatment they decline.

Applied Scenario

"Take It Out" — The Carer Who Wants Extraction

Scenario

A patient has occlusal caries on a lower six. The carer insists: "Just take it out. People used to come out all the time." You believe the tooth is restorable and extraction is not in the patient's best interests.

What You Must NOT Do

Extract the tooth against your clinical judgment. You would not be able to defend this — and the carer's instruction does not protect you.

What You SHOULD Do

Refuse to extract. Write a letter: state your recommendation, that extraction is not in the patient's best interests, and that the consequences are the carer's responsibility.

"I don't agree with it coming out. I wouldn't be able to defend that. I think you should reconsider. If you do not consent to this, that is your responsibility, not mine."

Safeguarding

Safeguarding — Why It Matters in Dentistry

Safeguarding is not optional. It is a professional obligation. Even if you work in an area where you rarely see it, the principles must be current in your mind.

The lecturer's honest view: "I go through it in the West End saying, I don't see any of this in the West End of London. But it does happen all over the country, and some of you will have more frequent interactions where there is a social problem within the family."

Safeguarding training is mandatory CPD — not a box-ticking exercise
  • Keep your safeguarding CPD up to date — go through it regularly
  • Know your local safeguarding pathways and contacts
  • Understand that detecting abuse triggers your duty to act
  • If you detect harm by a carer — flag it through the proper channels
Intersection

Where Safeguarding Meets Consent

Some consent dilemmas are actually safeguarding concerns in disguise. A carer who refuses necessary treatment may be neglecting the patient.

SituationConsent IssuePossible Safeguarding Flag
Carer refuses pain-relieving treatmentRefusal of treatmentPossible neglect
Family member coerces patient into expensive treatmentLack of voluntarinessFinancial abuse
"I don't want to spend money on my wife's teeth"Best interests conflictNeglect / coercive control
Patient appears fearful of accompanying personCapacity may be affectedDomestic abuse

Remember: If a carer is doing something to harm the person they are supposed to be looking after, that is not your responsibility to adjudicate — but it is your responsibility to detect and refer.

Regulatory Framework

GDC Standards for the Dental Team

The GDC Standards (Standard 1–9) set out the principles every dental professional must follow. When was the last time you read them cover to cover?

Practical tip from the lecturer: "Easy CPD for the whole practice. Two hours. Block off from 3:00 on a Friday. Go through the standards once a year — do we do this? Do we do this? Make people aware. Sign it off."

  • Put patients' interests first — not your bank balance, not your Instagram
  • Communicate effectively — patients must understand you
  • Obtain valid consent — they must comprehend what they're agreeing to
  • Maintain and protect patient information — don't share without authority
  • Have a clear complaints procedure — hopefully never used but must exist
  • Work with colleagues — medical, dental, and specialists
  • Maintain professional knowledge and skills — you're learning now
  • Raise concerns if patients are at risk — safeguarding duty
  • Personal behaviour — maintain confidence in the profession
Standard Deep Dive

Put Patients' Interests First

This is described as "a really big one." It underpins everything else in the standards.

"Not your bank balance. Not your Instagram page. Imagine that was your loved one going to see somebody. Ask yourself how you want them to be treated."

Patient-First Thinking

Recommending what you would do for your own family. Being honest about what is needed versus optional. Prioritising long-term outcomes over short-term revenue.

Non-Patient-First Thinking

Recommending the most expensive option when simpler suffices. Upselling unnecessary cosmetic procedures. Prioritising your schedule over the patient's best clinical pathway.

Standard Deep Dive

Maintain and Protect Patient Information

Scenario 1 — The Parent

A mother calls: "My daughter came in to see you the other day and had this done." The daughter is 26 years old.

Your response: "I'm really sorry, but I don't have any written authority to share information. I can't even confirm or deny that your daughter has been to this practice."

Scenario 2 — The Spouse

A husband says: "You saw my wife earlier, didn't you?" They are married, attend the same practice, and know each other's appointments.

Your response: "Yes, I had the pleasure of meeting her." — You can acknowledge the attendance since it's open knowledge. But what was done? "I'm really not at liberty to say." Common sense applies — but when in doubt, say nothing.

Applied Scenario

The Divorced Couple — Confidentiality Under Pressure

Complex Scenario

A husband and wife are divorced but still attend the same practice. They hate the sight of each other. The wife arrives and says: "My husband was here last week with his new partner, wasn't he?"

Your response: "I'm really sorry. I can't comment one way or the other."

  • Ensure separated/divorced patients don't meet in the waiting room
  • Never confirm or deny anything about one patient to another — regardless of prior relationship
  • The fact they once shared a life does not create a right to information
  • This is common sense professionalism — but it's also legally enforceable

Standard Deep Dive

Work with Colleagues in the Patient's Best Interest

"Colleagues" means more than the dentist next door. It includes medical professionals, specialists, and anyone involved in the patient's care.

Dental Colleagues

Hygienists, therapists, specialists, lab technicians — everyone in the dental team.

Medical Colleagues

GPs, cardiologists, endocrinologists — for medically compromised patients.

Specialist Referrals

ENT for airway issues, neurologists for facial pain, oral medicine for mucosal lesions.

The principle: Work with whoever is going to be able to provide that patient with a remedy to their problem. Don't hold onto patients out of pride — refer when it's in their interest.

Standard Deep Dive

Maintain, Develop and Work Within Your Competence

You are here on an implant course — you are extending your skills. But extending skills comes with responsibility.

"The way you get good at something is by thinking about it before you do it, understanding all the skill set that's required, and tripping your way through."
Skill acquisition requires preparation, supervision, and honest self-assessment
  • Know your limits — work within your competence at all times
  • Prepare thoroughly — study the procedure before attempting it
  • Seek mentorship — have experienced colleagues oversee early cases
  • Reflect honestly — identify what went well and what didn't after each case
Standard Deep Dive

Raise Concerns if Patients Are at Risk

This standard directly links to safeguarding but extends further — it includes concerns about colleagues, systems, and practice environments.

What to Raise Concerns About

Patient abuse or neglect, coercive decision-making, colleague impairment, unsafe practice environments, systemic failures affecting care.

How to Raise Concerns

Follow your practice's safeguarding lead initially. If internal routes fail, use the GDC's concerns process or contact relevant external bodies.

Real Example from the Lecture

"I don't want to spend money on my deteriorating wife's teeth now. He's not long for this world. I don't care if she's in pain or not." — This is a safeguarding concern. It involves potential neglect, coercion, and a decision that is not in the patient's best interests.

Standard Deep Dive

Personal Behaviour and Professional Confidence

"Make sure your personal behaviour maintains patients' confidence in you and the dental profession. And I think our profession is slipping from that in many ways."

This standard is about how you conduct yourself both inside and outside the practice. In the age of social media, the boundary between personal and professional has blurred.

In Practice

Professional appearance, respectful communication, appropriate boundaries with patients, punctuality, and honesty.

Online and Social Media

Before-and-after photos without consent, boasting about cases, unprofessional content, or anything that undermines public trust in the profession.

Practical Skill

How to Document Consent Disputes

When consent is refused or there is disagreement, your documentation becomes your defence. Here is a template approach:

Example letter / note:

"Following our conversation on [date], I recommended [treatment X] for [patient name].

This recommendation is based on [clinical reasoning].

The decision was made not to proceed with this treatment. I do not believe this is in the patient's best interests, and I am not happy to carry out the alternative requested [treatment Y].

My recommendation remains [treatment X]. If you do not consent to this, that is your responsibility, not mine. I will not be responsible for the aftermath of not proceeding.

I strongly advise [treatment X] as the most appropriate course of action."

The rule: The problem is you want to get involved in the social side of things. Don't. State your clinical position clearly, document it, and leave the decision to the legally responsible party.

Lecture 1 Summary

Key Takeaways

1
Valid consent requires capacity, information, and voluntariness — a signature alone is not enough.
2
You cannot override a legal guardian — but you can refuse to perform treatment you believe is wrong, and you must document your position.
3
Emergency treatment in a capacitated patient who refuses is battery — communication skills are your best tool to convert refusal into informed acceptance.
4
Safeguarding is everyone's responsibility — keep your CPD current, know your pathways, and act when you detect harm.
5
Confidentiality applies regardless of relationship — even parents of adult children have no automatic right to information.
6
Read the Dental Protection Concern 2016 guide — it is the most practical consent resource available for UK dentists.
