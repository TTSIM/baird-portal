CBCT Fundamentals
Training, Scope & Physics

Cone Beam CT Level 1 Training — what CBCT is, how it differs from medical CT and MRI, the physics of image acquisition, and the legal/training framework that governs its use in dentistry.

CBCT Training Structure

Level 1 vs Level 2

Level 1 — Today

The introductory core training in dental CBCT. Theory-heavy. Required for any dental team member who is going to take, refer for, or interpret CBCT scans.

Level 2 — Two flavours

Two distinct sub-streams: (a) operator training on a specific machine, and (b) reporting/interpretation training for clinical practice.

💡
CBCT training requirements are based on the European Academy of DentoMaxilloFacial Radiology (EADMFR) curriculum, adopted in UK guidance documents that we must comply with as part of our IRMER duties.

What is CBCT?

More than just "3D imaging"

CBCT is most accurately described as a cross-sectional imaging modality — not merely a "3D imaging" technique.

3D vs Cross-sectional

When you look at a patient clinically, you already have 3D information — about the surface anatomy. What you can't do is look inside the teeth and bone. CBCT lets you slice the patient's anatomy into thin sections and examine the internal structures. That's the real advantage.

Cross-sectional family

CBCT joins medical CT, MRI and ultrasound as cross-sectional modalities. The interesting question is why CBCT is the dental modality of choice — and that comes down to a mixture of inherent advantages and very practical considerations.

The "limitation" of poor soft-tissue contrast is actually an advantage — it focuses the information on what dentists need to see and makes the scan easier to learn to interpret.

What CBCT Can — and Cannot — Show

Case: horizontally impacted lower 8 with facial swelling

CBCT will show you the resorbed bone, the buccal cortex perforation and the relationship of the tooth to the ID canal — but it cannot tell you whether the patient's swollen face is just oedema or whether there's a frank abscess underneath the masseter. For that, you need medical CT with windowing, or MRI.

💡
Simple example: on CBCT you can assess the relationship of a lower 8 to the ID nerve (which runs in a bony canal) — but you cannot assess its relationship to the lingual nerve, which runs entirely in soft tissue.

Scope of Reporting

Where does "dentoalveolar" end?

The current UK guidance specifies training for "dental CBCT" but doesn't precisely define the term. The older 2010 HPA-CRCE-101 guidance was more explicit, and the European SedentexCT guidance fills in the gaps.

  • Anything above the orbital floors or up to the skull base is outside independent dentist reporting
  • The airway and cervical spine are also outside scope
  • TMJ imaging sits outside dentoalveolar — you can't image the TMJ without imaging the middle/inner ear
  • Up to about two-thirds into the maxillary sinus is reasonable, as you can't usefully scan the maxilla without seeing some sinus

Why Scope Matters: A Cautionary Case

The incidental pituitary tumour

Case

A very experienced oral surgeon took a large-volume CBCT for some bilateral sinus mucosal thickening, retained roots and an oroantral communication. The scan extended right up to the skull base. There was complete opacification of the sphenoid sinuses and destruction of the pituitary fossa with soft tissue herniating downward.

The patient had an asymptomatic pituitary tumour. The dentoalveolar findings were correctly reported — but the tumour was nearly missed. Fortunately a second-opinion radiology report caught it and the patient was referred for neurosurgery.

⚖️
Medico-legal lesson: if you image anatomy outside your scope and miss pathology, "I wasn't trained for that" is not a defence — the guidance clearly says it falls outside your remit. The right answer is either (a) take a smaller-volume scan, or (b) get the larger scan reported by a radiologist. A typical radiology report costs ~£100 — minimal compared with the cost of a missed diagnosis.

The 360° Rotation Principle

Like Jeremy Clarkson walking around a Ferrari…

To form a 3D mental picture of an object, you walk around it and look from many angles. To use X-rays to reconstruct a 3D image, the scanner must take projections from many angles around the patient. More projections → more information → higher resolution → higher dose.

🎯
The fundamental trade-off: you cannot have a high-resolution AND very-low-dose scan. Anyone who tells you otherwise is misleading you. There are only two ways to reduce dose: (1) compromise image quality, or (2) scan less anatomy.

Pulsed, Not Continuous

The exposure-time clue on your control panel

The X-ray tube does not emit continuously as it rotates. It pulses — pulse → rotate → pulse → rotate. Each pulse is one 2D projection. The total exposure time you see on the control panel is the sum of all those pulses.

Why this matters clinically

When you switch your machine from standard to high-resolution settings, watch the exposure time. It will go up, because the scanner is taking more pulses per rotation. Switch to low-dose and the exposure time drops because there are fewer pulses — but image quality also drops because there's less data to reconstruct from.

⏱️
The exposure time on the control panel is your real-time visual indicator of how much radiation the patient is receiving. Use it as a sanity check.

Fan Beam vs Cone Beam

Why "cone beam" CT is called cone beam CT

In medical CT, the X-ray beam is a flat fan — divergent horizontally but narrow vertically. One rotation acquires one slice, then the bed moves and another slice is acquired. In CBCT, the beam is a 3D cone — divergent in both directions — so a single rotation captures the entire volume at once.

The Loaf of Bread Analogy

A way to remember the difference

🍞 Medical CT — slices first

You bake the bread as individual slices, one at a time. Each rotation gives you one slice. Then you stack them together to make the whole loaf. To image less anatomy, you simply slice from a higher or lower point — but every slice is the full width of the patient.

🥖 CBCT — loaf first

You bake the whole loaf in one go (one rotation), then artificially slice it up on the computer afterwards to view different cross-sections. This is why with CBCT you can choose exactly the size and position of the volume you want — you control the cone shape and where its centre of rotation sits.

💡
Critical consequence: with medical CT you can only control the vertical extent, but you must always image the full width of the patient — left side, right side, cervical spine, everything in between. With CBCT you can target a 4×4 cm cylinder around just one tooth.

Field of View

CBCT volumes are cylinders

CBCT volumes are described in two dimensions — e.g. "5 × 5 cm" or "8 × 8 cm". Why only two? Because the volumes are cylinders, and we describe the diameter of the circle and the height of the cylinder.

Three workhorse volumes

For most dental practice you need just three options — 5×5 (or 4×4) for a section of jaw, 8×5 for a single arch, 8×8 for both jaws. Higher-end machines offer more, but you rarely need them.

Centre of Rotation & Scout Views

Targeting the cone-shaped beam

You decide where the centre of rotation sits — the machine targets the cone-shaped beam there. The X-rays still pass through contralateral anatomy, but only the volume in the cone is repeatedly irradiated and only that volume has enough information to be reconstructed.

▸ How do you actually tell the machine where to scan?
You don't move the patient or the source manually. You position the patient in the machine, and you tell the software which area you want centred. Most machines do this in one of two ways:

1. Standardised assumption — the machine assumes a standard patient size (e.g. some Carestream/CS machines). Faster, but more chance of mis-positioning.

2. Scout view — the machine takes a very-low-dose, blurry frontal and lateral projection. You drop a crosshair onto these to mark the centre. Adds about a minute to the workflow, but means you almost never need to repeat the scan due to mis-positioning. Always use scout view if your machine offers it.
📷
Scout views are not diagnostic images — they are just blurry positioning aids. You're not expected to interpret pathology on them.

Voxels — 3D Pixels

The building blocks of a CBCT dataset

A digital photograph is made of thousands of 2D pixels. A CBCT scan is made of thousands of 3D voxels — cube-shaped, isotropic 3D versions of a pixel.

"Voxel size" is one of the few easy numerical specs manufacturers use as a marketing surrogate for image quality. The smaller the voxel, the higher the spatial resolution — but the relationship is not as simple as the spec sheet suggests.

📐
Isotropic means the voxel has the same dimension in all three planes (e.g. 100 × 100 × 100 µm). This is why you can re-slice a CBCT dataset in axial, coronal or sagittal planes without loss of resolution — every direction looks identical.

Putting this in perspective

There are 1000 micrometres in 1 mm. The best medical CT achieves a voxel size of about half a millimetre. The best CBCT on the market reaches 50 µm — that's 10× the spatial resolution of medical CT, in the worst case still 3–5× better.

Combined with lower dose, lower cost, smaller machines, and the fact that it shows you exactly the structures dentists need (teeth & bone), this is why CBCT is the imaging modality of choice in dentistry.

The Dose / Quality / FOV Triangle

  • Higher quality = more projections per rotation = longer exposure time = higher dose
  • Larger FOV = more anatomy irradiated = higher dose. The single biggest dose-saving lever is to scan the smallest possible volume to answer the clinical question.
  • You cannot have all three — high quality, large FOV, and very low dose. Pick two.
🎯
The clinical priority: get diagnostic image quality (because you need a confident diagnosis), and counterbalance the dose by minimising the FOV. Don't compromise quality — compromise size.

Why 2D Imaging Falls Short

Superimposition is the enemy

2D dental imaging actually has higher spatial resolution than 3D imaging — a periapical can resolve finer detail than any CBCT voxel. The killer disadvantage is superimposition: structures from different depths overlap into a single shadow. You lose the ability to know where things really are.

2D imaging
  • Higher spatial resolution
  • Lower dose
  • Cheap & quick
  • Superimposition kills depth
  • Multi-angle parallax helps but is not the same
3D / cross-sectional
  • True spatial relationships
  • No superimposition
  • Slice in any plane
  • Slightly lower resolution
  • Higher dose than 2D
💡
Perception trick: you've probably seen the Prince William / Barack Obama optical illusions where one perspective looks completely different from another. A single periapical can deceive you in exactly the same way.

Case: The Cracked Premolar

When 3D imaging is the only way

History

The patient (the father of a dentist) bit down on an upper left 4 and heard a crack. Their dentist daughter took a CBCT within a couple of days.

✅
No combination of periapicals from different angles could ever show this crack running between the two roots. Diagnosed early, the tooth was extracted and an immediate implant placed before secondary infection or bone loss could develop.

How to Optimise Dose in Practice

Two levers, one priority

Lever 1 — Quality

You can compromise image quality (fewer projections per rotation, lower exposure time). Usually a bad idea — you sacrifice diagnostic confidence and may have to repeat the scan, doubling the dose.

Lever 2 — FOV ⭐

Scan less anatomy. By far the most important way to optimise dose. Use the smallest field of view that answers your clinical question. Less data, less dose, less to interpret, less chance of incidental findings outside your scope.

⭐
The single most important practical message of this lecture: always image at good quality but at the smallest possible field of view. Don't sacrifice diagnostic clarity to save a fraction of dose — counterbalance dose by being surgical with the volume size.

Practical Workflow Recap

Putting it all together at the control panel

  1. 1 Justify the scan — does it answer a clinical question and change/confirm management?
  2. 2 Choose the smallest FOV that captures the area of interest
  3. 3 Position with a scout view if your machine offers it — saves repeats
  4. 4 Use diagnostic-quality settings — don't drop quality to chase low-dose marketing numbers
  5. 5 Stay within scope — anything above orbital floors or beyond cervical spine → second-opinion radiologist

Quick Check

Click each question to reveal the answer

Q1 ▸ Why can't CBCT be used to assess soft tissue pathology?
CBCT has very poor soft-tissue contrast resolution. It is essentially a hard-tissue imaging modality. To assess soft-tissue swelling, abscesses, muscle pathology or soft-tissue tumours, you need medical CT (with windowing) or MRI.
Q2 ▸ A dentist refers a patient to another practice for a CBCT scan. Does the referring dentist need Level 1 training?
Yes. UK guidance requires Level 1 CBCT training even just to refer a patient for a CBCT scan, even if someone else justifies, takes and reports the scan.
Q3 ▸ Where does "dentoalveolar" CBCT end?
Roughly: up to about ⅔ into the maxillary sinuses is fine. Beyond the orbital floors, into the skull base, the airway or the cervical spine is outside dentist scope. TMJ imaging is also outside scope because you can't image the TMJ without the middle/inner ear.
Q4 ▸ Why is the X-ray beam in CBCT called a "cone" beam?
Because it diverges in both the horizontal and vertical planes (forming a 3D cone), unlike medical CT's beam which is a flat fan (divergent only horizontally). The cone shape means CBCT can capture the entire volume in a single rotation.
Q5 ▸ What are the only two ways to reduce dose to the patient?
(1) Compromise image quality — usually a bad trade-off. (2) Scan less anatomy — by far the more important lever, and the one you should reach for first.
Q6 ▸ What is the best voxel size CBCT can achieve, and how does it compare with medical CT?
Best CBCT: 50 µm (highest-end machine on the market). Average CBCT: 100–150 µm. Best medical CT: ~500 µm (0.5 mm). CBCT delivers 3–10× the spatial resolution of medical CT for hard tissue imaging.
