Context

Why Case Law Matters for Dentists

Not one of the landmark consent cases is dental. They are all medical. And almost all involve some level of permanent disability that became the subject of legal wrangling.

This means every dental consent case that reaches a court or GDC hearing will be judged against these medical precedents. You need to understand them not as abstract legal theory — but as the framework that will be applied to your clinical decisions.

Case 1 of 7

Bolam v Friern Barnet Hospital (1957)

The Facts

Mr Bolam, a patient at a mental hospital, received electroconvulsive therapy (ECT). During the procedure, he fell off the gurney and fractured his hip. He was not physically restrained during treatment.

The key question: Was failing to restrain him negligent?

The expert evidence was divided: Some clinicians said restraining patients during ECT was standard. Others said patients injured themselves more when restrained. The camps were roughly equal.

Historical note from the lecturer: Friern Barnet Hospital had corridors of 420 metres — the longest in Europe. It is now luxury apartments.

Case 1 of 7 — Ruling

The Bolam Test: "Would Your Mates Do the Same?"

The ruling: "A man is not negligent if he is acting in accordance with such a practice as is accepted as proper by a responsible body of men skilled in that art… merely because there is a body of opinion who would take a contrary view."

In plain language: if a reasonable number of competent professionals would do what you did, you are not negligent — even if some would disagree.

Would Pass Bolam

Composite instead of amalgam. No rubber dam for RCT. Choosing extraction over RCT in a specific scenario.

Would Fail Bolam

Extracting a restorable tooth and immediately placing an implant without discussing conservative options.

Case 2 of 7

Sidaway v Board of Governors (1985)

The Facts

A patient underwent cervical cord decompression to alleviate pain. The surgeon failed to inform her of a 1% risk of paraplegia. The patient became paraplegic.

The court's reasoning: Giving unsought information might cause unreasonable fear of very minor risks and deter patients from appropriate treatment. The surgeon's discretion should apply — Bolam was used to judge what should be disclosed.

This is the paternalistic approach — and it has been absolutely thrown out in its entirety. This is no longer valid law. But it is essential to understand because it shows how recently the law has changed.

Case 2 of 7 — Dental Parallel

The Sidaway Mindset in Dentistry

"This is like saying, I'm not going to tell the patient about risk to the ID nerve because it happens so rarely. And then you prang the nerve. You don't use stop drills. You don't use a guide. Suddenly your implant's in an empty mandible and your ID nerve is wrapped around your drill tip."

Under Sidaway's logic, the surgeon could argue: "The risk was so small I didn't mention it." Under current law, that defence is completely unavailable.

Key lesson: Within the lecturer's practicing lifetime, the law shifted from "the doctor decides what to tell you" to "the patient decides what they need to know." Sidaway is the old world. Montgomery is the new one.

Case 3 of 7

Maynard v West Midlands RHA (1984)

The Facts

A patient presented with throat symptoms. Differential diagnoses: tuberculosis, Hodgkin's lymphoma, carcinoma, or sarcoidosis. Doctors recommended a biopsy rather than waiting weeks for TB results. During biopsy, the recurrent laryngeal nerve was damaged — permanent hoarse voice. The patient had TB — not cancer.

The ruling: Claim thrown out. Not doing the biopsy could have meant death from undiagnosed cancer. Nerve damage versus death — the doctors acted in the patient's best interests despite a known risk of harm.

Case 3 of 7 — Dental Parallel

Maynard and the Tongue Biopsy Dilemma

Dental Scenario

A patient has a lesion on the lateral tongue. You suspect squamous cell carcinoma. You take a biopsy. During the procedure, you hit a lingual artery branch — heavy bleeding, hospital admission. Histology shows benign leukoplakia.

Could you be criticised? The question becomes: should you have referred to a maxillofacial unit? The biopsy was within your capabilities, but complications were significant. Maynard protects you if the biopsy was clinically indicated — ruling out cancer justified the risk.

  • Warn the patient about bleeding risk before the biopsy
  • Consider referral if the lesion is in a high-risk anatomical area
  • A maxillofacial unit can assess and excise carcinoma in situ in one procedure
  • Biopsy of an active lesion carries theoretical risk of spread and metastasis

Case 4 of 7

Gillick Competency (1985)

The Facts

The Department of Health issued guidance allowing GPs to prescribe contraception to underage girls without parental consent, provided the child understood the issues. Victoria Gillick, an MP, challenged this, arguing it encouraged underage sex.

The ruling: There are no parental rights over a child who has sufficient understanding to make their own medical decisions. A child under 16 who demonstrates Gillick competency can consent to treatment without parental involvement.

The court held that parental rights diminish as the child's understanding grows. A 14-year-old who comprehends the implications can legally consent — and parents cannot stop them.

Case 4 of 7 — Dental Application

Gillick Competency and Tooth Whitening

The Clinical Dilemma

A 14-year-old girl is being teased at school for yellow teeth. She asks for whitening. Legally, whitening agents cannot be used on under-16s. But what is this based on?

The answer: The product licence — not evidence of harm. Bleaching is perfectly safe and causes no damage. The restriction has no medical basis.

The Gillick argument: A Gillick-competent 14-year-old understands whitening will stop bullying. She's requesting it for psychological welfare, not vanity. You could argue this is in her best interests. This has never been tested in court.

"Those are exactly the people who need it. So far it has never been tested. There is no case law."
Case 5 of 7

Rogers v Whitaker (1992) — Australian Case

Though an Australian case, it profoundly influenced UK law and is essential context.

The Facts

Christopher Rogers, a highly skilled ophthalmic surgeon, operated on Marie Whitaker's right eye. A 1 in 14,000 complication: antigens from inside the eye were exposed to the immune system, triggering a delayed hypersensitivity reaction that destroyed the left eye. She lost sight in both eyes.

The clinching statement: "You're not going to do any damage to my other eye, are you?" — "Had I known this was a risk, I would not have had the operation. I would rather have lost one eye than risked losing both."

Case 5 of 7 — Ruling

Rogers v Whitaker: No Risk Is Too Small

The court overruled Bolam for consent disclosure. A doctor could not rely on professional practice as a defence for failing to warn. This was a significant enough risk — even at 1 in 14,000 — that the patient should have been told.

This set the precedent: There is no risk that is too small to mention if you want valid consent that will stand up in court.

Even extremely rare risks must be disclosed when consequences are severe
Case 6 of 7

Chester v Afshar (2004) — "Had I Known"

The Facts

A patient underwent spinal surgery. She was not warned of cauda equina syndrome risk (loss of bowel/bladder function) because the surgeon considered it too small. The complication occurred. The surgery itself was not negligent.

The ruling: Even though the procedure was performed competently, the failure to warn was itself negligent. The patient's statement — "Had I known, I would never have consented" — established liability.

This is the solicitor's easiest tool: "Had the patient known about the risk of numbness? Sinus infection? Swelling on their daughter's wedding day? They would never have had it done."

Case 6 of 7 — Dental Application

"Had I Known" in Dental Practice

Think about every minor thing a patient could complain about that you might not have mentioned:

"Had I known I'd have stitches for two weeks"

Goes without saying after surgery — but did you explicitly tell them?

"Had I known I'd be numb for three hours"

A patient returning to work immediately might argue this. Did you warn about duration?

"Had I known my teeth would be cut down for crowns"

Irreversible tooth reduction. Was this fully explained and understood?

"Had I known about sinus infection risk"

Upper implant placement — sinus communication risk. Absolutely must be disclosed.

The lesson: Consent is about everything the patient could possibly complain about. This starts getting complicated — but that is the legal reality post-Chester v Afshar.

Case 7 of 7 — The Big One

Montgomery v Lanarkshire Health Board (2015)

The Facts

A diabetic woman of small stature was pregnant. In diabetic mothers, babies gather more fat around the shoulders, making them the widest part. The baby's head delivered but shoulders became stuck. Forceps were used. The child was born with brain damage and a brachial plexus injury — mentally disabled with no use of one arm.

The mother's statement: "Had I known this was a risk, I would never have done it. I would have insisted on a caesarean. I would not have destroyed my life and destroyed my baby's life."

Case 7 of 7 — Ruling

The Montgomery Judgment

"An adult person of sound mind is entitled to decide which, if any, of the available forms of treatment to undergo, and their consent must be obtained before treatment interfering with their bodily integrity is undertaken."

"The doctor is therefore under a duty to take reasonable care to ensure that the patient is aware of any material risk involved in any recommended treatment, and any reasonable alternative or variant treatment."

Bolam was disregarded for consent disclosure. The test is no longer "what would a doctor say?" but "what would this patient want to know?"

Case 7 of 7 — The Materiality Test

What Makes a Risk "Material"?

Montgomery established a two-part test — case-specific, not one-size-fits-all:

Objective Limb
Tap to reveal

Would a reasonable person in the patient's position be likely to attach significance to this risk?

Subjective Limb
Tap to reveal

Is the doctor aware (or should be aware) that this particular patient would likely attach significance to this risk?

Why this matters: "I'm going to remove that mole on your neck. Small risk of brachial plexus damage — tingling in your fingers." For most: negligible. For a concert pianist? That risk is material.

Case 7 of 7 — Practical Implications

Why Social History Is a Consent Tool

Under Montgomery, you must understand what your patient does, values, and fears — because this determines what is "material."

Deep Sea Diver / Oil Rig Worker

Restorations with voids or provisionals over cotton wool could crack under pressure changes. A compromised restoration is material for them.

Opera Singer / Musician

Any procedure affecting the oral cavity, TMJ, or facial sensation carries amplified significance. Lingual nerve injury could be career-ending.

Public Speaker / Teacher

Altered speech from new restorations, swelling affecting presentation schedule — material considerations.

Upcoming Life Event

Daughter's wedding in two days, important interview — swelling, bruising, or visible treatment effects are material for this patient at this time.

Case 7 of 7 — The Temper

McCulloch v Lavin — Tempering Montgomery

Montgomery pushed the law to an extreme. McCulloch pulled it back.

The Facts

Mr McCulloch had a cardiac arrest, was stabilised, then died. The family argued the consultant should have discussed NSAIDs as a treatment option for pericarditis.

The reality: McCulloch was overweight, drank heavily, chronically abused heart. NSAIDs were contraindicated due to bleeding risk. Not a reasonable option at all.

The ruling: Claim failed. Not all possible options must be discussed — only all reasonable ones. Reasonableness is judged by the clinician's experience, not what the patient feels should have been discussed.

Case 7 of 7 — Dental Application

Reasonable vs Possible Options

The Lecturer's Example

A patient is losing an upper 7. Do you need to discuss a removable partial denture as an alternative to an implant?

"No one makes a removable denture for a single upper seven." Just because something is possible doesn't make it reasonable. You recommend an implant — state-of-the-art dentistry. Not mentioning a cobalt chrome denture for one tooth is not a consent failure.

Must Discuss (Reasonable)

Implant, bridge, or leaving the space — reasonable alternatives the patient should know about.

Need Not Discuss (Possible but Unreasonable)

Single-tooth removable denture for an upper 7 — no reasonable practitioner would offer this as a primary option.

Lecture 2 Summary

Key Takeaways

1
Bolam survives for clinical judgment but is no longer the test for what you must disclose to patients.
2
Sidaway's paternalism is dead. You can no longer decide what the patient "doesn't need to know."
3
Chester v Afshar: "Had I known" is a powerful claim. Document every risk you discussed.
4
Montgomery is the current standard: material risks must be disclosed from the patient's perspective — know your patient's life, job, and values.
5
McCulloch tempers Montgomery: discuss all reasonable options, not every possible one. The clinician defines "reasonable."
6
No dental consent cases exist. All precedents are medical. Every dental dispute will be judged against these cases.
