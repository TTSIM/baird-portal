Core Concept

What Constitutes Coercion in Consent?

Coercion undermines the voluntariness pillar of valid consent. It doesn't have to be a threat — it can be an incentive that removes the patient's ability to make a free, considered decision.

The legal test: Did the clinician apply unreasonable pressure that a significant proportion of patients would find hard to resist? If yes, consent is invalid — regardless of whether the treatment was clinically appropriate.

The key insight from case law: An approach which requires the patient to question the doctor disregards the social and psychological realities of the relationship. Most patients will simply say "yes, doctor" and think about it later.

Case Study

The Coercion Case: Craftsman v S (2020)

The Facts

A patient needed neck decompression surgery. He was placed on a waiting list with the plan: physiotherapy first, then surgery if needed. Physiotherapy would help but not solve the problem — it was to keep him comfortable while waiting.

What Happened

When he arrived for what he thought was his physiotherapy appointment, he was told: "If you go for physiotherapy, you'll go to the back of the queue and wait another year. You might as well have the surgery now." He had the surgery. He suffered nerve damage.

The ruling: The patient was coerced. By saying he would lose his place, they removed his ability to choose physiotherapy first. He may have been content for years without surgery. He never got the chance to find out.

Case Study — Principle

Why This Matters for Dentists

"The patient should have questioned it. Sorry — here's the other thing. The patient didn't have the knowledge to question it. He'd been told by a medical professional what he needs to do. A significant proportion of the population will just say, yes, doctor, and think about it later."

The court explicitly rejected the idea that patients should be expected to push back against medical authority. The power imbalance is real, and the law recognises it.

The human approach the court expected: "Yes, you will go to the back of the queue. But let's try physiotherapy first. Let me see if I can get you the physio. And if it doesn't work, we'll see if we can bump you up the list because your turn had already come." — Patient care, not patient processing.

Controversial Topic

"Ethical Sales" — A Contradiction in Terms

"Ethical sales is like a square triangle. There's no such thing. You're not in sales. You're in patient care. Can we stop the sales thing? Can we stop the money thing?"

The lecturer is unequivocal: the language of "sales" has no place in dentistry. Consider the comparison:

Other Professions

There is no "ethical sales group" in cardiac surgery. No course on "how to maximise yield from your cancer patients." The idea is absurd.

The Right Approach

Tell the patient what is required. Get paid for the care you give. If you look after your patients well, you will not be short of work. Reputation brings patients.

Do not use the word "sales" in our profession. Stop thinking about maximising revenue. Think about providing excellent care. The money follows the care — not the other way around.

Practical Guidance

Discounts and Offers — What's Acceptable?

Acceptable Approach

"We have a discount on whitening this month." Patient comes in: "Oh, I'm on the right end. There's a discount on." — Fine. They made the decision before the incentive was applied. The discount rewards an existing intention, it doesn't create one.

Unacceptable Approach

"You need whitening. If you have it done today, it's 10% off." — Not fine. You're using urgency to prevent them thinking objectively. You're not giving them cooling-off time.

The distinction: A pre-existing offer that a patient responds to is different from a time-limited pressure applied during a consultation. One respects autonomy; the other undermines it.

Applied Scenario

"I Want My Fillings One at a Time"

The Scenario

A patient needs ten fillings and wants them done individually. You want to do adjacent teeth together to be efficient.

The consent approach: "If I do two together, you'll need one local anaesthetic instead of two. That halves the risk of any LA side effects. But if you have TMJ problems or can't hold your mouth open for long, separate appointments may be better for you." — Present the trade-offs and let them choose.

What you must NOT do: "Oh, while I was there, the tooth next to it needed a filling as well, so I did that too." — The patient didn't consent to that. It's a problem regardless of clinical benefit.

The analogy: "Saying you need cardiac bypass but I'll do one artery this month and another in two months, splitting the chest open again — it's not reasonable." But the patient still has the right to choose the less efficient option if they understand the trade-offs.

Difficult Conversations

When Can You Refuse to Treat a Patient?

You have the right to refuse to perform a treatment you disagree with — but you cannot refuse to treat a patient in a way that abandons them.

You CAN Refuse To

Perform a treatment you believe is clinically inappropriate. Extract a restorable tooth at a carer's insistence. Place an implant in a site you believe is unsuitable. Do something that you could not defend if challenged.

You CANNOT

Abandon a patient in pain without arranging alternative care. Refuse to treat someone based on personal prejudice. Dismiss a patient without reasonable notice and a pathway for ongoing care.

The key distinction: You can refuse to perform a specific treatment. You cannot refuse to continue being the patient's dentist without proper process. You can say "I won't do this procedure" — but you should offer to continue their routine care.

Practical Framework

How to Have the Refusal Conversation

The script: "I'm really sorry. I don't agree with your appraisal of the situation. It would be wrong of me to do what you're asking me to do, and I'm not prepared to do it. If you'd like to carry on having your hygiene and checkups here, that's fine. But I will not voluntarily do what you're asking me to do. I can't in good faith do it. I won't do it."

  • Be clear and direct — don't hedge or leave ambiguity
  • Frame it as your professional judgment — "I would not be able to defend this"
  • Offer to continue routine care — you're refusing the treatment, not the patient
  • Document everything — what was requested, your reasoning, what you offered instead
  • Don't get drawn into the social side — state your position and leave it
Applied Scenario

"Take It Out Now, I'll Think About the Implant Later"

The Dilemma

A patient needs a tooth out. You want to do an immediate implant — maintain bone and tissue volume, guided surgery, minimal trauma, best outcome. The patient says: "Just take it out. I might not even get an implant. I'll think about it."

From the moment you extract that tooth without planning for replacement, the site starts to compromise. Buccal plate resorbs. Bone volume decreases. What could have been a straightforward guided implant becomes a case requiring bone grafting, connective tissue grafting, and possibly still insufficient bone.

The consent conversation: "I'd prefer not to take the tooth out until you're ready for the implant. We can pulpectomise and put calcium hydroxide in to keep you comfortable. If we do the extraction and implant together, we get the best result with the least intervention."

Applied Scenario — Outcome

When the Patient Insists on Early Extraction

Ideal Pathway

Keep the tooth. Plan the implant. Extract and place simultaneously. Guided surgery through the socket. Bone in the gaps. Clean, minimal trauma. This is what the lecturer would do for his own parents.

If Patient Insists

Extract alone. Bone resorption begins. Buccal plate may be lost. Future implant requires grafting, possibly CTG. More surgery, more cost, worse aesthetic outcome. But it's their choice.

Your responsibility ends at giving the information. "I understand your advice, but I want it out. I may never have an implant. I accept the consequences." — That's their decision, their level of involvement with their dentistry, not your responsibility. You've done your job.

"If you don't agree with it, but that's what they decided, despite the fact that you've given them the knowledge and the information — that's their decision."

Practical Consent

Consent for Local Anaesthesia

This was flagged as a question from the audience: "Even when we give something as simple as an inferior alveolar nerve block, there is a risk of paraesthesia. Do we actually sit and explain before we inject?"

The short answer: You don't need to recite every possible complication before every injection. But you DO need to warn about things that are material — and under Montgomery, materiality depends on the patient. A musician needs to know about paraesthesia risk more than a non-musician.

Let's go through the risks systematically — and think about which ones matter for consent.

Local anaesthetic: routine but not risk-free

LA Risks — Clinical Anatomy

"I Can't Swallow" — The Mylohyoid Issue

The Clinical Moment

After an ID block, the patient says: "I can't swallow." Your instinct might be to dismiss this — but they may actually be right.

Anatomy check: Where does the inferior dental nerve carry motor branches? The mylohyoid nerve to mylohyoid runs with it. This is a motor nerve. If your LA reaches it, the patient cannot elevate their floor of mouth — and they genuinely feel they cannot swallow.

This is different from the patient who says they can't swallow because they simply can't feel the swallow. The mylohyoid paralysis is a real, explainable effect — and acknowledging it builds trust.

For consent: "You may feel like you can't swallow properly for a while. This is because the anaesthetic can temporarily affect a small muscle in the floor of your mouth. It will wear off." — Knowing the anatomy makes you confident, and the patient feels heard.

Paraesthesia is the big one. Under Montgomery, this is material for most patients — permanent or prolonged numbness affects speech, eating, and quality of life. For a patient who is a wind instrument player or public speaker, it is highly material.

Practical Guidance

Do You Need to Explain Every LA Risk Every Time?

The lecturer was asked this directly. The answer is nuanced — and it comes back to Montgomery and McCulloch working together.

Montgomery Says

Disclose any risk that is material to THIS patient. A trumpet player needs to know about paraesthesia. A patient about to drive needs to know about duration of numbness.

McCulloch Says

You don't need to list every possible complication. Discuss all reasonable risks for the procedure — not every theoretical possibility no matter how remote.

The practical approach: For a straightforward restoration with a simple ID block, a brief "you'll be numb for a couple of hours, there's a small risk of some bruising, and very rarely the numbness can persist longer than expected" covers what's reasonable. For surgical procedures, be more thorough. For high-risk patients (musicians, public speakers), be specific.

Practical Skill

Building an Efficient Consent Conversation

You don't need a five-minute monologue before every injection. But you do need a consistent framework that covers what's material.

Template for Routine LA Consent

"I'm going to numb you now. You'll feel a small scratch and then the area will go numb for about [X] hours. You might feel like your nose is blocked — that's normal, you can still breathe. Very rarely, the numbness can last longer than expected or a small area of numbness can persist. If you notice anything unusual after the anaesthetic wears off, please contact us."

Additional for Surgical Procedures

"Because we're doing a surgical extraction today, there's a chance of some bruising or swelling in the area. There's also a small risk of bleeding afterwards — I'll give you instructions for that. For lower teeth, there's a very small risk of temporary or permanent numbness in the lip or tongue."

Time taken: 15–20 seconds. That's all it takes to have a defensible consent conversation.

Common Pitfall

"While I Was There" — The Consent Trap

The Scenario

You're doing a filling on UL6. You notice UL7 also needs a filling. You think: "I'm already numb, I'm already here, I'll just do it." You complete both. The patient later complains: "I didn't consent to you doing UL7."

This is a consent failure. Regardless of how minor or clinically appropriate the additional treatment was, the patient did not consent to it. You performed a procedure without consent — that is battery in legal terms.

The correct approach: "I can see the tooth next to it also needs attention. Would you like me to take care of that today while we're set up, or shall we plan it for another visit?" — Ask first. Always ask first.

Professional Boundaries

Knowing When to Refer — Not Everything Is Yours to Do

Part of valid consent is offering options that are reasonable (McCulloch). Sometimes the most reasonable option is referral.

Tongue Biopsy

Within your surgical capability? Maybe. But a maxillofacial unit can assess, biopsy, and excise carcinoma in situ in one procedure. Referral may be the more reasonable option.

Complex Implant Site

You've just learned implant placement. A severely resorbed ridge with minimal bone may be beyond your current competence. Referring is not failure — it's professional judgment.

The consent implication: Under McCulloch, you don't have to offer every possible option — but the options you DO offer must be reasonable. Offering to do a complex procedure you're not experienced enough for may itself be a consent failure.

Lecture 3 Summary

Key Takeaways

1
Coercion invalidates consent. Financial incentives with time pressure, fear-based urgency, or removing options are all coercive. Give patients cooling-off time.
2
"Ethical sales" is an oxymoron. You are in patient care, not sales. Tell patients what they need. Get paid for your work. The reputation will follow.
3
You can refuse to perform specific treatments you believe are wrong — but document your reasoning and offer to continue routine care.
4
For implant timing, advise but respect the patient's decision. Explain consequences clearly, offer to temporise, but don't force the ideal pathway.
5
LA consent doesn't require listing every risk. Use Montgomery (material to this patient) and McCulloch (reasonable, not exhaustive) together. 15–20 seconds is enough for routine procedures.
6
"While I was there" is never an excuse. Always obtain consent for additional treatment before performing it — no matter how minor.
