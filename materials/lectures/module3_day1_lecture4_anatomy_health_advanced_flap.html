Oral Anatomy — The Three Gingival Zones
Foundation anatomy for every implant assessment

The gingiva surrounds the teeth and covers the alveolar bone, protecting underlying tissues from mechanical damage and bacterial invasion.

1. Free Gingiva

Forms a collar around the tooth. Once the tooth is removed, the free gingiva collapses — as healing progresses it merges with surrounding tissue. The free gingiva effectively disappears after tooth extraction.

2. Attached Gingiva

Firmly bound to the alveolar bone. Provides stability and protection. Withstands mechanical forces of chewing and brushing. Acts as a barrier to bacteria protecting periodontal structures. This is the zone of interest for implant health.

3. Non-Attached Mucosa (Alveolar Mucosa)

The movable tissue beyond the attached gingiva. Thinner, non-keratinized, loosely connected to bone. Less resilient, more prone to injury, less effective against mechanical forces or bacterial invasion. Its flexibility allows lip and cheek movement.

Peri-implant vs Natural Tooth Mucosa
Five key structural differences

The soft tissue architecture around dental implants differs fundamentally from that around natural teeth. This affects bleeding response, probing interpretation, and susceptibility to disease.

1. Blood Supply

Natural teeth: rich blood supply from PDL vessels + supraperiosteal vessels. Implants: no PDL → reduced vascularity. The connective tissue seal is weaker and less vascularised.

2. CT Fibre Direction

Implants: parallel to the implant surface. Teeth: perpendicular (Sharpey's fibres anchored in cementum). Parallel fibres = weaker connective tissue attachment.

3. Fibroblasts & Collagen

Reduced fibroblast density and collagen fibre content around implants. The connective tissue seal is less robust and less capable of resisting bacterial invasion.

4. Junctional Epithelium Permeability

Junctional epithelium around implants is more permeable than around natural teeth. Higher permeability = easier bacterial penetration toward the crestal zone.

5. Minimal Keratinized Mucosa Width

Implants require a minimal width of attached keratinized mucosa more critically than natural teeth — because the other four defences are all reduced. KM is the primary protective barrier.

Clinical Implication

All five differences point in the same direction: peri-implant soft tissue is inherently more vulnerable than natural tooth gingiva. This is why strict maintenance protocols and adequate keratinized mucosa are non-negotiable.

Probing Around Implants — 4 Reasons for Caution
Why standard periodontal probing is not directly transferable to implants
Reason 1 — No Cementum / PDL

Probing can disrupt the delicate epithelial attachment around the implant. Without cementum and PDL, the junctional epithelium has weaker anchorage. Disruption can lead to inflammation or infection.

Reason 2 — Fragile Tissue

Peri-implant tissues are more fragile than natural tooth gingiva. Aggressive probing can cause trauma to the peri-implant soft tissue, potentially triggering peri-implantitis.

Reason 3 — Depth Variability

The sulcus depth around implants varies significantly depending on the depth of implant placement. Pocket depth measurements are not accurate or meaningful data for clinical assessment.

Reason 4 — False Negatives Around Crowns & Bridges

Probing around implant crowns or bridges will most likely give false negative results — due to the lack of flexibility of the periodontal probe and the high chance it will be contacting the contoured metal abutment rather than actually reaching the bone level.

BAIRD Probing Protocol

BAIRD recommends using the probe to check for bleeding by inserting the probe 1–2mm only, then moving it in a lateral movement. Inflamed mucosa will bleed; healthy mucosa will not.

BAIRD does not recommend using the probe to check pocket depth — they consider it irrelevant to clinical decision-making around implants.

Annual Check-Up for Dental Implants
BAIRD recommended annual review framework

Annual review of dental implants should be systematic, covering all components from patient symptoms to radiographic assessment.

Check 1 — Symptoms

• Comfort / Pain — any new onset of pain or discomfort around the implant site?

Check 2 — Stability

Assess all components:
• Crown — movement, wear, marginal integrity
• Abutment — stable, no rotation or looseness
• Implant — osseointegration confirmed by rigidity

Check 3 — Occlusion

Check occlusal contacts in ICP and excursive movements. Fremitus, heavy contacts, or parafunctional wear patterns should all be addressed.

Check 4 — Gum Health (Full Assessment)

Recession: assess vertically, tissue thickness, and papilla height vs baseline

Discharge on pressure: use the back of the dental mirror handle to apply gentle pressure — pus or exudate indicates active disease

Bleeding on probing: lateral movement at 1–2mm depth only, no deeper. Inflamed = bleeds; healthy = no bleeding

Pockets: debatable. Instead, check for loss of attachment at the implant level

If BOP Present

BAIRD recommends removal of the prosthesis and using the periodontal probe to check for loss of attachment around the implant — then plan treatment accordingly. Do not probe deeper than 1–2mm through the crown/bridge.

X-rays for Dental Implant Check-Ups
BAIRD radiographic monitoring protocol

The frequency of periapical radiographs varies based on the patient's oral health, complexity of the implant procedure, and presence of complications or disease.

1
Post-operative periapical X-ray — at the prosthesis fitting visit. Purpose: confirm full crown seating, establish baseline marginal bone levels, confirm absence of any abnormalities.
2
Long-term monitoring — after the first year, if the implant remains stable and there are no signs of complications (such as BOP), periapical X-rays may be taken every 2 years.
3
Signs of disease — if the patient presents with signs of peri-implant disease (increased bleeding, swelling, or implant mobility), a periapical X-ray should be taken promptly to assess bone and surrounding tissues.
4
Patient-specific factors — history of periodontal disease, smoking, or systemic health issues may necessitate more frequent imaging to ensure ongoing implant health.
Key Principle

The post-operative periapical at crown fit is mandatory — it establishes the baseline bone level that all future radiographs are compared against. Without this baseline, progressive bone loss cannot be reliably detected.

XLA Bone Changes — Thin Biotype
The biological hypothesis for post-extraction bone behaviour

Understanding why thin and thick biotype behave so differently after extraction is essential for timing implant placement and planning grafting.

Thin Biotype — The Cascade

The thin facial bone wall is rapidly resorbed after extraction. This resorption facilitates the ingrowth of facial soft tissue due to the high proliferation rate of soft tissue cells.

These soft tissue cells then occupy the majority of the available space in the extraction socket defect and thus promote spontaneous thickening of the soft tissue — but at the cost of the labial bony architecture.

Clinical Consequence

Thin biotype: >50% volume loss, in apical and lingual direction. The ridge collapses labially and apically. What appears as tissue thickening is scar tissue filling the socket — not bone. This is why thin biotype requires grafting intervention.

XLA Bone Changes — Thick Biotype
Why thick biotype heals so differently from thin

The thick biotype response to extraction is the biological inverse of thin biotype — the bone wall acts as a protective barrier rather than a liability.

Thick Biotype — The Mechanism

The thick facial bone wall represents a barrier that allows the extraction alveolus to remain as a self-contained bony defect.

This morphology favours the ingrowth of cells from the bony socket walls and surrounding bone marrow spaces. Only crestal soft tissue ingrowth occurs, due to the minimal bone resorption on the facial aspect of the extraction socket.

Clinical Result

Only 5–20% volume loss. Ridge maintains its width and height. The bone wall barrier contains the clot, enabling bone formation from multiple walls simultaneously. Predictable healing and stable contours.

Thick biotype is forgiving and predictable. Thin biotype requires anticipation: graft early, place carefully, accept that healing will be less predictable. Never treat thin biotype the same way as thick biotype in any clinical scenario.

10 Biology Facts — Flap Design (1–5)
BAIRD course: biology facts every surgeon must consider before designing a flap
01
Primary wound closure and uneventful early wound stability over biomaterials are the critical parts of successful implant surgery and guided bone regeneration.
02
Surgical elevation of the interdental papilla to access deep intrabony defects interferes with the papillary blood supply and can impair the healing process — possibly even preventing primary closure in the early healing phase.
03
Subsequent bacterial contamination may deteriorate the healing process in later phases — another reason why primary wound closure is non-negotiable.
04
Crown and root exposure and increase in interdental spacing following flap surgery is highly unacceptable. An ideal flap design must maintain gingival marginal anatomy and maximum papilla height throughout implant therapy.
05
A surgical approach that splits the papilla contributes to shrinkage and decrease in interdental papilla height — leading to exposure of interproximal embrasures. Vertical incisions must either include or exclude the papilla — never split it.
10 Biology Facts — Flap Design (6–10)
BAIRD course: continued
06
In the maxilla, a remote crestal (para-crestal) incision is recommended: moves keratinized mucosa labially, places incision on sound bone (not titanium cover screw), enables good re-epithelialisation if dehiscence occurs (both sides = keratinized mucosa).
07
In the mandible, a mid-crestal incision is recommended: equal keratinized mucosa labially and lingually. Note: the lingual flap in the mandible has very limited keratinized mucosa compared to the palatal flap in the maxilla.
08
The papilla acts like normal mucosa when there is no contact area. If interproximal bony peaks are >3mm from the papilla, expect papillary recession and root exposure of adjacent teeth following elevation — due to the biological width concept.
09

10
Suturing must be under no tension and minimally traumatic: small needle size, sufficient distance between adjacent sutures, small thread size — allowing optimal flap margin closure.
Maxilla vs Mandible — Crestal Incision
Why the correct incision position differs between arches
Maxilla — Remote (Para-crestal) Incision
  • Incision offset palatally from the crest
  • Moves keratinized mucosa labially (more KM buccally)
  • Incision line sits on sound bone — not on titanium cover screw or healing abutment
  • If dehiscence: both sides are keratinized mucosa → fast, quality re-epithelialisation
  • Long healing abutment at high torque pushes palatal KM buccally → FGG effect
  • Recommended for GBR cases and aesthetic zone
Mandible — Mid-crestal Incision
  • Incision placed at the mid-crest
  • Provides equal KM labially and lingually
  • The lingual flap in the mandible has very limited keratinized mucosa
  • Unlike the maxilla (generous palatal KM), the mandible has minimal lingual KM
  • A palatal offset equivalent in the mandible would place incision on non-keratinized lingual mucosa — unhelpful
  • Mid-crestal therefore the practical recommendation
The Core Reason for the Difference

The palate has generous keratinized mucosa — making a palatal offset a gain in keratinized tissue buccally. The lingual mandible has minimal keratinized tissue — so a lingual offset creates a problem, not a benefit. The recommendation follows the anatomy.

Two-Sided Flap — Anterior vs Posterior Relieving Incision
Which side to place the single relieving incision — and why

A two-sided flap is an envelope flap with one relieving incision. The choice of whether to place it anteriorly or posteriorly has specific logic.

Anterior Relieving Incision — Standard
  • Recommended in most cases
  • Increases vision and access to the surgical field anteriorly
  • Makes it easier to suture at the end of the procedure
  • Suturing a vertical incision anteriorly is technically more accessible than posteriorly
  • Default choice when no aesthetic concerns apply
Posterior Relieving Incision — Aesthetic Zone Exception
  • Used as exception in the aesthetic zone
  • Placing the vertical line posteriorly keeps the scar away from the visible anterior zone
  • The scar from a posterior incision is less visible during smiling
  • Trade-off: reduced access anteriorly and more challenging suturing
  • Justified when avoiding visible anterior scarring is the priority
Clinical Decision Logic

The anterior position is the default because it gives better access and easier suturing. Move posteriorly only when cosmesis explicitly demands it — typically high smile line + thick phenotype patients where a visible anterior scar would be problematic.

Golf Club Incision — Full Anatomical Detail
Vertical component + oblique extension — the complete design

The Golf Club incision has two components that together form its characteristic shape. Understanding both components is essential for correct execution.

Component 1 — The Vertical Shaft

Placed on the flat surface between two roots, at the middle of the mesio-distal width of the base of the papilla. Away from the maximum root convexity. Minimum 3mm clearance from the implant margin.

Component 2 — The Oblique Extension (Club Head)

Starting at the base of the papilla, an oblique line extends toward the mesial tooth, parallel to the mesial curvature of the posterior tooth. This oblique extension directs the incision away from the papilla vascular supply and toward more robust tissue.

The 3mm Papilla Island

The combined effect of the vertical shaft and the oblique extension leaves a minimum 3mm island of papilla behind — providing adequate tissue for suturing and preserving the papilla's blood supply.

Why "Golf Club"?

The vertical line = the shaft of the golf club. The oblique line at the papilla base = the club head. Together they form the distinctive golf club silhouette. This shape is designed to route the incision safely through complex anatomy without splitting the papilla.

Papillary Preserving Flap with GBR — Step/Clefting Risk
A specific limitation when performing GBR within the surgical field

When GBR is performed within a papillary-preserving flap's surgical field, a specific anatomical problem arises at wound closure — the step/clefting effect.

The Step/Clefting Problem

During GBR, bone graft material and membrane are placed within the surgical field. This elevates the central portion of the flap. However, the preserved papillae — on either side of the elevated central section — remain at their original lower, flatter level.

Result: the central flap margin sits higher than the papilla margins → a step effect (also called clefting) at the transition. This creates a prominent, visible scar at the junction between the central flap and the preserved papillae.

Prevention / Mitigation

• Careful periosteal release to maximise flap advancement
• Ensure membrane placed as far from flap margins as possible
• Suturing technique must accommodate the height difference
• Consider: is GBR this extensive truly compatible with papillary-preserving design?

Clinical Decision

If the GBR extent is significant, a three-sided flap with periosteal release may provide better closure and fewer steps than forcing GBR into a papillary-preserving design. The flap must match the procedure's needs.

De-epithelialised Papilla Technique
Preserving the anatomical papilla for advanced soft tissue augmentation

In advanced soft tissue augmentation cases, the anatomical papilla's connective tissue can be used to vascularise a surgically created papilla placed on top of it.

The Principle

Instead of simply discarding the papilla during flap elevation, consider leaving the de-epithelialised anatomical part of the papilla in place. This de-epithelialised connective tissue base provides:

• A vascular bed for the surgical papilla placed on top
• Structural support for the augmented tissue
• Better long-term stability of the augmented result

Application in Advanced Cases

In cases requiring significant coronal advancement or papilla reconstruction: the de-epithelialised anatomical papilla base acts as a scaffold. The surgical papilla (connective tissue or grafted tissue) is advanced coronally on top of this anatomical base.

This technique is particularly useful when: (1) there is existing papilla tissue of adequate volume, and (2) a standard papillary-preserving flap would not provide sufficient access or tissue advancement for the planned augmentation.

Why De-epithelialise?

Epithelium to epithelium will not fuse — it creates a pocket. By removing the epithelium from the anatomical papilla base, its underlying connective tissue is exposed. Connective tissue to connective tissue integration is predictable and vascular.

Suturing — Technical Specifications
BAIRD course guidance on minimally traumatic suture technique

Suturing must be under no tension and minimally traumatic. Technical details matter as much as technique selection.

Needle Size

Use a small needle. A smaller needle creates a smaller passage through tissue, reduces trauma, and minimises the risk of tearing delicate peri-implant mucosa.

Suture Spacing

Allow sufficient distance between adjacent sutures. Over-dense suturing strangulates the tissue between sutures and creates ischaemic zones that necrosis during healing.

Thread Size

Use a small thread size (e.g. 5-0 or 6-0 in aesthetic zones). Finer threads create less inflammatory reaction and cause less tissue drag during placement.

Optimal Flap Margin Closure

All suture choices serve one goal: optimal flap margin closure. Margins must be in stable contact without blanching or ischaemia. Suture tension, not just suture technique, is the primary determinant of wound healing success.

Technique Selection Reminder

Vertical mattress sutures for papilla preservation. Interrupted sutures acceptable away from aesthetic zones. Horizontal mattress for broader tissue mobilisation. All techniques require the underlying flap to be tension-free before the first suture is placed.

5 Factors for Papillary Growth
All five must be considered when assessing papilla potential — Biology Fact 9

The papilla does not simply exist or not exist — it responds to biological conditions. These five factors collectively determine whether a papilla will form, maintain, or recede.

1
Bone sounding at the papilla region — ≤3mm from mucosa crest to bone = favourable. The papilla cannot maintain height above 3mm from its bony support due to the biological width principle.
2
Presence or absence of a contact area — Without a contact area, the papilla acts like normal mid-labial mucosa (flat). A contact area between adjacent teeth or crowns provides the anatomical reference point for papilla height.
3
Thickness of the mucosa — Thicker tissue provides better volume and vascular support for papilla height. Thin tissue cannot maintain papilla height under normal healing pressures.
4
Blood supply to the papillary mucosa — Smokers have reduced blood supply. Compromised vascularity = compromised healing = increased papilla recession risk. Smoking is a specific, named risk factor for papilla loss.
5
Oral hygiene and absence of plaque in the gingival embrasure — Plaque in the embrasure causes inflammation at the papilla base → chronic tissue volume loss. Good oral hygiene is a prerequisite for papilla stability.
Smoking & Systemic Factors
How patient-specific factors affect flap design decisions and outcomes

The five papillary growth factors include blood supply — and smoking is explicitly named as reducing this. It is not just a general risk factor but a tissue-level biological impairment.

Smoking — Specific Effects

• Vasoconstriction reduces blood supply to papillary mucosa
• Reduced healing capacity in both thin and thick phenotype
• Higher risk of wound dehiscence
• Higher risk of papilla loss post-elevation
• More aggressive bone loss with peri-implantitis

Flap Design Implication

In a smoker, the four-yeses assessment should weight the phenotype factor more cautiously. Even a thick phenotype patient who smokes has compromised blood supply — tipping borderline cases toward papilla preservation rather than elevation.

Other Systemic Factors

• Diabetes — impaired wound healing, increased infection risk
• Immunosuppression — reduced capacity for primary closure
• Anticoagulants — haemostasis management required
• Bisphosphonates — MRONJ risk with surgical flaps
• All systemic factors affect wound healing and papilla recovery

X-Ray Frequency Impact

Systemic factors (smoking, periodontal history) also necessitate more frequent radiographic monitoring per BAIRD guidelines — patient-specific factors mandate individually tailored recall intervals, not a single fixed protocol for all.

Three-Sided Flap — Apically Repositioned Flap
Advanced indications and the full range of benefits

The three-sided flap is the most powerful of the four designs — offering maximum access, easiest periosteal release, and coronal flap advancement when needed.

Also Known As

The three-sided flap is also called the apically repositioned flap — because after closure, the flap margin may be repositioned at a different (often more apical) level than its pre-surgical position.

Four Advantages

1. Best access and reflection — full visualisation of the surgical field
2. Indicated for GBR and advanced bone augmentation — including bone block grafting
3. Allows easier periosteal release — more surface area accessible for the 1mm notch
4. Coronal advancement of flap margins when needed — critically important for tension-free GBR closure

When to Choose Three-Sided Over Two-Sided

• Bone block grafting
• Large GBR defects requiring maximum closure advancement
• Cases where coronal flap advancement is planned
• Cases requiring more passive closure than a two-sided flap allows
• Any case where the extent of surgery cannot be accommodated by an envelope or two-sided design

Trade-Off

Two vertical incisions = two scar lines. Both must be placed using Golf Club and scarring rules. In thin phenotype, two vertical lines carry higher scar risk than in thick phenotype — but recession risk from insufficient access is usually greater.

Extent of the Flap — Minimally Invasive Principle
BAIRD Biology Fact 2: why a minimally invasive flap is always preferable when possible

"A minimally invasive flap will ensure better blood supply, preferred healing conditions, and less post-operative pain and discomfort."

Simple Implant

1 unit each side. Minimal papilla involvement. Even flapless if bone adequate. Minimise the flap to minimise the risk. Fewer papillae disturbed = fewer complications possible.

GBR / Staged Augmentation

Requires a wider flap for better access and easier operation. GBR membrane must be positioned away from flap margins — needs sufficient field to achieve this safely.

Coronal Advancement Cases

Cases requiring coronal flap advancement due to extensive grafting benefit from a wider flap — wider base means less tension on closure after advancement. The periosteal release is more effective with a larger flap.

Aesthetic Zone — Distal Extension of Vertical Incision

In the aesthetic zone, cases may benefit from extending the flap to keep the vertical incision more distal — away from the centre of the aesthetic zone. The vertical line's visual impact is reduced when it is placed further from the most visible area.

Keratinized Mucosa — Recording & Consent
Pre-extraction assessment and patient communication

Keratinized mucosa width varies from site to site and patient to patient — and must be recorded and discussed before any extraction near an implant site.

Pre-Extraction Assessment

Measure and record the amount of keratinized mucosa before extraction. Warn the patient of the risk of reduction in keratinized mucosa after tooth removal — this is a consent point, not just a clinical note.

When to Act Proactively

Cases with minimal or no keratinized mucosa may require:
• FGG — to increase keratinized mucosa width
• Mucoderm — if palatal harvest is not preferred
• Timing: before extraction, or after implant healing (never simultaneously with GBR)

When Surgery May Not Be Needed

Some patients with little to no keratinized mucosa who maintain careful cleaning habits, use a soft toothbrush, and exhibit good oral hygiene may not require surgical intervention. The clinical picture must be assessed individually — not all deficiencies require grafting.

Why KM Matters — The Clinical Case

KM protects against bacterial invasion, reduces tissue mobility during brushing, contributes to aesthetic appearance, and helps secure the gingival attachment. Without adequate KM, patients experience pain on brushing, plaque accumulation, and increased peri-implant disease risk.

Position of Vertical Incision — Complete Rules
All rules in one consolidated reference slide
Rule 1 — No Convex Surfaces

No vertical incisions on convex surfaces. Root convexity = guaranteed mid-labial recession at the scar site. Always place incisions on the flat surfaces between convexities.

Rule 2 — Do Not Split the Papilla

Incision must include or exclude the papilla — never split it. Splitting contributes to shrinkage, height loss, and interproximal embrasure exposure. The Golf Club design prevents this.

Rule 3 — Vertical Heals Better

Vertical lines heal better than curved ones. This is the rationale for the transition at the MGJ to oblique (not curved) — the oblique is a straighter, more predictable scar line than a curve.

Rule 4 — Away from Implant Site

Make vertical lines away from the implant site. Minimum 3mm. This ensures the GBR membrane or graft is not directly beneath the scar line — reducing dehiscence risk.

Rule 5 — No Vertical in Mobile Mucosa

Vertical lines in mobile (non-keratinized) mucosa scar badly. Transition to oblique at the MGJ. Never continue straight vertical beyond the attached gingiva.

Rule 6 — No Horizontal in Attached Gingiva

Horizontal lines in attached/keratinized mucosa scar horribly — worse than any other tissue type. This is the most severe scarring error. Absolute prohibition, no exceptions.

Envelope Flap — Full Profile
Complete BAIRD characterisation including GBR considerations

The envelope flap: a one-sided extended crevicular incision freeing the interdental papillae. It requires sufficient length to permit adequate reflection and visualisation.

Advantages

• No vertical scars — useful for patients with high smile line and thick phenotype
• Preserves blood supply on both sides of the flap
• Simpler to execute
• Can be extended 2 units each side for GBR cases

Contraindication

The envelope flap is not recommended for patients with thin phenotype — to avoid papillary recession and black triangles. Elevating papillae in thin phenotype predictably causes permanent aesthetic damage.

GBR Extension Rule

If guided bone regeneration is required: extend the envelope flap by 2 units on each side. This larger flap allows the GBR membrane to be placed well away from the flap margins (which must rest on vital bone) and gives adequate access for correct GBR technique.

When It Is the Right Choice

Thick phenotype + high smile line + all four yeses confirmed. The no-scar advantage is significant in high smile-line patients where vertical scars would be constantly visible — but only if all papillae can safely be elevated.

Papillary Preserving Flap — Complete Profile
Full BAIRD characterisation including the GBR step/clefting limitation

Designed to provide access to the surgical field without disturbing adjacent papillae — used when the papillae were found non-favourable via the four-yeses assessment.

Three Advantages

1. Minimises risk of papillary loss and black triangles
2. Less interproximal bone loss (papilla blood supply preserved)
3. Minimal disruption to flap vascularisation

Golf Club Detail

Leave minimum 3mm island of papilla. Golf Club incision: vertical shaft at MD midpoint of papilla base → oblique extension toward mesial tooth, parallel to the posterior tooth's mesial curvature.

Three Limitations

1. Small surgical field — limited GBR access
2. Visible scarring in thick phenotype (dense fibrous tissue scars more)
3. Step/clefting effect when GBR within the field: central flap elevated by graft volume, papillae at lower level → step scar at the junction

Challenging Suturing

Suturing the papillary-preserving flap after GBR is technically difficult: the elevated central portion and flat papillae create a height discrepancy. Small needle, fine thread, and accurate placement are essential to prevent clefting.

Pre-Surgical & Maintenance Integration
Linking surgical planning to long-term peri-implant health monitoring
Before Surgery — Assess & Record

• KM width at implant site and adjacent teeth
• Gingival phenotype (probe shadow test)
• Bone sounding: papilla bases + mid-labial adjacent
• Neighbour assessment: health of adjacent teeth/implants
• Smile line and aesthetic demands
• Systemic factors (smoking, diabetes, medications)

Surgery — Execute & Document

• Flap design chosen: document rationale
• Periosteal release: tweezers test passed
• Grafting performed: type, amount
• Sutures: type, number
• Post-op periapical X-ray at crown fit (mandatory baseline)

Annual Review — BAIRD Protocol

• Comfort / pain
• Stability: crown, abutment, implant
• Occlusion
• Recession: vertical, thickness, papillae
• Discharge on mirror pressure
• BOP: 1–2mm lateral probing
• Pockets: debatable → check loss of attachment instead
• X-ray: every 2 years if stable; promptly if disease signs

If BOP Found at Review

Remove prosthesis. Probe for loss of attachment at implant level. Plan treatment. Do not probe deeper than 1–2mm through the crown. Aggressive probing risks disrupting the hemidesmosomal attachment and triggering the very disease you are investigating.
