The Modern Challenge
Why implant dentistry evolved beyond osseointegration

In the 1970s, the challenge was getting titanium to integrate with bone. Today — any titanium rod will integrate.

The real challenges of modern implant dentistry are:

  • Preserving osseointegration over the long term
  • Maintaining bone levels which support soft tissue
  • Achieving stable, healthy soft tissue
  • Understanding that losing soft tissue = losing bone and vice versa
Key Principle

An implant is a foreign body. Success depends on building the right biological environment around it — adequate bone and stable soft tissue — not merely on the implant integrating.

Peri-Implant Zones
Click each zone to explore its clinical significance
E Zone (Aesthetic Zone)
The visible portion of the peri-implant mucosa. What the patient sees. Goal: correct colour, texture, and emergence profile. Supported entirely by the bone and tissue beneath it.
B Zone (Biological Seal)
Where hemidesmosomal attachment occurs to the abutment. In health, this creates a partial seal slowing bacterial spread. Requires clean, polished titanium — not glazed zirconia.
C Zone (Crestal Zone)
If bacteria reach here — the body dispatches leukocytes which damage bone non-selectively. Result: marginal bone loss and initiation of peri-implantitis.
Dead / Transitional Zone
Between B and C. Thickness influenced by soft tissue thickness, oral hygiene, and abutment material/polish. Thicker tissue = deeper dead zone = better bone protection.
Clinical Implication

The entire architecture exists to stop bacteria reaching Zone C. Every clinical decision — tissue thickness, phenotype, KM width, abutment material — affects this defence.

Hemidesmosomal Attachment
The partial biological seal around implant abutments

The hemidesmosomal attachment is the implant's equivalent of the junctional epithelium — forming a partial biological seal between mucosa and abutment surface.

What Forms It

A partial seal of half-desmosomes (hemidesmosomes) between the junctional epithelium and the abutment surface. It is weaker than the natural tooth PDL attachment but functional in health.

Surfaces That Support Hemidesmosomes

Polished titanium — the gold standard. Clean surface allows epithelial cells to form partial desmosomes. Polished (not glazed) zirconia — also acceptable if surface is correct.

Surfaces That Do Not Support Hemidesmosomes

Glazed zirconia — too smooth, epithelium cannot attach. Creates a pocket rather than a seal. Rough or sandblasted surfaces — also problematic. Result: pocket formation, bacterial entry.

At Annual Review — Important

Removing a crown from a healthy implant causes bleeding points at the abutment-tissue interface — this is disrupted hemidesmosomes, not disease. This is why routine crown removal at check-ups is not recommended unless signs of disease are present.

Abutment Selection

The abutment material and surface quality directly affect the biological seal quality. Never overlook this as a "prosthetic detail" — it determines the security of the entire B-zone defence.

The 2+3 Rule
Minimum soft tissue requirements around dental implants

For the peri-implant biological architecture to function, two minimum dimensions must be satisfied — one for thickness, one for height.

2
mm THICKNESS
Minimum soft tissue thickness around the implant circumference
3
mm HEIGHT
Minimum soft tissue height above the implant shoulder
What Happens If Violated

The body enforces biological width through bone resorption. If only 1mm of mucosa is present above the implant, the body needs 3mm total — so it resorbs 2mm of bone. This is not a failure of the implant — it is biology correcting a dimensional problem.

Clinical Application

A patient with thin tissue (1mm) + implant placed at bone level = predicted marginal bone loss of 2mm. Solution: place the implant 2mm deeper, or perform CTG to augment tissue thickness to ≥2mm before placement.

Biological Width
Supracrestal attachment — the non-negotiable dimensional requirement
Natural Teeth

Biological width = 2–3mm. Composed of: junctional epithelium + connective tissue attachment. Measured from the gingival crest to the crest of the alveolar bone.

Dental Implants

Biological width = 3–4mm. Larger because implants lack sulcular epithelium — the continuous distance from mucosa crest to bone is wider. Non-negotiable for bone stability.

"The body will always establish biological width — if not created surgically, it will be created by bone resorption."
Core biological principle
Consequence of Violation

Implant placed too shallow → inadequate tissue height → bone resorbs apically to create required 3–4mm distance. Digital guides may violate this if tissue depth is not accounted for.

Modern Terminology

Biological width is increasingly called supracrestal attachment in contemporary literature. Be familiar with both terms. They describe the same concept.

Crown Design & Soft Tissue
How subgingival profile determines tissue health and aesthetics

The subgingival contour of the implant crown directly impacts how soft tissue adapts around it — and whether it thrives or recedes.

✗ Convex Profile
  • Pushes on soft tissue as crown is seated
  • Pressure-induced ischaemia → recession
  • Especially damaging in thin phenotype
  • Common error in lab-fabricated crowns
✓ Concave Profile
  • Allows tissue to drape naturally
  • No pressure on labial mucosa
  • Use for temporary crown initially
  • Progressively increase convexity to sculpt
Prosthetic Sculpting Protocol

Start with concave profile at abutment connection. Progressively add composite to increase convexity at 4–6 week intervals. The final crown should replicate the tissue architecture created by the temporary.

The Cervical "Waist" Myth

Some clinicians believe narrowing the crown at the gingival margin automatically provides good tissue support. This is wrong. The subgingival contour — the part hidden below the gum — determines tissue response, not the visible cervical line.

Abutment vs Crown Profile

Both the abutment and the crown subgingival contour matter. A well-designed abutment with a poorly contoured crown, or vice versa, will not achieve ideal tissue response.

Thin Phenotype — 4mm Depth

Thin tissue provides less "buffer" above the biological width requirement. Placing 4mm deep gives extra tissue height to compensate for the thinner tissue that sits above the implant.

Never Compromise Depth for Bone Concerns

If there is insufficient bone to place at the correct depth — use a shorter implant, perform a sinus lift, or place bone graft. Do not sacrifice implant depth to avoid bone issues. Depth is non-negotiable for long-term tissue health.

Gingival Phenotype
The single most important patient variable in implant soft tissue management

Phenotype determines how tissue responds to surgery, trauma, and inflammation. Getting this assessment right changes every clinical decision that follows.

How to Assess Clinically

Insert a periodontal probe 1mm into the sulcus at the mid-labial of adjacent teeth. If you can see the metal shadow through the tissue: thin phenotype. If invisible: thick phenotype. No anaesthesia required.

Why It Matters So Much

Thick phenotype: forgiving, predictable healing, accepts surgery well. Thin phenotype: unpredictable, high recession risk, requires completely different approach. Same surgery on different phenotypes = very different outcomes.

"You cannot treat thin phenotype the same way as thick phenotype."
The most important clinical principle in this lecture
Phenotype — Detailed Characteristics
Gingival thickness ≥2mm = thick · <1.5mm = thin
Thin Phenotype

• Triangular or cylindrical shaped teeth
• Incisal location of contact areas
• High gingival scallop
• Thin band of keratinized gingiva
• Thin cortical plate (fenestration or dehiscence)
• More apical alveolar crest position
• Thin and pointed interdental papilla
• >50% volume loss after extraction

Thick Phenotype

• Square or rectangular shaped teeth
• More apically positioned contact area
• Fairly flat gingival scallop and CEJ
• Thick keratinized gingiva and cortical plate
• Broader mesiodistal root dimension
• High/more coronal alveolar crest position
• Thick and prominent interdental papilla
• 5–20% volume loss after extraction

Phenotype — Clinical Consequences
Select phenotype to see implications across clinical scenarios
Inflammation Response

Cyanosis, oedema, fibrosis, bleeding, bone loss with pocket formation and infra-bony defects. Responds to treatment.

Surgery Outcome

Predictable soft and hard tissue contours after surgery. Healing reliable. Suitable for more aggressive procedures.

Flap Design

Higher risk of scarring, less risk of recession. Avoid vertical lines if possible, but recession is less likely if you must use them.

Extraction (XLA)

Minimal ridge atrophy: 5–20% volume loss. Bone acts as a barrier, self-contained bony defect. Predictable healing.

Inflammation Response

Rapid bone loss with soft tissue recession. Tissue does not mount fibrotic response — bone loss happens quickly and quietly.

Surgery Outcome

Difficult to predict where tissue will heal and stabilise. Higher chance of unexpected recession even with careful technique.

Flap Design

Higher risk of recession, less risk of scarring. Avoid involving papillae if possible. Papillary-preserving flap strongly preferred.

Extraction (XLA)

Ridge absorbs in apical and lingual direction: >50% volume loss. Thin labial bone rapidly resorbed, facilitating soft tissue ingrowth.

The same clinical scenario produces fundamentally different outcomes depending on whether the patient is thick or thin phenotype. Understanding this drives every treatment planning decision.

Keratinized Mucosa
The minimum 2mm evidence-based requirement

A minimum 2mm band of keratinized mucosa around dental implants is now evidence-based consensus — not opinion.

Why KM Matters at Implants

Keratinized tissue is resistant to mechanical forces and acts as a bacterial barrier. Non-keratinized mobile mucosa moves during function — disrupting the biological seal, accumulating plaque, and causing patient discomfort on brushing.

Consensus Support

Italian Academy of Periodontology and Academy of Osseointegration both state: inadequate KM is associated with increased plaque, bleeding, recession, marginal bone loss, patient discomfort, and compromised oral hygiene maintenance.

Recording at Baseline

Always record KM width before extraction and warn the patient of the risk of reduction after tooth removal. This is a consent point, not merely a clinical note.

When to Intervene

Cases with minimal or no KM may require FGG or Mucoderm to increase keratinized width.

Not all deficiencies require surgery — patients with no KM but excellent oral hygiene using a soft brush may be managed without intervention. Assess individually.

FGG vs CTG — Quick Reference

FGG = epithelium kept → placed on periosteum → increases KM width
CTG = epithelium removed → placed under flap → increases tissue volume

Keratinized Mucosa — Evidence Base
What the research tells us — click each finding to expand
Cases reviewed at 14 years post-FGG show stable keratinized mucosa, with healthy tissue on grafted sides versus persistent inflammation and recession on non-grafted control sites. The grafted tissue behaves as native KM in all measurable parameters including plaque index, recession rates, bone levels, and brushing comfort.
FGG is technically easier to perform when at least 1.5mm of KM remains. Waiting until complete deficiency makes the procedure technically harder and outcomes less predictable. Act early — don't wait for complete loss before intervening.
Multiple longitudinal studies demonstrate a direct association between inadequate KM (<2mm) and increased marginal bone loss over 5+ years. The mechanism: mobile tissue disrupts the biological seal, allowing bacteria to reach Zone C, triggering non-selective bone resorption from leukocyte activity.
Patients with non-keratinized mobile mucosa around implants report significantly higher pain scores on toothbrushing. This leads to avoidance of the implant site during oral hygiene — creating a vicious cycle of plaque accumulation, inflammation, and further tissue and bone loss.
Some patients with minimal or no KM who maintain careful cleaning with a soft toothbrush and exhibit consistently good oral hygiene do not require surgical intervention. The decision must be individual — KM width is one factor among several. Perfect oral hygiene can partially compensate for reduced KM.
Papilla Stability
Five conditions required to maintain or regain papilla height

The interdental papilla is supported by five biological requirements. All five must be present for the papilla to maintain its height:

1. Contact Area

A contact area between adjacent teeth or restorations provides the anatomical reference for papilla height. No contact area = papilla behaves like flat mid-labial mucosa.

2. Bony Peak ≤3mm

Bone sounding at the papilla base ≤3mm from the mucosa crest. Above 3mm, tissue cannot maintain height — biological width forces recession when the papilla is disturbed.

3. Embrasure Space

The interdental embrasure space must be adequate but not excessive. Very wide embrasures cannot be filled by papilla regardless of all other conditions.

4. Oral Hygiene

Plaque in the gingival embrasure causes chronic inflammation at the papilla base → tissue volume loss over time. Perfect hygiene is a prerequisite for papilla stability.

5. Blood Supply

Adequate blood supply to papillary mucosa — compromised by smoking, inflamed adjacent restorations, and adjacent implants (no PDL = no blood supply from that side).

Pre-Extraction Assessment
Four factors to assess and document before every extraction prior to implant
Factor 1 — KM Width

Measure and record the keratinized mucosa width at the planned implant site. Warn the patient of expected reduction after extraction. Plan FGG timing if width is already marginal.

Factor 2 — Phenotype

Probe shadow test at mid-labial. Document thick or thin. This determines implant timing, grafting strategy, flap design, and whether immediate placement is appropriate.

Factor 3 — Tissue Thickness

Bone sounding or CBCT measurement of labial soft tissue thickness at the implant site. <2mm = augmentation required (CTG) to meet the 2+3 rule minimum.

Factor 4 — Supracrestal Attachment

Probe the mesial/distal papillae to establish the bone-to-mucosa distance. ≤3mm = favourable. >3mm = papilla cannot be safely elevated without causing recession.

Clinical Flow

Document all four factors at the pre-extraction consultation. Your findings directly determine: implant timing, grafting requirements, flap design, and expected aesthetic outcome. This assessment takes 5 minutes and changes everything.

Immediate vs Delayed Placement
How phenotype determines the right timing
Thick Phenotype — Immediate
  • Intact labial plate (multi-layered cortical bone)
  • Predictable bone preservation
  • 5–20% ridge volume loss expected
  • CTG still often beneficial but not always essential
  • Outcomes predictable and reliable
Thin Phenotype — Challenging to Contraindicated
  • Labial plate is predominantly bundle bone
  • Bundle bone disappears after PDL removal
  • Socket collapses rapidly — >50% volume loss
  • If immediate: CTG mandatory, risk still higher
  • Delayed placement often preferable to allow assessment
The Bundle Bone Problem

Thin phenotype labial bone is almost entirely bundle bone — bone whose existence is dependent on the periodontal ligament. Once the PDL is removed at extraction, the bundle bone has no stimulus to exist and rapidly resorbs. No technique reliably prevents this in thin phenotype.

Digital Planning & CBCT
Using technology to satisfy the 2+3 rule and phenotype requirements
CBCT Measurement of Labial Plate

CBCT allows measurement of the labial bone plate thickness. <1mm = high risk zone, especially in thin phenotype. Measure at the planned implant level. This data drives the decision for bone grafting before or at implant placement.

Surgical Guide Limitations

Surgical guides planned on CBCT must account for soft tissue thickness. A guide that places the implant "at bone level" on the scan may be placing it too shallow in real life if soft tissue thickness is not measured and incorporated into the plan.

The Digital Guide Trap

Many digital surgical guides are designed to optimal bone-centric positions. They do not automatically satisfy the 2+3 rule unless the planning explicitly incorporates soft tissue measurements. Always verify: does the planned depth satisfy biological width given the patient's tissue thickness?

Planning Checklist

1. Measure soft tissue thickness at implant site on CBCT or by bone sounding. 2. Add 3–4mm biological width requirement. 3. This gives you the minimum depth from the planned gingival margin to the implant shoulder. 4. Verify the guide achieves this depth.

Soft Tissue Architecture
What healthy peri-implant tissue looks like
Attached Gingiva

Keratinized, firmly bound to bone. Resistant to mechanical forces. The priority tissue around implants. Goal: ≥2mm width around all implants.

Free Marginal Gingiva

The 1–2mm collar around the abutment. Forms the sulcus. Contains the hemidesmosomal B-zone attachment. After extraction, free gingiva collapses — it is gone.

Alveolar Mucosa

Non-keratinized, mobile, loosely attached. Does not provide protective properties. Moving during function disrupts the biological seal. Must be kept away from the implant margin.

The Free Gingiva After Extraction

Once a tooth is removed, the free gingiva collapses and merges with surrounding tissue — effectively disappearing. This is why planning must account for soft tissue changes at extraction, not just bone changes.

Connective Tissue Fibres — Implants vs Teeth

Around natural teeth: CT fibres run perpendicular to the root (Sharpey's fibres — anchored in cementum). Around implants: CT fibres run parallel to the implant surface — weaker attachment, less resistance to bacterial invasion.

Clinical Scenarios
Applying soft tissue fundamentals to real cases
Scenario A
A 28-year-old presents with triangular tooth shapes, high scalloping, and 0.5mm labial tissue at UL1 which needs extracting. They request an immediate implant. What are your primary concerns?
Thin phenotype indicators: triangular teeth, high scalloping, 0.5mm labial tissue. Immediate implant is high-risk or contraindicated. Bundle bone will disappear after extraction → >50% volume loss. Plan: consider delayed placement (8 weeks), socket graft at extraction, CTG at implant placement, inform patient of high recession risk regardless of technique.
Scenario B
A 52-year-old with square tooth shapes, 3mm KM at the implant site, and 3.2mm labial tissue has an extraction planned. They have 1mm KM remaining at the adjacent site. What do you prioritise?
Thick phenotype indicators: square teeth, adequate KM, good tissue thickness — this is a good immediate placement candidate. However: 1mm KM at adjacent site is below the 2mm threshold. Consider performing FGG at adjacent site 4–6 weeks before implant surgery to bring it to ≥2mm. Immediate placement with socket graft and CTG is appropriate here.
Maintenance & Monitoring
Keeping peri-implant tissue healthy long term
Annual Review Protocol

Comfort/pain • Stability (crown, abutment, implant) • Occlusion • Gum health: recession, discharge on pressure, BOP (1–2mm lateral probing only) • Loss of attachment (pockets debatable)

BAIRD Probing Protocol

Insert probe 1–2mm only, lateral movement. Inflamed mucosa bleeds; healthy does not. Do NOT probe for pocket depth around implants — considered irrelevant and potentially harmful to the hemidesmosomal seal.

If BOP Detected at Review

Remove the prosthesis. Use a periodontal probe to check for loss of attachment at the implant level. Plan treatment accordingly. Do not probe through the crown — this gives false negatives and risks disrupting the biological seal.

Radiographic Protocol

Post-op periapical at crown fitting (mandatory baseline). Every 2 years if stable. Immediately if signs of disease. Patient-specific factors (smoking, periodontal history) may require more frequent imaging.

Implant Position — The T-Concept
Correct 3D positioning underpins every soft tissue strategy
Apicocoronal (Depth)

3–4mm below future gingival margin. Thin phenotype: 4mm. Thick phenotype: 3mm. Never compromise depth for bone concerns — use shorter implant or augment instead.

Mesiodistal

≥1.5mm from adjacent tooth roots. ≥3mm between adjacent implants. Closer than this: interdental bone resorbs → papilla lost → black triangles.

Buccopalatal (T-Concept)

As palatal as possible — aimed at the cingulum. Protects the labial plate, preserves buccal bone, and creates the "jumping gap" for labial bone grafting.

Wrong Position = Inevitable Failure

An implant placed at the bone crest with 2mm of thin mucosa above it will lose bone through biological width re-establishment. No flap design, no graft, no technique prevents this. Correct position at placement is the only solution.

Socket Grafting at Extraction
Preserving ridge volume for future implant placement
When to Socket Graft

Thin phenotype + high aesthetic zone + anticipated significant bone loss + insufficient bone for early placement stability. Socket graft at extraction preserves ridge volume for later implant placement.

When Not to Socket Graft

Thick phenotype + non-aesthetic zone + adequate residual bone + patient prefers fewer procedures. The extra procedure may not be justified in favourable cases.

1
Atraumatic extraction — Preserve labial plate. Periotomes, minimal bone elevation.
2
Degranulation — Remove all granulation tissue. Degranulation burs at 800rpm are efficient and bone-sparing.
3
Graft packing — Bone graft material packed into socket. Collagen membrane over graft.
CBCT in Soft Tissue Planning
Using cross-sectional imaging for tissue assessment
Labial Bone Plate Assessment

CBCT shows labial plate thickness. <1mm = high risk, especially thin phenotype. This predicts whether immediate placement is appropriate and whether bone graft is required. Measure at the planned implant level, not just the crest.

Soft Tissue Thickness Measurement

CBCT can measure soft tissue thickness on the cross-sectional view. The mucosal layer appears as a grey band above the bone. Measurements <2mm confirm augmentation need before implant placement.

Implant Depth Calculation

From planned gingival margin: add 3–4mm (biological width) + adjust for phenotype. This gives the minimum depth of the implant shoulder below the gingival margin. Verify the surgical guide achieves this. Never trust a guide that only plans for bone position.

Interdental Bone Peaks

CBCT and periapical radiographs show the interdental bone peak levels. Mesial/distal peaks >3mm from the planned papilla crest predicts papilla loss after flap elevation. This directly influences flap design choices (Lecture 3).

Key Terminology Reference
Essential terms for this lecture and the course
Biological Width / Supracrestal Attachment

The minimum tissue height required between gingival crest and alveolar bone: 2–3mm in teeth, 3–4mm in implants. The body enforces this through bone resorption if violated.

Hemidesmosomal Attachment (B Zone)

Half-desmosome connection between junctional epithelium and implant abutment surface. Forms a partial biological seal. Requires clean, polished titanium or appropriate zirconia surface.

Keratinized Mucosa

Attached, keratinized gingival tissue resistant to mechanical forces. Minimum 2mm required around implants for peri-implant health and stable oral hygiene access.

Phenotype (Biotype)

The thickness and architecture of the gingival tissues. ≥2mm = thick phenotype; <1.5mm = thin phenotype. Determines risk of recession, scarring, and predictability of surgical outcomes.

Peri-implant Mucosa

The soft tissue collar surrounding a dental implant. Differs from natural tooth gingiva in: blood supply, CT fibre direction (parallel), fibroblast density, junctional epithelium permeability, and KM requirements.

Bone Sounding

Measuring the distance from the soft tissue crest to underlying bone by passing a probe through the tissue (requires anaesthesia). Essential for papilla and flap design decisions.

  • The 2+3 rule: 2mm thickness + 3mm height — violations trigger biological width-driven bone resorption
