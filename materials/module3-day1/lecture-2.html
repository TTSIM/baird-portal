Five Steps to Ideal Soft Tissue
Click each step to explore the rationale
Step 1 — Flap Design (Most Critical)

The most important decision. Wrong flap = wrong result regardless of everything else. Covered in detail in Lecture 3. Every other step depends on the flap choice.

Why Flap Design Comes First

The wrong flap can cause irreversible recession on neighbouring teeth before the implant is even placed. This is the outcome patients sue over. Plan the flap before every incision.

Step 2 — Tension-Free Closure

Requires periosteal release. Tension → suture failure → dehiscence → bacterial contamination → bone loss. Even without GBR, tension exists after implant placement due to ridge expansion. Periosteal release is nearly always needed. This lecture.

Step 3 — Soft Tissue Grafting

CTG for volume/thickness; FGG for keratinized width. Different tools for different deficiencies. A dedicated grafting day with a specialist covers hands-on technique. This lecture provides the overview.

Step 4 — Closure Technique

Vertical mattress sutures maintain papilla height. Interrupted sutures alone flatten papillae. Small needle, fine thread, appropriate spacing. BAIRD technical specifications covered in Slide 13.

Step 5 — Prosthetic Soft Tissue Sculpting

Temporary crown contouring to guide tissue emergence profile. Start concave, progress to convex over weeks. The final crown captures what the temporary created. Covered in Slide 16.

Why Tension-Free Closure Matters
The cascade from wound tension to bone loss

Even without bone grafting, closing a flap after implant placement creates tension. Tension is the primary cause of wound dehiscence.

Why Tension Exists Even Without GBR

The osteotomy is drilled slightly smaller than the implant for primary stability. When seated, the implant expands the ridge. Normal wound contraction then pulls margins apart. Result: tension is almost always present after implant placement.

The Cascade

Tension → suture weakens → dehiscence → bacteria reach graft/implant → marginal bone loss → exposed threads → peri-implantitis

Research Threshold

Flap tension >25 Newton centimetres leads to dehiscence. Clinically assessed by the tweezers test and visual blanching assessment — not by measurement.

Prevention — The Only Solution

There is no treatment for a dehisced GBR wound that restores the original outcome. The bone graft is contaminated, the membrane is exposed, and progressive bone loss follows.

Prevention is surgical discipline: periosteal release + tweezers test = non-negotiable.

The Tweezers Test
Clinical test for passive flap closure — perform before every suture

Before placing any suture, assess whether the flap can close passively. The tweezers test takes 10 seconds and prevents catastrophic outcomes.

✓ Passive — Safe to Suture
  • Grasp flap with tissue forceps
  • Bring to desired closure position
  • Release forceps
  • Flap stays 3–5 seconds, then slowly retracts
  • → Proceed to suturing
✗ Tension — Do Not Suture Yet
  • Grasp flap, bring to position
  • Release forceps
  • Flap springs back immediately
  • → Perform periosteal release first
  • → Re-test before suturing
Post-Suture Blanching = Emergency

After suturing, inspect the wound. White blanching at wound margins = ischaemia from tension. Remove sutures immediately, perform periosteal release, and re-suture. Ignoring blanching guarantees dehiscence at 7–10 days.

Periosteal Release — Anatomy
Understand the layers before you cut
Mucosa (Outer Layer)

Elastic and stretchable. Can expand significantly. Contains no major vessels at this level. The layer that stretches. The left hand provides the tension that makes this layer move.

Periosteum (Inner Layer — The "Glue")

Inelastic. Firmly attaches mucosa to bone. From the crest to the mucogingival junction (MGJ). Cutting it releases the "glue" and allows the elastic mucosa above to stretch freely.

Below the MGJ — The Danger Zone

Below the MGJ lies the muscle layer containing the infraorbital/mental nerve branches and vessels. Cutting apical to the MGJ risks: nerve damage (numbness), vessel damage (haematoma, black eye). The MGJ is your absolute inferior limit.

Periosteal Release — Technique
The 6 steps — click to expand each

The key insight: the right hand makes 1mm — the left hand gives the stretch. Less than 50% of implantologists perform this correctly.

Step 1 — Patient Position ▾
Patient supine, head tilted back. Surgeon behind the patient (12 o'clock). Never work from in front — blade angle control is lost. Nurse retracts the lip for access.
Step 2 — Left Hand Creates Tension ▾
Use tissue forceps to evert the flap edge posteriorly. Create tension in the tissue. Cutting taut tissue is far more controlled than loose tissue. The left hand does most of the work — not the blade.
Step 3 — Use a Fresh Blade (15c) ▾
The blade used for the initial incision has contacted bone and is now blunt. A blunt blade requires excess pressure → perforation risk doubles. Use a fresh blade 15c for its fine tip.
Step 4 — Blade Angle: 5 Degrees Only ▾
Lay blade nearly parallel to the flap surface, tip pointing toward bone. Rotate upright 5 degrees only — not 45°. This allows only the blade tip to engage the periosteum without the blade body perforating the mucosa above.
Step 5 — 1mm Deep, Full Width ▾
The blade tip enters the periosteum 1mm only. Run this 1mm notch edge-to-edge across the full width of the flap. The left-hand stretch produces 5–10mm of effective release from this single shallow notch.
Step 6 — Verify: Dry, No Perforation, 5–10mm Stretch ▾
Signs of correct technique: dry procedure (no bleeding — away from vessels), no perforation (angle respected), visible 5–10mm stretch on left-hand pull. Re-run tweezers test — should now pass.
Periosteal Release — Errors & Signs
Common mistakes and how to diagnose correct vs incorrect technique
Error 1 — Too Deep

>1mm into periosteum → perforates mucosa. Creates hole in flap → dehiscence. The 1mm rule is absolute. More depth does not mean more release.

Error 2 — Too Apical

Cutting below MGJ = muscle layer. Result: infraorbital/mental nerve → numbness; vessels → haematoma → black eye. Stay coronal to MGJ always.

Error 3 — Incomplete

Release line not run full width. Attached tissue remains at edges. Result: 1–2mm gain only instead of 5–10mm. Always run edge-to-edge.

Signs of Correct Technique

✓ Dry — no bleeding
✓ No perforation — blade angle respected
✓ 5–10mm stretch visible on pull
✓ Tweezers test passes — flap holds 3–5s

If Bleeding Occurs

You are in the muscle layer — too apical. Reposition the blade coronally immediately. Bleeding = vessels = below MGJ. Stop. Reassess the MGJ position. Move coronally and retry.

Frenectomy
When prominent frena compromise implant healing

A prominent frenum crossing or close to the MGJ creates continuous dynamic pull on healing flap margins — the primary cause of dehiscence in this region.

Problems from Prominent Frenum

• Continuous pull on sutures during healing → dehiscence
• Patient cannot clean the area → plaque accumulation
• Impairs tension-free closure even after periosteal release
• Compromises long-term tissue stability

Timing

Perform frenectomy 4–6 weeks before implant surgery for full healing. If done simultaneously: risk of perforation through weakened tissue. Frenectomy does not affect the smile line.

Technique: 5 Steps

1. Mark frenum extent
2. Clamp horizontally across frenum base
3. Incise above + below clamps — vertical cuts
4. Undermine mucosa from muscle on both sides (suture mucosa-to-mucosa, never muscle)
5. Suture horizontally — incisions were vertical but sutures are horizontal

Laser Frenectomy

Identical procedure. No sutures required — laser coagulates simultaneously. Less patient morbidity. Preferred where available.

FGG vs CTG — The Core Distinction
Select to compare — different tools for different problems
FGG — What It Does

Increases keratinized mucosa WIDTH. Epithelium is kept. Placed on the periosteum (not buried). Becomes permanent keratinized tissue at the recipient site.

FGG — Key Facts

• Epithelium KEPT
• Placed ON periosteum
• Periosteum must be preserved (blood supply)
• Expect ~50% shrinkage — plan graft size accordingly
• Remove all yellow fatty tissue from deep surface
• NOT simultaneous with GBR

Donor Site Management

Palate preferred (richest source). Leave periosteum on bone. Collagen sponge + flowable composite splint for 72hr. Heals by secondary intention over 2–4 weeks.

14-Year Evidence

Long-term studies show stable KM with better health parameters (plaque, recession, bone levels, patient comfort) on grafted vs non-grafted sides at 14 years.

CTG — What It Does

Increases soft tissue THICKNESS / VOLUME. Epithelium removed. Placed under the flap (buried). Does not directly increase KM width.

CTG — Key Facts

• Epithelium REMOVED (de-epithelialised)
• Placed UNDER flap (buried)
• Identifies depth: epithelium = shiny; CT = matte
• Tunnel technique available — no full flap required
• Compatible with simultaneous implant surgery
• Mucoderm = porcine alternative if palate not preferred

Why Remove the Epithelium?

Buried epithelium creates a pocket (epithelium does not fuse with tissue — it forms a lining). Connective tissue fuses with connective tissue. De-epithelialise fully.

Identifying Correct Depth

Epithelium = shiny surface. Connective tissue = matte and dull. Once the shiny surface disappears during de-epithelialisation (~0.3–0.4mm depth), stop.

Select FGG or CTG above to compare

FGG and CTG address different deficiencies — width deficiency vs volume deficiency. Selecting the wrong graft type will not solve the clinical problem. Always diagnose before selecting.

Mucoderm — Allograft Alternative
Porcine acellular dermal matrix — second-choice option

Mucoderm is an acellular collagen matrix from porcine (pig) dermis. Always second choice to autogenous CTG — but an important tool when palatal harvest is not appropriate.

Mucoderm — Advantages
  • No second surgical site
  • Unlimited supply
  • Reduced patient morbidity
  • Can simultaneously act as GBR membrane
  • Consistent thickness
Mucoderm — Limitations
  • Less effective than autogenous CTG
  • Always second choice
  • Higher cost
  • Porcine origin — religious/cultural considerations
  • No living cells — structural scaffold only
Dual Role in GBR Cases

Mucoderm can simultaneously function as soft tissue augmentation AND a resorbable GBR barrier membrane. Attractive for combined bone + soft tissue defect cases where minimising surgical sites benefits the patient.

Wound Dehiscence
Prevention, recognition, and consequences

Wound dehiscence is the most preventable serious complication in implant surgery. Its consequences are disproportionately severe relative to the effort required to prevent it.

Primary Causes

• Closure under tension (primary cause)
• Prominent frenum not addressed
• Patient smoking / poor healing
• Infection
• Early membrane exposure

Recognition at 7–14 Days

• Wound open at suture removal
• White scar tissue centrally (necrosis + re-epithelialisation)
• Bone loss is already occurring beneath
• Even re-epithelialised: bacteria already introduced

Consequences

• Marginal bone loss guaranteed if open >48hrs
• Exposed threads → aesthetic failure
• Graft failure if membrane exposed
• Implant failure if infection spreads

Prevention Protocol

Frenectomy 4–6 weeks prior if indicated → correct flap design → periosteal release → tweezers test → passive closure confirmed → suture with correct technique → inspect for blanching. Prevention is surgical discipline, not luck.

Suturing — Techniques & Specifications
BAIRD requirements for minimally traumatic closure
✗ Interrupted Sutures Alone
  • Flatten and compress interdental papillae
  • Pull papilla tips horizontally
  • Acceptable only where aesthetics not critical
  • Not suitable for anterior implant cases
✓ Vertical Mattress Sutures
  • Evert wound margins
  • Hold papilla at natural height
  • Better tension distribution
  • Preferred for all aesthetic zones
  • Hands-on practice tomorrow (pig heads)
Small Needle

Smaller needle = smaller passage through tissue, less trauma, lower tearing risk in delicate peri-implant mucosa.

Adequate Spacing

Sufficient distance between adjacent sutures. Over-dense suturing strangulates tissue between sutures → ischaemic necrosis.

Fine Thread (5-0 / 6-0)

Thinner thread = less inflammatory reaction = less tissue drag. 5-0 or 6-0 for aesthetic zones. 4-0 acceptable posteriorly.

Socket Grafting at Extraction
Preserving ridge volume for future implant placement
1
Atraumatic Extraction — Periotomes, minimal bone elevation. Preserve labial plate at all costs.
2
Degranulation at 800rpm — Remove all granulation tissue and PDL remnants. Degranulation burs: fast enough for granulation tissue, too slow to damage bone. Available in graduated sizes.
3
Graft Packing — Bone graft material (xenograft, autogenous, alloplast or combination) packed into socket.
4
Membrane Coverage — Resorbable collagen membrane over graft. Sutured or tucked under flap margins.
When to Socket Graft

Thin phenotype + high aesthetic zone + anticipated significant bone loss + insufficient residual bone for early placement stability.

When Not to Socket Graft

Thick phenotype + non-aesthetic zone + adequate residual bone + patient prefers minimum procedures.

Prosthetic Soft Tissue Sculpting
Using the temporary crown to shape tissue architecture

After osseointegration, the temporary crown is your most powerful tool for creating — and refining — the peri-implant soft tissue profile.

1
Initial Temporary (8–12 wks) — Concave subgingival profile. Tissue settles without pressure into a natural position.
2
Progressive Contouring (4–6 wk intervals) — Add composite to subgingival aspect. Gradually increase convexity to "push" tissue toward desired emergence profile.
3
Papilla Management — Adjust contact point height progressively. Narrow embrasure space as tissue fills in (12–24 weeks).
4
Assess Before Final Impression — Stable ≥4 weeks, no recession, adequate KM, healthy probing, good colour/texture match.
"The final crown captures what the temporary created."
Fixed Temporisation vs Removable Denture
Why fixed always wins for tissue maintenance during healing
✗ Removable Denture
  • Rests directly on soft tissue
  • Compresses and starves papilla of blood supply
  • Papilla loses anatomical reference without lateral support
  • Tissue architecture deteriorates during healing
  • Patient struggles to clean beneath it
✓ Fixed Temporary Bridge
  • Maintains papilla support throughout healing
  • Patient can clean around it
  • Stable reference for progressive tissue sculpting
  • Preserves tissue architecture during osseointegration
  • Visible through healing — early warning of problems
Staged Approach — Adjacent Implant Cases

Patient needs implant adjacent to existing implant crown: (1) Fabricate 2-unit temporary bridge on existing implant BEFORE removing the failing tooth. (2) Extract and immediately fit bridge. (3) Papilla supported throughout healing. (4) Graft at surgical date with protected soft tissue architecture.

Tunnel Technique for CTG
Minimally invasive CTG delivery — no full flap required

The tunnel technique allows CTG placement without disturbing existing papillae — ideal when an implant is already placed and tissue architecture must be preserved.

Steps

1. Harvest CTG from palate — de-epithelialise to matte CT surface (~0.3–0.4mm)
2. Create tunnel — subperiosteal tunnel instrument at recipient site, no flap raised
3. Insert CTG — tucked into tunnel, stabilised by the tunnel itself
4. Close — sling or interrupted sutures close the tunnel entrance

Ideal Indication

Existing implant with recession or inadequate tissue volume. Cannot raise a full flap without disturbing the precious papilla (which has limited blood supply on the implant side). Tunnel preserves all architecture while adding volume beneath.

GBR — Soft Tissue Closure Demands
Why GBR demands extra periosteal release effort

GBR significantly increases the volume under the flap — the "big belly" effect. Periosteal release is mandatory, not optional.

The Big Belly Problem

After GBR, graft + membrane increase the volume the flap must cover. Normal release may be insufficient. May require: (1) wider flap, (2) more thorough periosteal release, (3) blunt mechanical stretching (Minimi instrument) in addition to the 1mm notch.

Flap Margin Must Sit on Vital Bone

Flap margins must rest on vital bone — not on membrane or graft material. If the margin sits over graft material, any minor dehiscence exposes the membrane directly to bacteria → graft failure.

Dealing with Existing Implant Recession
Diagnosis first, then the correct intervention
1
Diagnose the Deficiency — KM width deficiency → FGG. Tissue volume deficiency → CTG. Incorrect crown contour → replace crown. Active peri-implantitis → treat disease first, then augment.
2
Treat Active Disease First — Never augment an infected site. All active inflammation must be controlled before any soft tissue procedure.
3
Pre-warn About Post-Treatment Recession — Treating peri-implantitis by cleaning the implant surface causes some recession. This is a treatment success, not failure. Pre-warn patients to prevent misunderstanding at review.
Recession After Cleaning = Normal

Cleaning disrupts hemidesmosomal attachments → tissue re-establishes at a slightly more apical level. As long as the tissue is now healthy, this is an acceptable and expected outcome. Communicate proactively.

Expected from FGG. Plan graft 2× desired final size.

Consensus Evidence

Italian Academy of Periodontology + Academy of Osseointegration: inadequate KM associated with increased plaque, bleeding, recession, marginal bone loss, patient discomfort, and difficulty maintaining oral hygiene. This is evidence, not preference.

1.5mm Rule — Act Early

FGG is technically easier to perform when ≥1.5mm KM remains. Waiting until complete deficiency makes the procedure technically harder and outcomes less predictable. Do not wait.

Abutment Design & Soft Tissue Interface
How abutment selection affects long-term tissue health
Polished Titanium — Gold Standard

Supports hemidesmosomal attachment. Clean surface allows junctional epithelium to form partial desmosomes (B zone seal). Most evidence available. Preferred for standard implant cases.

Zirconia — Conditions Apply

Polished (not glazed) zirconia supports hemidesmosomes. Glazed zirconia: too smooth → epithelium cannot anchor → pocket forms rather than seal. Always specify surface finish when ordering zirconia abutments.

Crown Contour — Subgingival Profile

Convex subgingival profile pushes on mucosa as crown is seated → pressure-induced ischaemia → recession. Use concave or flat profile initially. Increase convexity progressively with the temporary crown over weeks. The lab fabricates the final crown from an impression of the tissue shaped by the temporary.

Keratinized Mucosa — Recording & Consent
Pre-extraction assessment is a consent point, not just a clinical note
Mandatory Pre-Extraction Recording

Measure and document KM width at the planned implant site before extraction. Warn the patient of the risk of reduction in KM after tooth removal. This is a consent requirement — not just a clinical observation.

When to Schedule FGG

Immediately if KM is already ≤1.5mm. Plan as part of the treatment sequence. Ideal timing: before extraction on native healed bone, or after implant healing. Never simultaneously with GBR.

When Surgery Is Not Required

Patients with minimal KM but excellent oral hygiene using a soft toothbrush may not require intervention. Individual assessment is essential — KM width is one factor, not the only factor.

Sinus Lift Overview
Context: internal sinus lift with Versah burs

When bone height below the sinus is 5–6mm but a longer implant is desired, an internal sinus lift allows placement of a 7mm implant where only 5mm native bone exists.

Internal Sinus Lift Technique

Versah burs (Densah) rotate in an osseodensification pattern — compacting rather than cutting bone. The sinus floor is "nudged" 2mm upward. Osteotome-free, mallet-free. Eliminates the tinnitus risk (ossicle disturbance) of traditional technique.

Masterclass Available

One-day masterclass (Dr Waleed, Qatar) covers internal sinus lift + ridge expansion with hands-on training. Enables handling of cases previously requiring referral. Ask about scheduling.

Ridge Expansion

For ridges ≤4mm width, Versah burs expand the ridge laterally — allowing standard-diameter implant placement without separate bone grafting in select cases.

Managing Denture-Wearing Patients
Special considerations for edentulous and near-edentulous patients
Atrophic Mucosa

Years of denture pressure cause tissue atrophy. Very thin, fragile mucosa. Biological width demands harder to satisfy. High healing risk.

Keratinized Mucosa

Often dramatically reduced in edentulous ridges. Pre-implant FGG may be indicated — particularly for mandibular overdenture implants where cleaning access is critical.

Denture After Surgery

Existing denture must be relieved over implant sites. Tissue conditioner applied. Unrelieved denture loading is a common cause of early implant failure in denture patients.

Clinical Scenarios
Applying soft tissue management principles to real cases
Scenario A

At suture removal (10 days post-surgery), you find the wound has opened 3mm with white scar tissue visible. The site had GBR performed. What is your priority?

Reveal Analysis ▾
Wound dehiscence over GBR graft. Marginal bone loss is already occurring if the wound has been open >48 hours. Management: irrigate with CHX, trim exposed membrane edges if visible, apply collagen membrane/sponge to protect, review weekly. Do NOT re-suture under tension. The grafting outcome will be compromised — partial success is the realistic expectation. Prevention: periosteal release + tweezers test at original surgery.
Scenario B

You notice white blanching at the wound margins immediately after placing your sutures. The patient is still anaesthetised. What do you do?

Reveal Analysis ▾
Remove the sutures immediately. Blanching = ischaemia from tension. The sutures are too tight or the flap was not passively closed. Perform periosteal release (if not already done, or more extensively if done), re-test with tweezers, then re-suture. This is not optional — ignoring blanching guarantees dehiscence at 7–10 days.
Key Terms Reference
Essential vocabulary for Lecture 2
Periosteal Release

Single 1mm notch at or just above the MGJ, run edge-to-edge. Releases the periosteal "glue". Allows elastic mucosa to stretch 5–10mm. Blade at 5°. Fresh blade 15c.

Tweezers Test

Grasp flap, bring to position, release. Flap holds 3–5s = passive closure = safe to suture. Springs back immediately = tension = periosteal release required.

FGG (Free Gingival Graft)

Epithelium kept. Placed on periosteum. Increases KM width. ~50% shrinkage expected. Never with GBR. 14-year data supports.

CTG (Connective Tissue Graft)

Epithelium removed (matte = CT). Placed under flap. Increases tissue thickness/volume. Tunnel technique available. Compatible with implant surgery.

Mucoderm

Porcine acellular dermal matrix. Second choice to autogenous CTG. No second surgical site. Can simultaneously act as GBR membrane. Porcine origin consideration.

Wound Dehiscence

Wound opening at 7–14 days. Primary cause: tension. Consequence: marginal bone loss guaranteed if >48 hours. White tissue = necrosis + re-epithelialisation beginning.

Key Takeaways
  • Tension >25 Ncm causes dehiscence — always use the tweezers test before suturing
  • Periosteal release: 1mm notch at MGJ, blade at 5°, left hand provides the stretch — dry procedure when correct
  • FGG (epithelium kept, on periosteum) = increases KM width. Expect 50% shrinkage.
  • CTG (epithelium removed, buried) = increases tissue volume/thickness
  • Frenectomy: perform 4–6 weeks before implant surgery if prominent frenum identified
  • Vertical mattress sutures preserve papilla height. Small needle, fine thread, adequate spacing.
  • Fixed temporisation > removable denture for papilla and tissue stability during healing
