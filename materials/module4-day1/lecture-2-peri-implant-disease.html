The scale of it

You will see peri-implantitis
about once a week.

It is the commonest long-term implant complication. You must recognise it, diagnose it, and understand the cause — because the cause dictates the treatment.

Set expectations

Nobody has the definitive answer. This lecture may raise as many questions as it settles — that's the honest state of the evidence.

Where it started · Sweden, late 1980s

The first case Esposito ever saw

A machined (smooth) surface implant, followed radiographically by rule. The timeline that defined the disease:

At 3 years it was dismissed as "physiological resorption." It wasn't — it was the unusual-then, typical-now failure pattern.

Diagnosis · 1

Peri-implant mucositis

A plaque-induced, reversible soft-tissue inflammation around a functioning implant, without appreciable bone loss — the implant equivalent of gingivitis.

  • Probing depth creeps >4 mm (say 4–5 mm) with bleeding.
  • Radiograph shows little or no bone loss — but you must know the bone level at crown placement to judge.
  • Very common, and we can't predict which cases progress — so we treat (remove plaque, keep clean).
Diagnosis · 2

Peri-implantitis

Plaque-induced progressive marginal bone loss with clinical signs of infection — visible redness, recession, bleeding, sometimes pus.

When it's frank

Sometimes the best decision is to remove the implant early — before it destroys the last of the bone — then choose between regrafting or a bridge. Diagnosed late, it's often too late to save.

Cause

An imbalance between host & bacteria

Bacterially induced — but it tips two ways:

Bacteria win

The plaque load overwhelms the host defence.

Defence "wins" — destructively

The host reaction is so aggressive it destroys its own tissue. In experiments, anti-inflammatories delay the disease — proof that inflammation drives the damage (not a prescription to give them).

Myth check

Occlusal overload does not cause peri-implantitis

Animal research never proved overload causes this bone loss — unless the overload actually fractures the implant. Physiological loading tends to increase bone density.

  • Excessive overload → you fracture the implant, or get fibrous separation (a thin non-healing line under continuous load) — a different failure pattern.
  • So "adjust the occlusion" was a dead end. In the original case it failed; the disease progressed.
Proof of cause · the Pontoriero experiment

Stop brushing → mucositis. Resume → health.

The cause is plaque; removing the plaque removes the aetiology. Mucositis is reversible — peri-implantitis (with bone loss) is not.

How to diagnose · 1

Look first

Swelling, redness, bleeding, pus. Esposito doesn't probe routinely — only when he suspects a problem.

The thick-tissue catch

A serious lesion can look calm externally — thick tissue hides the inflammation. It's still inflamed; it just doesn't show. Keep this in mind.

How to diagnose · 2 · Interactive

Probing an implant ≠ probing a tooth

How to diagnose · 3

Interpret the number — don't just read it

  • Threads block the probe, so you underestimate the lesion. Radiographs underestimate it too — opening the flap consistently reveals a bigger defect.
  • A 4–5 mm pocket round a tooth = disease. A 6–7 mm pocket round a deep implant may just be mucositis — deep placement + abundant soft tissue.
  • Inflamed tissue is loose, so the probe sinks — not from lost attachment. Resolve inflammation → tissue firms up (no true "gain").

The situation is usually worse than it appears.

Reading the radiograph

Crater or horizontal? Bone thickness decides

Thick bone → crater

A circumferential infrabony defect. Good for an augmentation/graft approach.

Thin bone → horizontal

Flat loss all around. Less graftable — think resective or smoothing.

Unlike periodontitis (often site-specific), peri-implantitis tends to lose bone all around. The shape guides therapy.

The evidence on roughness

Rougher surface → ~20% more disease at 3 years

Individually the four 2014 studies were non-significant (small numbers). Pooled, the meta-analysis cleared the line: ~20% more peri-implantitis with truly rough surfaces at 3 years.

Publication bias in action

These are conservative figures — the planned 5-year data were never published because the studies were stopped once problems mounted. The literature can't help you if the data is buried.

Management distinction

Failed-to-integrate is a 5-minute fix. Peri-implantitis is not.

Non-integrated implant

Mobile, rotating, no real infection. Under existing anaesthesia: unscrew it, place a wider-diameter implant same session. No drama.

Integrated + peri-implantitis

The real problem. Repeated treatment, possible removal — and the patient may have run out of money. Surgical cleaning (essentially just time, no graft) is cheaper and can save the implant for years.

Risk factor

A history of real periodontitis raises risk

Consistent across old and new studies. But be careful — many implant patients didn't lose teeth to true periodontitis.

Esposito, bluntly

"You don't buy a Porsche treating mild periodontitis — you buy one by extracting the teeth and doing All-on-4." So establish whether it was genuinely advanced periodontitis (real risk) or not (near-normal prognosis).

The counter-intuitive finding

More (thicker) tissue → more mucositis

The opposite of the "graft more soft tissue" mantra — a finding the original authors quietly downplayed at conferences "to avoid having tomatoes thrown." It depends on diagnosis:

Thin tissue

Inflammation → recession → the pocket reduces. Less attractive, but shallower.

Thick tissue

Doesn't recede → the pocket persists and hides the inflammation. Measured as more mucositis.

Practice point

Soft-tissue grafting is overused

  • Around implants it's harder and less successful than around teeth.
  • Over-optimistic grafting (especially over active pathology) can leave you worse than you started.
  • The main legitimate indication is aesthetics — not "protection."
The strategic point

Maintenance is the business

Largely hygienist-delivered, near-zero material cost, small regular fee. Customise the recall:

High-risk patient

Advanced perio and/or smoker → ~3-monthly.

Normal, good cleaner

6–12 monthly is fine.

Why it wins

Early detection of mucositis is simple to treat; waiting until peri-implantitis is not. Most dentists ignore maintenance — periodontists don't.

Remember you're treating a chronic infection — not one treatment, but potentially many over years.

Treatment · principle

Reduce the bacterial load — mechanically

Get below the (unknown) critical threshold: no visible plaque, no bleeding. The route depends on bone loss:

Non-surgical

When bone loss is limited. Any instrument that works — they perform similarly.

Surgical (open flap)

When threads/defect shape block blind cleaning. Often implantoplasty — but don't over-thin internally-connected implants (fracture/perforation risk).

Treatment · antibiotics

Adjuncts, not solutions

systemic

Amoxicillin

Cheap; first choice (if no allergy) after surface cleaning, in selected/recurrent cases. Not a standard step.

anti-anaerobic

Metronidazole

Not needed routinely — opening & oxygenating kills anaerobes. Combine with amoxicillin only if there's an abscess.

local

Local antibiotics

No systemic effect, more expensive, weak evidence. Only as an adjunct to mechanical debridement — alone, the effect lasts weeks then relapses.

Myth-buster · Interactive

What chlorhexidine actually does

Does it kill established plaque bacteria?+
No. It competes with bacteria for surface attachment. Used on a plaque-covered surface, it just "showers the bacteria" — they stay put. With plaque present, nothing happens.
So when does it work?+
Only after cleaning — it binds the surface before bacteria can re-colonise. Use after surgery or after scaling/root planing, twice daily for ~1–2 weeks.
Why twice a day, not four times?+
Substantivity is ~12 hours — it then detaches, so you re-apply. More often is pointless.
What inactivates it?+
Proteins (blood). A bleeding pocket full of protein deactivates it. And H₂O₂ flushing? The dramatic bubbling makes people happy but doesn't change the outcome.

Light-activated disinfection (LAD / PDT)

The 80-patient study that found nothing

A blue photosensitiser + light + oxygen → reactive radicals that kill microbes without harming host cells. The company-funded Swiss study used the least reliable primary outcome (bleeding) "not by chance."

Esposito's own RCT — the largest at the time

80 patients, 4 centres, bone loss ≥3 mm. Couldn't even use a placebo dye (every blue dye killed bacteria). Result: failure, recurrence, retreatment, bone loss (~0.1 mm) all the same with or without LAD. No benefit — and the company refused to pay.

Treatment · the honest summary

What the evidence concludes

✓

Peri-implantitis can be arrested — but medium-to-long-term outcomes are unpredictable.

✓

No single intervention is superior — laser, Vector, air-abrasive, curette all perform similarly. Use what you like.

✓

Don't wait — intervene as soon as you notice it. Praying won't work.

✓

The goal is to re-create tissue, implant and prosthetic morphology that makes hygiene easy.

★

Prevention is paramount: good prosthesis, good positioning, good hygiene, good maintenance.
